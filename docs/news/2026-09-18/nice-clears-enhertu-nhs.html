NICE clears AstraZeneca's Enhertu for NHS breast cancer patients in England

AstraZeneca's Cambridge headquarters, where the company co-develops Enhertu with Japan's Daiichi Sankyo. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
NICE approved Enhertu for NHS use in England for HER2-low advanced breast cancer, opening access to roughly 1,000 women a year after a two-year rejection.

England's drug-cost watchdog has cleared Enhertu for routine NHS use in a form of advanced breast cancer, ending a fight that has run since the medicine was first rejected two years ago.

The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) said on 17 September that trastuzumab deruxtecan — sold as Enhertu by AstraZeneca and Japan's Daiichi Sankyo — can now be offered to adults in England with HER2-low metastatic or unresectable breast cancer who have already had chemotherapy for their metastatic disease, or whose cancer returned during or within six months of finishing chemotherapy after surgery. NICE estimates roughly 1,000 women a year will qualify.

A drug NICE couldn't say yes to

Enhertu is not new. It has been licensed and reimbursed in Scotland since December 2023, and is available across much of Europe and the United States. What changed is the arithmetic NICE uses to weigh cost against benefit for the NHS in England.

In the pivotal DESTINY-Breast04 trial, patients on trastuzumab deruxtecan lived a median of 22.9 months, against 16.8 months for those on standard chemotherapy of the physician's choosing — a gap of roughly six months that NICE's own reviewers had not disputed. What NICE disputed, when it rejected the drug in 2024, was the price: cost-effectiveness estimates came in above the upper end of what the institute considered an acceptable use of NHS resources. Since that rejection, NICE had approved 25 other breast cancer treatments; Enhertu for this specific patient group was the one holdout.

Helen Knight, NICE's director of medicines evaluation, said the reversal followed changes to the institute's assessment methods — a new approach to valuing quality of life, published 27 August, combined with a widened cost-effectiveness threshold of £25,000–£35,000 per quality-adjusted life year, up from £20,000–£30,000. That threshold change was itself a product of December's UK–US Pharmaceutical Pricing Agreement. AstraZeneca and Daiichi Sankyo also agreed a confidential commercial discount, the mechanism NICE typically uses to bring list prices within its cost ceiling without disclosing the actual price paid by the NHS.

The campaign behind the numbers

The reversal was not simply a technical recalculation. Breast Cancer Now, the charity that led a public campaign against the 2024 rejection, framed the decision as vindication after more than two years of pressure. Its chief executive, Claire Rowney, said the moment "belongs to every person who refused to accept that access to a life-extending treatment was out of reach," while noting the decision "cannot be celebrated without remembering" the women who died in the intervening period. The charity is now pressing Wales and Northern Ireland, which set reimbursement separately from NICE, to follow England's lead.

The guidance is still in draft form; stakeholders have until 5pm on 7 October to lodge an appeal, though such challenges to a positive recommendation are uncommon.

What it means for AstraZeneca

For AstraZeneca, this is a reimbursement unlock rather than a regulatory one — Enhertu was already licensed for use in the UK, and English clinicians could not prescribe it on the NHS only because NICE had not funded it. The practical effect is a few thousand patients a year gaining access in Europe's largest public health system, a meaningful clinical outcome but a modest one financially set against Enhertu's global sales, which run into the billions of dollars annually across all its approved indications. AstraZeneca has separately been expanding Enhertu's reach in lung cancer, where a large trial reported in this edition showed the drug cut disease-progression risk by 37%; that data, not the NHS reimbursement, is the more likely near-term driver of how investors price the drug going forward.

The next marker is 7 October, when the appeal window on the NICE draft guidance closes and the recommendation is expected to become final.
