AstraZeneca's Enhertu posts major lung-cancer trial win, cutting progression risk 37%

In one sentence
Enhertu cut lung-cancer progression risk 37% in a Phase III trial, but an early look at survival data trended the other way, complicating AstraZeneca's first-line expansion push.

AstraZeneca and its Japanese partner Daiichi Sankyo told a lung cancer conference in Seoul this week that Enhertu, already one of the best-selling cancer drugs in the world, cut the risk of tumour progression or death by 37 percent compared with standard chemo-immunotherapy in patients with a hard-to-treat form of lung cancer. It is a genuine win, the first Phase III success in this specific patient population, and it strengthens the case for AstraZeneca's biggest oncology bet. It is not, on the evidence presented, an unqualified one.

What the trial actually showed

The study, DESTINY-Lung04, enrolled 454 to 469 patients (reports vary slightly by source) with previously untreated, advanced non-squamous non-small-cell lung cancer whose tumours carry HER2 exon 19 or 20 mutations — a genetic subgroup that makes up roughly 2 to 4 percent of NSCLC cases and has historically had few targeted options.¹ Patients were randomised to Enhertu (fam-trastuzumab deruxtecan) alone or to the current global standard of care, a platinum-pemetrexed chemotherapy doublet plus the immunotherapy pembrolizumab (Merck's Keytruda).

On the trial's primary endpoint, progression-free survival, Enhertu won clearly: a median of 14.3 months versus 8.3 months on chemo-immunotherapy, a hazard ratio of 0.63 (95% CI 0.50–0.79, p<0.0001).² Put plainly, patients on Enhertu went nearly six months longer, on average, before their cancer measurably worsened. The data were presented during the Presidential Symposium at the IASLC 2026 World Conference on Lung Cancer — the marquee slot reserved for results the organisers judge most likely to change practice.³

The overall-survival caveat

Here is where a cautious reading is warranted. Progression-free survival is a surrogate — it measures whether scans show tumour growth, not whether patients live longer. On the trial's overall-survival endpoint, an interim analysis (46.9 percent mature, not yet formally tested) found no benefit for Enhertu and a numerical signal in the other direction: median overall survival was 29.3 months on Enhertu versus 33.1 months on chemo-immunotherapy, a hazard ratio of 1.15 (95% CI 0.88–1.52).⁴ Of 213 deaths recorded at that cut, 115 occurred in the Enhertu arm against 98 in the control arm.

A hazard ratio whose confidence interval straddles 1.0, as this one does, does not establish that Enhertu shortens life — the trial simply cannot rule that out yet, nor can it confirm a survival benefit. AstraZeneca's own investigators have pointed to an asymmetry in what patients received after progressing: 48.0 percent of the control-arm patients went on to a HER2-directed therapy after their disease worsened, versus 23.3 percent of those originally on Enhertu, who had less access to a comparable rescue option once their first-line drug stopped working.⁵ That is a plausible explanation, but it is an explanation offered by the trial's sponsor, not an independently adjudicated finding, and the mature overall-survival data — expected as the trial matures further — will be the number regulators and prescribers ultimately weigh most heavily.

Separately, the trial reaffirmed a known liability of Enhertu's drug class: interstitial lung disease, a form of lung inflammation that can be fatal. Adjudicated drug-related ILD or pneumonitis occurred in 20.8 percent of Enhertu patients against 2.3 percent on chemo-immunotherapy, and all four Grade 5 (fatal) drug-related events on the Enhertu arm were attributed to pneumonitis or ILD.⁶ AstraZeneca said some of those deaths involved delayed recognition or under-dosing of the steroids used to manage the condition — a management issue, potentially, rather than an unavoidable feature of the drug, but one that will require close monitoring protocols if Enhertu moves into first-line use.

Why AstraZeneca is pushing ahead anyway

None of this changes the commercial logic driving the trial. Enhertu is already approved in more than 70 countries for previously-treated HER2-mutant NSCLC, based on the earlier DESTINY-Lung02 and DESTINY-Lung05 studies, but that is a second-line market — smaller, and reached only after patients have failed another regimen first.⁷ DESTINY-Lung04 tested Enhertu as the very first treatment a newly diagnosed patient receives, which is the larger, more lucrative slice of the lung cancer market and the one AstraZeneca needs if Enhertu is to keep growing at its current pace.

That pace has been striking. Enhertu's global sales rose 40 percent to $2.78 billion in AstraZeneca's 2025 accounting, with the two companies' Japanese partner Daiichi Sankyo reporting even faster growth on its own books.⁸ AstraZeneca has named Enhertu, alongside its lung cancer drug Imfinzi, as one of the two products expected to carry group oncology revenue growth in 2026, with lung cancer expansion explicitly part of that plan.⁹ A first-line label in HER2-mutant NSCLC — should the progression-free survival data hold up under regulatory scrutiny and the overall-survival picture not deteriorate further — would open exactly that door.

The trial's design means this is not the final word. DESTINY-Lung04 will continue collecting data specifically to mature the overall-survival endpoint, and it is that number, not the progression-free survival result presented in Seoul, that health authorities in the US, Europe and Britain will scrutinise most closely before any first-line approval. NICE, the UK's cost-effectiveness watchdog, only cleared Enhertu for NHS breast cancer patients this year — a reminder that a positive trial readout and a funded, prescribable indication are two different milestones separated by months of regulatory and reimbursement review.¹⁰ Investors reacted to the headline risk-reduction figure; clinicians will wait for the survival curves to separate, or not, before changing how they treat a newly diagnosed patient.
