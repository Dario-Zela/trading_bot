Oil surges past $100 as pipeline attacks disrupt Saudi and regional supply

A crude oil tanker — the kind of vessel traders have been chasing for replacement barrels as Saudi pipeline capacity stays offline. — Stahl Erz / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline shutdown after drone strikes pushed Brent past $100, with Middle East crude premiums hitting their widest since March.

Brent crude broke back above $100 a barrel this month and briefly touched $109, the highest level since March, after drone strikes forced Saudi Arabia to shut the pipeline that lets its crude bypass the Strait of Hormuz entirely. For traders who had spent much of the year pricing Middle East risk as background noise, the past two weeks have been a reminder that noise can become signal very quickly.

The proximate cause was an attack on the East-West pipeline — known locally as the Petroline — which Saudi Arabia shut down on September 11th as a precaution after drones launched from Iraq struck it in the Riyadh and Medina regions. Satellite imagery reviewed by analysts appeared to show significant damage to at least one pumping station. Andy Lipow, president of Lipow Oil Associates, told reporters that judging by the images, repairs "will take months," a starker read than Saudi Aramco's own guidance that it can restore roughly half the line's capacity within days and full service in about six weeks. US Energy Secretary Chris Wright split the difference, telling CNBC the outage would be "measured in days."

Whichever estimate proves right, the pipeline's importance is not in dispute. Built in 1981, the 1,200-kilometre Petroline carries crude from the Abqaiq processing hub across the Arabian Peninsula to export terminals at Yanbu on the Red Sea, allowing Saudi barrels to reach Europe and Asia without transiting the Strait of Hormuz — the 33-kilometre chokepoint through which roughly a fifth of the world's oil and LNG normally passes. In recent months the line had been moving four to five million barrels a day, some 4-5% of global supply. Its shutdown does not just remove volume from the market; it removes the one workaround that made Hormuz-related anxiety tolerable.

Traders scramble for replacement barrels

The pipeline shutdown did not happen in isolation. It came after weeks of attacks on Saudi energy infrastructure that Riyadh has linked to Iran-aligned groups, including strikes the Houthis claimed on the 400,000-barrel-a-day Jazan refinery, which serves the domestic market rather than exports. Wikipedia's running account of the campaign — which began on 28 February — lists strikes on the Ras Tanura refinery, the Shaybah oil field and the SAMREF refinery, alongside attacks on US military and diplomatic targets in the kingdom, with Riyadh reporting three civilians killed and Washington reporting two soldiers dead among the wider toll.

The market response has been less about the headline Brent number than about where traders have had to go looking for oil. Bloomberg reported that Middle East grades hit their highest levels since March as buyers, mostly in Asia, scoured the market for replacement barrels once the pipeline outage began cutting into Saudi Aramco's ability to guarantee deliveries. Oman crude — a benchmark grade unaffected by the pipeline outage directly but suddenly in much higher demand — jumped to $132.09 a barrel, pushing its premium over Brent to almost $24, itself the widest gap since March. That kind of spread is a cleaner stress signal than the Brent price alone: it shows real buyers paying up for barrels they can actually get their hands on, not just algorithmic repricing of geopolitical risk.

Diesel markets have amplified the unease. Traders have described themselves as unusually sensitive to disruption in the smaller diesel pool, which is already being squeezed by Ukrainian strikes on Russian refineries. With two separate supply chains under simultaneous pressure, the market has had less spare capacity to absorb either shock on its own.

What the price is actually pricing

By Thursday, Brent had eased back from its $109 peak toward the low $100s as Aramco's partial-restart timeline took some of the acute panic out of the market — a pattern typical of geopolitical spikes, where the first move overshoots before settling on a smaller, more durable risk premium. Analysts at Bernstein have floated $150 as a tail-risk scenario if the disruption widens into what they describe as a "triple supply shock" spanning the pipeline, the strait itself and Red Sea shipping lanes. That is not a base case, but it is no longer a fringe one either.

The more durable question for traders is less about the price level than about what it implies for models built on the assumption that Gulf supply is broadly stable. A region that supplies roughly a quarter of the world's oil has spent seven months under intermittent attack, and the one piece of infrastructure designed as insurance against a Hormuz closure has itself now been taken offline. Inventory data has so far been unremarkable — US crude stocks fell a modest 640,000 barrels last week, smaller than forecast — which suggests the disruption has not yet shown up in physical shortages so much as in the price traders are willing to pay to avoid the risk of one.

The next marker is mechanical rather than diplomatic: whether Aramco's partial restart, expected within days, actually materialises, and whether the six-week timeline for full capacity holds against Lipow's more pessimistic read. A slip in either direction will move Brent well before any ceasefire headline does.
