China's August data shows consumer spending languishing despite factory strength

The People's Bank of China headquarters in Beijing, where policymakers face a widening gap between factory and consumer data — Max12Max / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
China's retail sales rose just 0.4% in August against a 5.2% industrial beat, leaving Beijing torn between holding stimulus fire and risking a bigger package later.

China's factories had a good August. Its shoppers did not, and the gap between the two numbers has widened enough to complicate Beijing's next move on stimulus.

Retail sales rose 0.4% year-on-year last month, the National Bureau of Statistics said on September 15 — below the 0.8% economists polled by Bloomberg had expected, and a slowdown from July's already-soft 0.6%. Industrial output told the opposite story: up 5.2% year-on-year, ahead of the 4.8% forecast and a marked acceleration from July's 4.5%. Fixed-asset investment was worse than either figure suggests at first glance, down 7.2% over the first eight months of the year, a deeper contraction than the 6.7% drop recorded through July.

A familiar fault line, wider than before

None of this is new in kind. China's economy has run on a two-speed model since the pandemic: factories and exports carrying growth, households sitting on their wallets. What's notable in August is how far the two speeds diverged. Industrial output's acceleration owes a good deal to an AI-linked tech manufacturing boom — chipmaking, servers, electronics — that has kept factory lines busy even as four typhoons disrupted logistics and production schedules during the month. Retail sales, by contrast, missed forecasts for the second straight month, a run that included an outright 0.6% contraction in May.

The NBS's own language captured the tension without resolving it. "The economy was overall stable in August," the bureau said, while also warning that "the negative impact from the external environment is deepening, and the imbalance between strong supply and weak demand is still prominent domestically." That is about as candid as an official statistics release gets: the headline growth number is being carried by supply, not demand.

Why the consumer keeps sitting this out

The household side of the ledger has three separate drags feeding into one number. Property remains the biggest: new-home prices fell 0.1% month-on-month in August, and while the annual decline narrowed slightly to 3.0% from 3.2%, that improvement was uneven — tier-one cities posted their first monthly price gain in this cycle, up 0.1%, while smaller tier-two and tier-three markets kept falling. Beijing has responded by extending the maximum mortgage term to 40 years from 30, a measure aimed at affordability rather than confidence.

Labour-market softness compounds the caution. Youth unemployment hit 17.9% in July, its worst reading since August 2025, and persistently high joblessness among younger urban workers has fed directly into the household savings behaviour that keeps retail sales flat. Meanwhile inflation offers little comfort: consumer prices rose 0.8% in August, up from July's six-month low of 0.5%, but producer prices have now been negative for more than three years — a sign that mild disinflationary pressure, not demand-pull inflation, still defines the industrial side of the economy even as headline CPI ticks up on volatile commodity costs.

Beijing's calculus

This is the trap the headline numbers set for policymakers. Read industrial strength as evidence the economy is coping, and the case for aggressive fresh stimulus weakens — Beijing can point to 5.2% factory growth and rapid export momentum as proof the model still works. Read it as a supply-side number that says nothing about the households who aren't spending, and the case for emergency measures gets stronger, because retail sales and investment are the two indicators most exposed to what happens next in the property market and the labour market.

So far, Beijing has chosen the middle path: faster government bond issuance and expanded loan-interest subsidies for small private firms and consumers, rather than the kind of broad rate cut or fiscal bazooka some economists have called for. Julian Evans-Pritchard, head of China economics at Capital Economics, described the recent package of measures as "a step in the right direction" that would "probably be insufficient to drive a turnaround in growth unless followed up with greater fiscal support." Dinny McMahon, head of China markets research at Trivium China, was blunter: "This isn't a stimulus package; it should be considered a relief package."

For traders, the read-through matters more than the headline. A Beijing that treats August's industrial beat as licence to hold fire risks having to deliver a much larger, more disruptive package later if consumption keeps sliding. A Beijing that treats the retail miss as confirmation of systemic demand weakness and over-corrects risks the kind of liquidity flood that has previously fed capital outflows rather than domestic spending. Neither read is obviously wrong from the data alone — which is precisely the problem.

What to watch

The next test comes before October's Golden Week holiday, the week-long national holiday when retail and travel spending typically offer the clearest read on consumer sentiment. Economists expect any further easing — whether from the PBOC on rates or the NDRC on bond issuance — to land in that window, timed either to support holiday spending or to react to it. Q3 GDP, due in mid-October, will show whether the industrial-consumption gap narrowed or widened further, and with it, which side of Beijing's dilemma is winning the internal argument.
