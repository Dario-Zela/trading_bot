Ukraine strikes Russian oil refinery and military aircraft in cross-border raids

The Slavneft-YANOS refinery in Yaroslavl, Russia — among the country's five largest — pictured before this week's Ukrainian strike. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Ukraine says it hit Russia's Yaroslavl refinery and downed six aircraft at Rostov airfield, in a week that also saw 278 front-line clashes in a single day.

Ukraine's military says it struck one of Russia's largest oil refineries and destroyed six aircraft at a military airfield in a single overnight operation this week, part of what General Staff figures show has been an unusually intense stretch of fighting across the front line.

The strikes, confirmed by Kyiv's General Staff, hit the Slavneft-YANOS refinery in Yaroslavl — a city roughly 250 km northeast of Moscow, far outside the immediate war zone — and a military airfield near Rostov-on-Don in southern Russia. President Volodymyr Zelensky said the operation, carried out jointly by the Security Service of Ukraine, the Unmanned Systems Forces, and both military and foreign intelligence services, destroyed one An-12 and two An-26 transport aircraft along with three helicopters on the ground at Rostov, and set the refinery's primary distillation unit ablaze.

A refinery Russia can't easily replace

Slavneft-YANOS is not a marginal target. With a processing capacity of roughly 300,000 barrels a day — about 15 million tonnes annually — it ranks among Russia's five largest refineries and is the biggest in the country's Central Federal District, supplying gasoline, diesel and aviation fuel to the region around Moscow as well as to military-industrial customers. Ukrainian officials say the strike disabled the AVT-3 primary distillation unit, which alone processes over 17,000 tonnes of crude a day, forcing the plant to halt processing entirely. Yaroslavl region governor Mikhail Yevrayev said the resulting fire was extinguished by Thursday morning and reported no casualties.

This was not an isolated hit. Ukraine has attacked Russian refineries at least 21 times in August alone, according to industry tracking cited by Kyiv Post — a record for a single month — and roughly half of Russia's six largest diesel producers have significantly cut or halted output this month. The cumulative effect has been visible on Russian streets rather than just balance sheets: fuel shortages have hit nearly all of the country's 83 regions since mid-2026, with rationing in two-thirds of them and gas stations entirely dry in parts of the North Caucasus and the Far East, according to Meduza and CNN reporting. Moscow has responded by repeatedly extending bans on gasoline and diesel exports, most recently through January 2027 for gasoline.

The airfield hit and a bruising day of ground combat

The Rostov-on-Don airfield, roughly 40 km from Ukrainian-held territory in places, has become one of the more frequently struck Russian military sites this year, part of a long-range drone campaign that Zelensky has said aims to make Russian airspace progressively less usable for military logistics. Six aircraft destroyed in one raid — three transport planes and three helicopters — would be among the larger single-strike tallies Ukraine has claimed against a Russian airbase this year, though Moscow has not issued its own damage assessment and the figures rest on Kyiv's account, corroborated in this instance by satellite-adjacent reporting on fires and secondary detonations at the site.

The aerial raids came in the same week Ukraine's General Staff reported 278 combat engagements along the front line in a single 24-hour period — a figure that, by the standard of recent months, sits toward the high end of a steadily climbing baseline. Comparable daily reports through August and early September had clustered in the 200-to-240 range; 278 marks one of the more intense days recorded this year. The General Staff said the heaviest fighting was concentrated in the Pokrovsk and Kostiantynivka sectors, where Ukrainian forces reported repelling 43 separate Russian attacks, alongside 106 Russian airstrikes, more than 7,100 first-person-view drone sorties, and over 2,300 shelling attacks in the same period.

What the numbers do and don't show

Taken together, the refinery strike, the airfield raid and the engagement count point to a Ukrainian military still capable of operating on two fronts simultaneously — grinding, high-volume defense along hundreds of kilometers of trench lines, and long-range precision strikes deep inside Russia — at a moment when the durability of Western military and financial aid remains an open question in Washington and European capitals. That is a meaningful data point about capacity. It is a weaker one about trajectory: a single day's engagement count, without the preceding weeks charted alongside it, says more about the intensity of this particular 24 hours than about whether the war is entering a new phase. Neither side's claims in this account have been independently verified by outside monitors, standard practice in a conflict where battlefield access is tightly restricted on both sides.

What is measurable is the accumulating damage to Russia's refining base, and that is the figure worth tracking next. Russia's state statistics agency and energy ministry are due to publish August-September refining and export data in the coming weeks; a further drop in throughput, layered on top of the export bans already in place, would be the clearest sign yet that the drone campaign is reshaping Russian fuel markets rather than simply making headlines.
