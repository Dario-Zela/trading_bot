Airbus's regional-jet venture grounds planes over toxic-fume complaints

An Air Corsica ATR 72-600, the aircraft type at the center of the fume-event grounding. — Eric Salard / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Air Corsica grounded four ATR 72-600s over cockpit fume complaints, putting Airbus, Leonardo and engine-maker Pratt & Whitney under scrutiny.

Air Corsica, the airline that connects Corsica to mainland France, has pulled four of its seven ATR 72-600 turboprops from service after pilots repeatedly reported fumes leaking into the cockpit and cabin — some crew members describing migraines after exposure. The Wall Street Journal reports that ATR, the aircraft's manufacturer, and Pratt & Whitney, which builds its engines, are now investigating, with one grounded aircraft sent to ATR's Toulouse facility for testing.

The episode is small in scale but awkward in ownership. ATR is a 50-50 joint venture between Airbus and Italy's Leonardo, and the aircraft in question use the PW127XT, a newer Pratt & Whitney Canada engine that became the default option on ATR deliveries after Air Corsica took the type's first unit in 2022. The same engine has since been ordered by FedEx and IndiGo, which is the detail that matters most for anyone pricing this as an isolated glitch versus a design flaw: if investigators trace the fumes to the engine itself rather than a maintenance lapse or a one-off contamination event, the finding travels well beyond a Corsican regional carrier's fleet of 14 aircraft.

What's leaking, and why it's familiar

The mechanism under scrutiny is a so-called fume event: engine oil seeping past worn seals and vaporizing into the bleed air that most commercial aircraft — turboprops and jets alike — draw from the engine to pressurize the cabin. Air Corsica says no causal link has yet been confirmed between the reported "technical events" and any specific fault, and that it is working with ATR and its equipment partners to isolate the source. ATR maintains the aircraft meet regulatory safety standards; Pratt & Whitney and Airbus have both declined to comment.

The timing works against generous interpretation. A broader Journal investigation published earlier this year found fume-event reports climbing sharply across the industry — a combined 660% rise for JetBlue and Spirit between 2016 and 2024 — concentrated on aircraft using bleed-air designs, with occupational studies linking the leaked vapors to neurotoxic compounds such as tricresyl phosphate. The FAA's 2024 reauthorization now mandates better fume-event reporting and crew training, a regulatory backdrop that leaves little room for manufacturers to wave off individual incidents as noise.

The cost of grounding four turboprops

For now this reads as operational rather than financial. Air Corsica has chartered replacement capacity — an Airbus A319 from Amelia, an A350 from Air Caraïbes, and an extra ATR from Chalair — to cover more than 100 substitute flights through the end of October and preserve the public-service routes linking Corsica to Paris, Marseille, Nice and Lyon. One of the grounded aircraft had also been chartered by Airbus itself for inter-factory shuttle flights, forcing a swap there too.

The real exposure sits with the investigation's outcome. A contamination finding closes the story quickly; a design finding reopens questions about ATR's standing in a regional-jet and turboprop market it already shares uneasily with Embraer, and about how much scrutiny Pratt & Whitney's engine family — already dealing with unrelated delivery bottlenecks flagged by Airbus — can absorb before it dents order confidence at FedEx and IndiGo. ATR's Toulouse test results, and any EASA response, are the next things worth watching.
