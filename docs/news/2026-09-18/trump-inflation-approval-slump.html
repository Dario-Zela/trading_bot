Trump's approval on inflation slumps to 19% as oil-driven price spike bites ahead of midterms

President Trump's approval on inflation has fallen to 19%, the worst reading for any U.S. president since polling began tracking the issue. — Daniel Torok / The White House, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Trump's inflation approval has crashed to 19% — the worst on record for any president — as Iran-war oil shocks squeeze households seven weeks before the midterms.

Seven weeks from the midterms, President Trump's standing on the issue voters say matters most has collapsed to a level no occupant of the White House has recorded before. A new Marquette Law School national poll, conducted September 2–9, puts his approval on handling inflation at just 19% — with 79% disapproving, for a net rating of minus 61. CNN's chief data analyst, Harry Enten, called it the worst presidential inflation reading on record, worse than anything logged for Biden, Trump's first term, or any predecessor Marquette has tracked.

The number lands at an awkward moment. Oil prices have climbed more than 18% this month alone as the Iran war grinds past its sixth month, with Brent crude pushing above $108 a barrel after Saudi Arabia shut a key pipeline following a drone attack traced to Iraq-based militias. U.S. gasoline hit a record for the Labor Day holiday, eclipsing the previous high set in 2012, and diesel has touched an all-time peak above $5.80 a gallon — a cost that filters directly into freight, food and the rest of the consumer basket.

A rating that crosses party lines

What distinguishes this Marquette survey from the run of soft polling Trump has absorbed all year is how little partisan cover is left. Net approval on inflation sits at minus 95 among Democrats and minus 74 among independents — unsurprising numbers for a Republican president facing a cost-of-living squeeze. But it is minus 4 among Republicans, meaning the president's own base is now, on balance, unhappy with his record on prices. Sixty-four percent of all respondents say Trump's policies are making inflation worse; only 18% think they're bringing it down. That marks a reversal from the optimism of his early second term: 76% of Republicans expected inflation to fall under Trump after the 2024 election, and just 37% still believe it will.

The damage has spread beyond the single issue. Trump's overall job approval fell to 37% in the same poll, down from 40% in July, while his approval on the economy broadly sits at 28% and on the Iran war itself at 26%. On the generic congressional ballot, Democrats lead Republicans 54% to 41% among likely voters — a 13-point margin, and one Marquette says exceeds the poll's margin of error of roughly four points.

Supply shock, not demand

The inconvenient feature of this inflation spike, for a White House looking for remedies, is that it is largely a supply story. The Strait of Hormuz, through which more than a fifth of global oil trade passes, has been intermittently disrupted since the war began, and the Saudi pipeline attack this month added a fresh shock on top of it. Economists estimate a 20% rise in crude prices adds roughly three-tenths of a percentage point to headline inflation directly, before accounting for knock-on effects on airfares, plastics and food transport — channels that tend to push the real number higher still.

That framing matters because it narrows the policy options available. A demand-driven inflation problem responds, however painfully, to higher interest rates. An oil-shock-driven one does not: the Federal Reserve's recent rate hike, its first in three years, was aimed at controlling exactly this kind of imported price pressure even as it risks slowing growth further. Tightening does little to unclog a pipeline in Saudi Arabia or reopen a shipping lane in the Gulf, leaving the administration with blunter, less popular tools — strategic reserve releases, price-gouging rhetoric, or appeals to Gulf producers to pump more, none of which have moved prices much so far.

What's next

Republican strategists are now openly divided over how to respond seven weeks out, with some pushing for a preelection stimulus or gas-tax relief and others warning that such moves would look like panic. The next data point that will shape the debate is the Labor Department's CPI report due before Election Day, alongside any signs that the Iran ceasefire — which has already frayed past the 200-day mark — either holds long enough to ease oil markets or deteriorates further and pushes crude toward the $120–140 range some forecasters now flag as possible. Marquette's polling suggests the political cost of waiting is already being paid.
