BEYOND THE TAPE · COUNCIL TAX · By L. Bordeaux · 2026-09-18 · 4 min read

Badenoch instructs lawyers to challenge government over Wandsworth's 94% council tax rise

Kemi Badenoch, Conservative Party leader, has instructed lawyers to challenge the government's council funding formula. — House of Commons / Roger Harris (Wikimedia Commons)
In one sentence
Badenoch has instructed lawyers to challenge a government funding formula she calls politically motivated after it forced Wandsworth toward a 94% council tax rise.

There is a certain irony in Wandsworth — for decades the borough Conservatives held up as proof that low taxes and lean government could coexist — now finding itself the flashpoint in a war over who pays for what. Its residents are facing a 94% rise in council tax. Its Conservative leadership says it has no choice. And the leader of the national Conservative Party says she is prepared to go to court to stop it.

"So we have actually instructed lawyers, as the Conservative Party, to see if we can challenge the Government on this decision."

That was Kemi Badenoch, speaking about a funding overhaul that has hit Wandsworth harder than almost anywhere else in the country. The borough's Band D council tax, historically among the lowest in England at £509.84, is set to rise to £1,468 — a jump that ends four decades of Wandsworth's low-tax identity in one budget cycle.

What actually changed

The trigger is a rewrite of the formula used to distribute central government grants to councils, part of the government's push to make funding fairer between wealthier and poorer parts of England. Under the new formula, money is being redirected away from affluent boroughs in London and the South East and towards more deprived areas of the north and Midlands. Wandsworth says its central grant has been cut by 40%.

To soften the blow — or perhaps to make it politically survivable — ministers have given six councils "funding realignment powers": a two-year dispensation letting them raise council tax above the usual 5% ceiling that would otherwise trigger a local referendum. Wandsworth, Kensington and Chelsea, Westminster, Hammersmith and Fulham, the City of London and Windsor and Maidenhead all qualify. All are Conservative-run or Conservative-leaning; all have historically kept council tax low. The Institute for Fiscal Studies has estimated that Wandsworth and Westminster would need increases of up to 75% just to balance their books under the new arrangement — Wandsworth's own figure runs higher still.

Robert Morritt, who took over as Wandsworth's Conservative leader in July after a messy internal contest — the council has been under no overall control since May's election, with the Conservatives holding it by a single seat over Labour — has joined counterparts at Westminster and Kensington and Chelsea in arguing that the new formula "punishes low tax councils." It is not, on its face, an unreasonable complaint: boroughs that kept bills down for years are now being asked to raise them fastest.

The political charge

Badenoch's language went well beyond a technical dispute over grant formulas. Asked about Wandsworth, she said:

"I think it is outrageous that Wandsworth Council is having to put up council tax... Because what it looks like Labour is doing is taking money from areas that don't vote Labour and giving it to places that do vote Labour."

"Right now it's about the principle of it: that you don't just take money from areas because they're not voting for you and give them elsewhere."

That is a serious allegation — that a funding formula, ostensibly a technical exercise in redistribution, is being wielded as an instrument of electoral reward and punishment. It is also, notably, unproven; the government has not responded publicly to the claim, and the redistribution broadly tracks deprivation data rather than voting patterns, even if the two often correlate. Legal challenges to funding formulas have a mixed history — courts have historically been reluctant to second-guess ministers' discretion over how they allocate resources between councils, so long as the process itself was lawful. Badenoch's lawyers will need to show something more than an unwelcome outcome.

What a court fight would test

If the challenge proceeds, it puts the courts in an unusual position: adjudicating not whether a policy is wise, but whether a government can legally reweight funding formulas in ways that happen to disadvantage areas that vote against it. Success would hand opposition-run councils a new lever against future funding settlements from any government. Failure — the likelier outcome given the courts' traditional deference on fiscal policy — would confirm that these fights are meant to be settled at the ballot box, not the bench.

For Wandsworth residents, the immediate stakes are simpler: a bill nearly triple what many have budgeted for. The council is due to set its final budget in the coming months, and any legal challenge would need to move quickly to have a bearing on it. Whether Badenoch's lawyers file before then — and what the government says in response — is the next thing to watch.
