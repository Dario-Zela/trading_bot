BoE holds but MPC splits 6-3 as gilt yields sit near three-decade highs

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, London. — George Rex / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
BoE held rates at 3.75% but split 6-3, with three MPC members pushing for a hike as CPI hit 3.1% and 30-year gilt yields touched a 28-year high.

The Bank of England held Bank Rate at 3.75% on Wednesday, but the vote behind that headline was closer than it looked. Six of the nine members of the Monetary Policy Committee backed a hold; three — Megan Greene, Catherine Mann and chief economist Huw Pill — voted to raise rates by 25 basis points to 4%. A 6-3 split is not a committee at ease. It is a committee one bad inflation print away from moving.

The backdrop explains the nerves. UK CPI rose to 3.1% in August, up from 2.9% in July, driven overwhelmingly by fuel: petrol prices hit their highest since November 2022 as Brent crude climbed 36% since July to around $106 a barrel, and UK wholesale gas prices jumped 78% to 207p per therm. In its published minutes, the Bank said spot energy prices now sit "closer to those in the July adverse scenario" — the bad branch of its own forecast tree — and it now expects inflation to reach roughly 3¾% by the fourth quarter and "slightly above 4%" by early 2027.

A committee split on how much slack is left

The majority's case for holding rests on a bet that the energy shock stays contained. Members backing the hold pointed to "very limited evidence of emerging second-round effects" — the mechanism by which a one-off price jump feeds into wage demands and becomes embedded — and argued that restrictive financial conditions and domestic economic slack are doing enough of the work already.

The three dissenters read the same data differently. Their argument, laid out in the minutes, is that "resilience in labour demand and activity suggest slack may have already peaked," and that waiting for hard proof of second-round effects before acting "would leave policy behind the curve." It's a classic hawk-versus-dove split on timing, but the fact that three MPC members — including the Bank's own chief economist — are willing to go on record for a hike while inflation is running at 3.1% and rising is the signal traders should not wave past. A unanimous hold would have said the Bank has room to wait. A 6-3 hold says patience is thin.

The gilt market has already made up its mind

While the MPC was debating basis points, the bond market was doing its own repricing. The 30-year gilt yield touched 5.89% on 1 September — up 10bp in a single session and the highest since March 1998, nearly three decades ago. The 10-year yield has pushed to around 5.22-5.25%, a level last seen during the 2008 financial crisis. Both moves predate Wednesday's decision and reflect a separate but related worry: that persistent inflation and a heavier debt-servicing bill are squeezing the room available to Chancellor Healey's fiscal plans, forcing harder choices between spending cuts, tax rises and a larger deficit.

The two stories reinforce each other. Higher long-end yields raise the government's borrowing costs regardless of what the Bank does with Bank Rate, and a central bank that looks closer to hiking than cutting removes one of the few forces that might otherwise have pulled the curve back down. Gilt investors are not waiting for the MPC to catch up to the inflation data — they have already priced in a rockier path.

What to watch

The next CPI print, due in mid-October, is the immediate catalyst: another upside surprise on fuel or energy pass-through would hand the three dissenters new arguments, and a fourth vote flipping to hike at the November MPC meeting would not be a shock on these numbers. Until then, the practical read for markets is straightforward — sterling longs, UK rate-sensitive equities and gilt-curve positioning all carry more two-sided risk than a simple "BoE holds" headline implies, with the tightening scenario now closer than the committee's own arithmetic (6 votes to 3) would suggest to a casual reader.
