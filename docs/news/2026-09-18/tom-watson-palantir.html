Tom Watson, former Labour deputy leader, joins Palantir

Tom Watson, the former Labour deputy leader, is now a senior vice-president at Palantir. — UK Parliament / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Tom Watson, Labour's former deputy leader, becomes a Palantir senior vice-president as the firm's £330m NHS data contract faces an active government review.

Tom Watson spent four decades in Labour politics building a reputation as the party's chief fixer — the man who helped topple Tony Blair, steadied Jeremy Corbyn's front bench as deputy leader, and later became a vocal critic of Silicon Valley's grip on public life. On Wednesday he became a senior vice-president at Palantir Technologies, the US data-analytics firm whose UK subsidiary is fighting to keep hold of a £330m NHS contract that ministers are actively reconsidering.

Watson, now Lord Watson of Wyre Forest, will work alongside Louis Mosley, Palantir's UK and Europe chief executive, on what the company describes as deepening "the social and economic value its work creates through better British public services, jobs and skills." He has taken a leave of absence from the House of Lords and given up his parliamentary pass — a formal break from frontline politics, though not from the parts of the state Palantir most wants to sell into.

"Terrifically excited" was how Watson described the move, saying he wanted to help the firm speed up NHS diagnosis, treatment and hospital discharge, and to help "our police to crack crimes and root out corruption." Palantir's chief executive, Alex Karp, framed it as continuity rather than conversion: "This is just the latest chapter of Tom's forty-year fight for better public services. We are fortunate to have him guide us as we help the British government to deliver for the British public."

A contract under active review

The appointment lands in the middle of the tensest period yet for Palantir's UK public-sector business. The company holds the £330m contract to run the NHS Federated Data Platform, signed in 2023 and due to run to early 2027, with a break clause that has turned the second half of this year into a genuine decision point. Nearly a third of the 16 NHS trusts using the platform are carrying out fewer procedures than before it went live, according to figures compiled by campaign group Foxglove — though one trust, Chelsea and Westminster, accounts for 84% of that fall on its own. Palantir points instead to 110,078 additional hospital procedures it says the platform has enabled since rollout.

The scrutiny goes beyond performance data. The Financial Times has reported that NHS England allowed Palantir staff "unlimited" access to patient records, and the National Data Guardian, Dr Nicola Byrne, pushed NHS England into admitting this summer that its own data protection assessment had wrongly stated that only NHS staff could see identifiable patient data on the system. NHS England has since commissioned Imperial College Projects to run a £700,000 independent evaluation of whether the platform delivers value for money. Separately, Palantir is in court proceedings with London mayor Sadiq Khan over a blocked £50m Metropolitan Police AI contract.

The access question

Watson is not the first senior political or security figure to land at Palantir's door — former MI6 chief Sir John Sawers and ex-Ministry of Defence strategy director Polly Scully both hold advisory roles there, and Watson himself has been a paid member of the company's Public Services Advisory Board since 2024, disclosed in the House of Lords register of interests. The pattern fits a company that, according to reporting on its UK operations, has spent heavily on securing political access: think-tank donations, Davos receptions, and advisory seats for former officials with standing relationships across Whitehall.

Whether that access converts into contract survival is a separate question from whether it should. A former deputy leader of the governing party's own tradition, arguing publicly for the NHS platform's value at the exact moment officials are deciding whether to trigger its 2027 extension, is either a coincidence of timing or the whole point of the hire. Palantir did not frame it as the latter. The optics do the arguing anyway.

What's next

The practical test comes at the Department of Health and Social Care's extension decision ahead of the March 2027 break clause, with the Imperial College Projects evaluation due to feed into that call. Until then, expect Watson and Mosley to be the public faces defending a contract that Foxglove, the National Data Guardian and a chunk of NHS trusts have already made politically expensive.
