Boeing's $10bn free cash flow target pushed out further as 737 Max ramp-up slips

A Boeing 737 Max rolls out of the Renton, Washington factory, where wing production has become the bottleneck holding back the output ramp. — Aka The Beav / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Boeing CEO Kelly Ortberg says 737 Max output is still short of 47 jets a month, pushing the firm's $10bn cash flow goal further out and dragging the stock down.

Boeing's chief executive has, for the third time in as many quarters, told investors that the company's road back to durable profitability runs a little longer than previously advertised. Speaking at Morgan Stanley's Laguna Conference on 16 September, Kelly Ortberg said stabilising 737 Max production at the target rate of 47 jets a month is taking "a little bit longer" than planned — and with it, Boeing's long-standing goal of $10bn in annual free cash flow slides further over the horizon.

"We are not stable yet at 47 a month," Ortberg told the conference. "We just have not seen the flow improvements that we expected in the time frame." The admission cost Boeing shares roughly 2.5% on the day, extending a slide that has left the stock down more than 10% over the trailing month and trading near $202, well below its 200-day average of $220.

Wings, not fuselages, are the holdup

The bottleneck, Ortberg said, sits at Boeing's Renton, Washington plant, where wings for the Max are built before being shipped to the Everett line for final assembly. The FAA capped Max output at 38 a month in the wake of the 2024 door-plug blowout on an Alaska Airlines jet; clearing that ceiling to 47, and eventually 52, was meant to be largely a matter of discipline and time. Instead, wing-flow rates have lagged the recovery plan Boeing laid out to regulators and investors, and hitting 52 a month next year will now require both a fix at Renton and FAA certification of a second Max line in Everett.

There was a rare bright spot: the FAA certified the smallest variant, the 737 Max 7, in August, and Ortberg said certification of the largest, the Max 10 — which accounts for roughly 30% of all Max orders and is running years behind its original schedule — is coming "very soon." Boeing is already building two Max 10s in Everett ahead of that sign-off.

The cash flow math gets pushed out again

The production slip flows straight through to the balance sheet. Boeing's 2026 guidance had pointed to $1bn-$3bn in free cash flow, with $3bn treated as the upside case; the company now expects to land closer to $2bn, the midpoint, as slower 737 output compounds with delays ramping the 787 Dreamliner to 10 jets a month — itself held back by engine shortages. The 787 line, held up as the model for how the Max ramp should eventually look, is stabilised at 8 a month but has yet to move higher.

Management still calls the long-term $10bn annual free cash flow target "very attainable," and points to a second-quarter print of $631m — Boeing's first meaningful positive free cash flow quarter in years — as evidence the underlying trajectory is real. But each slip in the production timeline pushes that $10bn goalpost further out, and with the 10-year Treasury yield sitting near 5%, the present value of cash promised in 2028 or 2029 is worth markedly less than cash promised in 2026. That arithmetic, as much as the delay itself, is what moved the stock.

Why the pattern matters more than the delay

A single missed month on a factory floor is not, on its own, remarkable for an aircraft maker still working through the aftermath of the Max's 2019-2020 grounding and the 2024 quality crisis. What is harder to wave away is the repetition: Boeing has now pushed back its cash-generation timeline enough times that investors are pricing in a structural gap between what management projects and what the factory floor delivers. Ortberg was hired in 2024 in part to close that gap and rebuild credibility with regulators, airlines and shareholders alike. Wing production at Renton was flagged as a fixable, mechanical problem — not a redesign of the jet or a supplier crisis — which makes the miss more a story about execution than about engineering.

Boeing carries a record backlog of $715bn, including $597bn in commercial orders across more than 6,200 aircraft, so demand is not the constraint. The constraint is Boeing's own ability to convert a factory back to full health years after grounding it. Watch the Max 10 certification decision, expected imminently, and the next quarterly cash flow print for whether the Renton wing line has actually turned the corner — or whether the $10bn target quietly slips again.
