NHS trust admits women had unnecessary mastectomies in breast cancer care scandal

Darlington Memorial Hospital, one of the two main sites run by County Durham and Darlington NHS Foundation Trust. — Steven Fruitsmaak / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A decade of ignored warnings at a County Durham NHS trust has left at least 270 women harmed by missed diagnoses and unnecessary mastectomies, with litigation, a police probe and a public inquiry now looming.

County Durham and Darlington NHS Foundation Trust has now confirmed what women in the North East had been alleging for more than a decade: its breast cancer service caused serious, preventable harm on a wide scale. Independent clinical reviews have identified at least 270 women harmed by missed diagnoses, delayed treatment or surgery more extensive than their cancer required — including mastectomies that reviewers now say should never have happened. Forty-five cases are classed as moderate harm, 19 as severe, and one death is under investigation.

The figures come from an ongoing lookback review covering 1,472 breast surgery cases treated at the trust's Darlington Memorial and University Hospital of North Durham sites between January 2023 and February 2025. Of the first 315 cases fully assessed, 51 — roughly one in six — were found to involve moderate or severe harm, a rate that, if it held across the full cohort, would push the final toll well past current figures. Trust leaders have said the reviewers are working through the remainder of the caseload, with results expected in stages rather than all at once.

A decade of ignored warnings

What distinguishes this scandal from a cluster of clinical errors is its duration. An independent external review of governance, published in November 2025, described "profoundly systemic" failures stretching back roughly ten years, and found that "persistent concerns" had been raised internally as early as 2012 — the same year the trust lost its accreditation to train breast surgeons. Staff, the review found, did not feel able to speak up, and there was no effective peer review to challenge individual surgeons' decisions.

Some of the clinical practice identified was, on its face, outdated rather than merely mistaken. Reviewers found continued reliance on fine needle aspiration, a sampling technique largely abandoned elsewhere in the NHS because it produces inadequate tissue samples and raises the risk of missed or delayed cancer diagnoses. Breast reconstruction after mastectomy was offered to just 7.5% of patients, against a national benchmark of 25% — a gap that, patient solicitors argue, reflects a service that was not routinely giving women the full range of options a modern cancer pathway should provide.

The Care Quality Commission has since downgraded the trust's "well-led" rating from Good to Inadequate, and Durham Constabulary has opened a criminal investigation into the breast service, working with the Crown Prosecution Service to determine whether any offences were committed. The trust has set up a dedicated support line and says nearly 1,500 records are still being examined.

The surgeon at the centre, and a conflict of interest

Amir Bhatti, the trust's former clinical lead for breast surgery, was restricted from operating on 20 February 2025 while investigations continued. He challenged the restriction in the High Court, arguing it should be lifted; in May 2026 the court sided with the trust, finding its decision had been properly grounded in patient safety concerns. The trust said afterward that its focus remained on patient support and on completing the service improvements already under way.

A separate and, for regulators, arguably more troubling detail has emerged from the review: between 2022 and 2024, more than half of patients referred for urgent breast cancer checks were initially routed to one of two external "two-week-wait" diagnostic clinics — Durham Surgical Services and Cathedral Surgical Services — of which Bhatti was a director. The arrangement has drawn scrutiny over whether referral pathways were shaped by a treating clinician's own financial interest, a question that sits squarely inside the remit of NHS England's conflict-of-interest guidance for consultants running private practice alongside NHS roles.

Steve Russell, the trust's newly appointed chief executive, has repeated the trust's apology to "the patients, families, communities and colleagues who have been affected," and says the breast service today is "very different" from the one under review. Patients and campaigners are not satisfied that internal reform is sufficient oversight. Amelia Green, whose sister Michaela died of cancer in 2009 and who has since campaigned alongside fellow patient Nadeen Lister, has led a petition — now past 1,200 signatures — demanding a full statutory public inquiry rather than a trust-led review. "There are thousands of women affected," Green has said, arguing that transparency has so far come only in pieces, through leaked reviews and journalist requests rather than proactive disclosure.

What litigation means for the NHS's books

Slater + Gordon and Hudgell Solicitors are both now running dedicated intake for County Durham and Darlington claims, and have described a caseload that could run into the hundreds once the full 1,472-case lookback concludes. Clinical negligence claims against NHS trusts are indemnified through NHS Resolution, which already pays out several billion pounds a year in claims and legal costs across the service; a scandal of this scale — combining missed cancers, unwarranted surgery and psychological harm — points toward a settlement bill that will land on national, not just local, NHS finances.

The wider question is regulatory rather than actuarial. This is at least the third major NHS scandal in under a decade in which a failing service ran for years despite internal warnings before an external review forced disclosure, following the maternity failures at Shrewsbury and Telford and East Kent. Each has prompted promises of stronger local governance rather than a change to how the CQC or NHS England catches slow-burning systemic failures before they reach hundreds of patients. Whether County Durham and Darlington becomes the case that finally shifts that model — through a public inquiry, tighter conflict-of-interest rules for consultants running parallel private clinics, or mandatory external peer review for surgical specialties — is now a live question for the Department of Health and Social Care as the remainder of the lookback review reports through the rest of 2026.
