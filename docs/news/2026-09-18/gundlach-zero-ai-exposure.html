DoubleLine's Gundlach says he holds zero exposure to 'AI epicenter' stocks

In one sentence
DoubleLine's Jeffrey Gundlach says he holds virtually no exposure to AI stocks, citing stretched valuations and early stress in AI-linked corporate bonds.

Jeffrey Gundlach, the DoubleLine Capital founder whose bond-market calls have earned him the nickname "Bond King," says his own portfolio holds none of the stocks driving this year's market gains. Asked about his exposure to the handful of companies powering the artificial-intelligence trade, Gundlach's answer was blunt: virtually zero.

Speaking on The Julia La Roche Show, Gundlach described wanting to be "out of the epicenter" of the AI race. His equity allocation — 30% of his overall portfolio — sits entirely in an equal-weight index of roughly 440 of the highest-revenue companies in the market, rather than the cap-weighted benchmarks where a handful of AI-linked mega-caps now dominate. "We're already close enough to that tipping point that I'd rather step away from the epicenter," he said.

Why the aversion

Gundlach's case rests on two legs. The first is valuation: he pointed to the S&P 500's Shiller cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings ratio, which he put above 42. Historically, he noted, whenever that measure has exceeded 35, real returns over the following decade have been negative — commonly by around 5% a year after inflation.

The second is credit. Gundlach has been tracking newly issued bonds from AI-linked borrowers and says the stress shows up almost immediately. "The spread starts to blow out," he said, sometimes within days or weeks of issuance — a signal, in his view, that bond investors are pricing risks equity markets have not yet absorbed. Debt markets have historically led equity markets in flagging credit problems, and Gundlach has argued that a diversified mix of investment-grade corporate bonds offers less protection from an AI-driven pullback than most investors assume.

He has also taken aim at the financing structures underpinning the AI buildout. Referring to Nvidia's push to mobilize more than $500 billion in AI infrastructure financing with a group of banks, Gundlach compared the arrangement to issuing 30-year asset-backed securities against warehouses of "newly engineered bananas of unknown life" — his shorthand for financial engineering dressed up with favorable credit ratings. He argues that AI and hyperscale cloud firms are chasing addressable markets that would amount to roughly a quarter of global GDP, calling that "a scenario that simply cannot hold up."

The trade he's making instead

Rather than shorting AI names outright, Gundlach has rotated into equal-weight equity exposure, shorter-duration corporate bonds, utilities, gold, and local-currency emerging-market debt. He has previously urged investors to hold around 20% of a portfolio in cash, describing the broader market as one of the least healthy of his career. Technology now accounts for roughly 40% of the S&P 500 by his estimate, which is precisely the concentration his equal-weight approach is built to avoid — the same handful of names that pushed the index higher this year could just as easily drag it down together.

Gundlach separately criticized the Federal Reserve's latest quarter-point rate cut as insufficient given the central bank's own forecast that inflation won't return to 2% until 2029, calling monetary policy "behind the curve."

What it doesn't prove

One manager's zero weighting is not a market call that AI stocks are about to fall — DoubleLine is fundamentally a bond shop, and Gundlach has flagged market tops before that took years to arrive, if they arrived at all. But his positioning adds a data point to a growing list of credit-market signals — widening spreads on AI-linked debt, scrutiny of vendor-financing arrangements between chipmakers and cloud providers — that suggest not everyone financing the AI buildout is convinced the economics close. The next test comes from the credit market itself: if spreads on freshly issued AI-sector bonds keep widening rather than settling after issuance, that would lend more weight to Gundlach's argument than any single interview can.
