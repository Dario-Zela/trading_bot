Diageo's Johnnie Walker and Bell's whisky supplies hit by worker walkout

Diageo's Cameronbridge distillery in Fife, Europe's largest grain distillery and the source of whisky base for Johnnie Walker, Bell's and other blends. — James Allan / Geograph, CC BY-SA 2.0
In one sentence
Unite members will strike at Diageo's Cameronbridge distillery from 28 September to 15 October over job cuts, threatening the grain-whisky supply behind Johnnie Walker and Bell's just as Diageo's third quarter closes.

Diageo's Scottish supply chain is about to run into a wall of picket lines just as the group closes out its third quarter. More than 100 Unite members at the Cameronbridge distillery in Fife will walk out on Monday 28 September, the start of a rolling three-week stoppage running to 15 October, in a dispute over roughly 10 job cuts at a site that makes the grain-whisky base for Johnnie Walker and Bell's.

"There is no justification for slashing hundreds of jobs across its operations when it is raking in hundreds of millions of profit."

That's Unite's line on Diageo, and it is not a small claim to level at a company that also insists it needs to cut. Cameronbridge — Europe's largest grain distillery, and by Diageo's own description the birthplace of Cameron Brig — employs around 200 people, with roughly 70 roles under review. It supplies not just Johnnie Walker and Bell's but J&B, Vat 69, White Horse, Black & White and Haig Club, plus the neutral spirit behind Smirnoff, Gordon's and Tanqueray.

A dispute rooted in a $1bn overhaul

The walkout is the sharpest local flashpoint yet in Diageo's group-wide restructuring, a $1bn cost programme under which the company has already trimmed headcount by around 2,000 globally. Diageo Scotland's after-tax profit fell to £595m in 2025 from £736m the year before — a decline management points to as the rationale for the cuts, and one Unite points to as evidence the reductions are unnecessary. The company's position, delivered through a spokesperson, was blunt: "We are disappointed by the decision to strike. The proposal to remove up to 10 roles at Cameronbridge Distillery is necessary as we maintain reduced production volumes to protect the long-term competitiveness of our business."

Unite's industrial officer Dougie Orchardson takes the opposite view of what happens next: "Industrial action will inevitably result in supply shortages of many people's favourite drinks." Diageo disagrees. Asked directly, a spokesperson said: "We do not envisage any supply shortages," citing contingency planning to keep bottling lines fed through the stoppage.

Timing that bites

Whichever side is right about shortages, the timing is awkward. The strike dates fall squarely across the final weeks of Diageo's fiscal third quarter, and Johnnie Walker is one of the group's highest-margin brands — the kind of volume that shows up quickly in scotch shipment data if a distillery goes quiet for even part of three weeks. Cameronbridge does not bottle the finished product, but it supplies the grain spirit that blenders need on a schedule; a three-week interruption to that upstream flow is the sort of thing that shows up downstream weeks later, not immediately, which is exactly why analysts will be watching Diageo's next trading update rather than this week's headlines for the real answer.

For now, the numbers in dispute are small next to Diageo's scale — 10 jobs, 100 picketing workers, one Scottish site among dozens of Diageo facilities worldwide. What makes it worth watching is the overlap of a live industrial dispute with a restructuring programme still finding its edges, and a spirits maker that has already told the market its margins are under pressure. The next marker is 28 September, when the first pickets go up; after that, whether Diageo's contingency planning holds through to 15 October will say more about the group's operational resilience than either side's public statements do today.
