NATO's Rutte presses PM Burnham for a 'credible path' to 3.5% GDP defence spending

NATO Secretary-General Mark Rutte, who is pressing allies including the UK for annual defence-spending increments toward the 2035 target. — Christophe Licoppe / European Commission, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
NATO's Rutte pressed PM Burnham to commit to yearly spending increases, not just a 2035 deadline, toward the alliance's 3.5%-of-GDP defence target.

NATO's secretary-general left Downing Street this week with praise for Andy Burnham's rhetoric and a pointed demand attached to it: show the money, and show it every year, not just at the finish line.

Mark Rutte met the prime minister on Wednesday, then Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband and Defence Secretary Wes Streeting on Thursday, in a two-day visit meant to test how firmly Britain's new government stands behind the target NATO leaders set at The Hague last summer — 3.5% of GDP on core military spending by 2035, with a further 1.5% on broader security and resilience costs on top. "I'm really happy with the Prime Minister... really committing to the 3.5%," Rutte told the Press Association afterwards, "but also committing to the need for a credible path to get there." Then the line that mattered more than the praise: "You cannot spend it today, but you can also not wait 'til 2035. And that is why you need a credible path towards that 3.5%, and yearly increments."

It is a small phrase — yearly increments — but it is doing a lot of work. NATO has learned, over successive summits, that a distant deadline without intermediate checkpoints is an invitation to defer. Rutte is trying to close that loophole before Britain, or anyone else, opens it.

The gap between the pledge and the plan

Britain's actual commitment on paper stops well short of the 2035 target. The government has budgeted to raise defence spending to 2.7% of GDP by 2027/28 — itself an acceleration on the 2.5% pathway set under the previous government — but has published nothing describing how it gets from there to 3.5% over the following seven years. Burnham's government has said only that the fuller trajectory will be set out at next year's spending review.

That gap is not accidental. It is the residue of a fight that already claimed one Cabinet career. John Healey resigned as defence secretary in June, under Keir Starmer, telling his prime minister that the financial settlement behind the government's own Defence Investment Plan fell well short of what the country needed and accusing the Treasury of being "unwilling" to commit the resources required. Healey is now Chancellor under Burnham — which means the man who quit demanding more defence money now sits on the side of the table that has to find it, a reversal that has not gone unnoticed in Westminster.

Rutte's framing — 50% of Russia's state budget now goes on defence, he has noted repeatedly — is meant to make the arithmetic feel less like an accounting exercise and more like a deterrence problem. "Let's not be naive about what he might do against NATO," he said of Vladimir Putin during the visit. The alliance's institutional memory of Ukraine, and of allies who spent the 2010s hollowing out their militaries on the assumption peace was permanent, sits behind every push for firmer numbers.

Burnham's domestic bind

The difficulty for Burnham is that the 3.5% question does not arrive in isolation — it lands on top of a government already fighting over where the money for anything comes from. At Prime Minister's Questions this week, pressed by Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch on whether the UK would hit 3% of GDP by 2030, Burnham refused to commit to the interim figure and instead drew a different line: "We do everything to support national security, but it can't come at the expense of social security... This side of the house will not be forced to choose between the two, and we will set out a plan to get to 3.5% by 2035 at the spending review."

Badenoch had suggested the money could come from housing benefit; Conservative officials accused Burnham of "prioritising the ballooning benefits bill over our national security." Burnham's own line is narrower than a flat refusal to trim welfare — he has previously said he wants to "get the welfare bill down" through measures aimed at young people out of work or training, rather than the disability-payment cuts that Starmer's government tried and failed to push through. But the political signal is unmistakable: this prime minister is not going to fund NATO's target by squeezing the benefits system, which leaves tax rises, borrowing, or slower delivery as the remaining levers — none of them costless with Reform UK holding a commanding poll lead and gilt yields already sitting near three-decade highs.

What The Hague actually requires

The target itself is less simple than "3.5% of GDP" suggests. The Hague Summit Declaration, agreed by NATO leaders in June 2025 in the alliance's shortest final communiqué in decades, set a two-tier formula: 3.5% of GDP on core military expenditure — personnel, equipment, operations — and a further 1.5% on broader security-related spending such as cyberdefence, critical infrastructure and industrial capacity, for a nominal 5% headline figure that was the price of securing Donald Trump's continued commitment to the alliance. Every member bar Spain, which secured an exemption and committed instead to 2.1%, signed on. National roadmaps were due by the middle of this year; a collective progress review is scheduled for 2029, with 2035 as the hard deadline. There is no enforcement mechanism beyond diplomatic pressure and, implicitly, Washington's patience.

That absence of teeth is precisely why Rutte is leaning so hard on process rather than just outcome. A country that commits to 3.5% by 2035 but back-loads every increase to the final two years is, in practical terms, no different from a country that never committed at all — and NATO's planners cannot build force structures around a number that might arrive all at once in 2034. Yearly increments make the pledge auditable.

Britain's own roadmap, promised for next year's spending review, will be the first real test of whether Burnham's government treats that distinction as binding or as another deadline to be managed. The 2029 alliance-wide review will be the next external checkpoint after that — but for a government already navigating a leadership transition, a restless backbench and a Chancellor who once resigned over exactly this dispute, the more immediate pressure point is domestic: the spending review itself, whenever in the coming months it lands.
