Shell warns shrinking LNG buffers leave global gas market exposed

Ras Laffan Industrial City, Qatar, home to the world's largest LNG export hub and the site of March's Iranian missile strikes. — Matthew Smith / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Shell says roughly 36 million tonnes of lost LNG capacity is exhausting spare market buffers, a warning with direct implications for European winter gas prices.

Shell's latest reckoning of the global LNG market carries a blunt message for anyone budgeting for a cold European winter: the cushion is gone. In prepared remarks around its LNG Outlook update and at Gastech in September, Shell executives put a number on it — roughly 36 million tonnes of Middle Eastern LNG supply, mostly Qatari and Emirati, has been absent from the market so far this year compared with 2025. Only about 20 million tonnes of that has been made good by new US and Canadian cargoes. The rest has come out of demand destruction and drawn-down storage, which is another way of saying the market has been quietly eating its own reserves for months.

Where the tonnes went

The hole traces back to March, when Iranian missiles struck Ras Laffan Industrial City, the sprawling complex 80km north of Doha that handles close to a fifth of the world's LNG trade. The strikes disabled Trains 4 and 6, wiping out about 12.8 million tonnes a year of capacity — roughly 17% of Qatar's export base — and also knocked out Shell's Pearl gas-to-liquids plant on the same site. Qatar's south production area, hit hardest, saw capacity cut from 36 million tonnes annually to 24 million. Wood Mackenzie has said a meaningful share of that lost capacity won't return for years, since replacement gas turbines carry lead times of two to four years. Two of three trains at the older Qatargas-1 site have since been restarted, but full recovery still hinges on stable transit through the Strait of Hormuz, which has not returned to pre-war shipping patterns even with a ceasefire nominally in place.

Shell's Peter Clarke counterpart at ExxonMobil made the same distinction Shell has been drawing all year: reopening the strait to traffic is not the same as restoring the trade flows that existed before it closed. Buyers who redirected long-term contracts elsewhere, insurers who repriced war risk, and shipowners who rerouted tonnage do not simply snap back once the missiles stop. Cederic Cremers, Shell's president of integrated gas, described the episode as "a system-wide shock with disruption cascading across all segments of the economy," while allowing that the LNG industry had "proved resilient." He was notably less sanguine on price, telling reporters flatly that "the prices are too high right now" — an unusual admission from a seller.

Why the buffer matters more than the shortfall

The 36-million-tonne figure alone doesn't explain the alarm; global LNG trade runs to roughly 400 million tonnes a year, so the absolute loss is a manageable single-digit percentage. What has Shell and its peers exercised is the depletion of the slack that normally exists to absorb a shock like this one. Spot cargoes that once floated between Asian and European buyers depending on price have been drawn down. Storage that would typically be topped up over summer has instead been used to plug the Qatari gap. The International Energy Forum has estimated spare liquefaction capacity fell around 14 billion cubic metres short of the volumes that would normally have moved through Hormuz in 2025 — a buffer that, in a calmer year, gives the market room to shrug off a terminal outage or a cold snap. That room has largely disappeared.

Europe enters the October-to-March heating season with storage at about 67–68% of capacity, against a five-year seasonal average near 88% and short of the EU's own 80%-by-December target — a shortfall put at somewhere between 14 and 20 billion cubic metres with, as one analyst note put it, no injection-season buffer left to draw on. Dutch TTF, the European benchmark, has been trading near €80/MWh in mid-September, having touched roughly €84 earlier in the month — levels last seen consistently during the 2022 crisis. Analysts at Morgan Stanley have sketched a base case near €85/MWh for the winter, a bear case near €50 if Qatari flows normalise by late November into a mild season, and a bull case as high as €210 if they do not and the winter turns cold. Wood Mackenzie's Simon Flowers summarised the asymmetry: "There's a big risk if we have a cold winter because there's very little spare supply available."

The read-through for markets

For traders, the mechanism is straightforward even if the outcome isn't: thin buffers convert ordinary variance — a colder week in Germany, a delayed cargo, a fresh incident near Hormuz — into outsized price moves, because there is no longer a comfortable margin of spare supply to absorb the surprise. That is a different risk profile from 2022's crisis, which was driven by the sudden loss of Russian pipeline gas; this one is layered on top of an already-tight Asian market and a shipping lane still shadowed by the Iran conflict, whose ceasefire has continued to fray past the 200-day mark. Energy volatility of this kind tends not to stay contained to gas contracts. It feeds directly into European industrial input costs, headline inflation prints, and — as the Bank of England's own split verdict on rates this month showed — central bank calculations already complicated by elevated gilt yields.

The next real test arrives with the first sustained cold snap, whenever it comes. Watch European storage withdrawal rates against the seasonal norm, and watch whether Qatar's south-site trains come back online ahead of Wood Mackenzie's multi-year timeline. Until either buffer — storage or Qatari capacity — visibly rebuilds, Shell's warning stands: the market has little room left to absorb another shock.
