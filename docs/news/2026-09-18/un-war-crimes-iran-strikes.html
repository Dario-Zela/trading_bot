UN experts say grounds to believe US committed war crimes in strikes on Iran

The Human Rights and Alliance of Civilizations Room in Geneva, where the UN Human Rights Council meets and where the fact-finding mission's report will be formally presented. — Ludovic Courtès / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
A UN fact-finding mission says the US likely committed war crimes in February strikes that killed 178 civilians at an Iranian school and sports complex, adding legal pressure to a fraying war.

Nearly seven months after the war between the United States and Iran opened with one of the most concentrated decapitation strikes in modern military history, the conflict has acquired a new and awkward dimension: a formal international finding that the strikes themselves may have broken the laws of war.

An independent panel of investigators appointed by the UN Human Rights Council said on Thursday it had found "reasonable grounds" to believe the United States committed war crimes in two of the airstrikes that opened the war on 28 February — attacks the mission says killed at least 178 civilians, including roughly 120 children, at a school and a sports complex hundreds of miles from the military and leadership targets the opening barrage was designed to destroy.

What the mission found

The Independent International Fact-Finding Mission on Iran, chaired by the Bangladeshi jurist Sara Hossain and created by the Human Rights Council in 2022, examined two strikes carried out on the war's opening day. One used Tomahawk cruise missiles against the Tree of Tayyiba Elementary School in Minab, in Iran's south, killing more than 150 people. The other struck a sports complex and residential area in Lamerd using precision munitions that dispersed tungsten pellets over a wide radius, killing 22 more.

The investigators concluded the school was not collateral damage from a strike on a nearby Revolutionary Guard facility but the intended point of impact — despite what the report describes as unmistakable markers of civilian use, including "child-oriented murals painted on exterior walls, school signage, and playground areas." Their finding: the US had failed "to do everything feasible to verify that it was a military objective," meeting the legal threshold for the war crime of an indiscriminate attack.

Those two strikes were a small part of a far larger operation. In the opening minutes of the war, US and Israeli forces launched close to 900 strikes within twelve hours, aimed squarely at Iran's command structure. Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei was killed, along with roughly 40 senior officials — among them the defence minister, the IRGC's overall commander, the head of the Basij paramilitary force, the intelligence minister and the armed forces' chief of staff. Whatever one makes of the legality of targeting a state's top leadership in an active conflict, the mission's war crimes finding is not about that campaign. It concerns two strikes, on the same day, that hit a school and a housing block instead.

A report with two targets

The mission did not train its findings on Washington alone. The same report accuses Iran's government of crimes against humanity in its crackdown on the protest movement that has run alongside the war since 2025, describing killings by state security forces "on a staggering scale" across every province. Investigators documented at least 221 children killed, tens of thousands held in arbitrary detention with reports of torture and solitary confinement, and at least 29 executions following summary trials, with more than 70 further death sentences pending. Tehran has put its own death toll from the unrest at just over 3,000; the mission suggests the real figure is higher.

That dual indictment matters for how the report lands politically. It gives Washington an obvious rhetorical exit — treat the document as proof of UN even-handedness gone wrong, or dismiss it wholesale. A State Department official did the latter within hours of publication, saying the US does not extend the report credibility and accusing the Human Rights Council of pushing "anti-American rhetoric and antisemitism" that "appeases repressive regimes." The White House has previously and repeatedly denied that the February strikes constituted war crimes.

Why a non-binding report still counts

The fact-finding mission has no power to prosecute anyone. Its report is a recommendation to the Human Rights Council, not a verdict, and the United States is not a member of the International Criminal Court, which limits the avenues by which any of this could turn into an actual case. But documents like this rarely die where they're published. They build an evidentiary record — witness testimony, satellite imagery, munitions analysis — that other courts, truth commissions or future administrations can draw on years later, the same function similar UN mechanisms have served for Syria and the occupied Palestinian territories.

More immediately, the finding narrows the diplomatic space available to end a war that, per Iran war ceasefire still fraying past the 200-day mark, has already produced a ceasefire in name only. A negotiated settlement typically requires both sides to accept some version of amnesty or forbearance on past conduct; a formal war-crimes determination against one party makes that harder to sell domestically, on either side. It also adds a legal frame to a conflict that financial markets have mostly been pricing as a supply-side oil shock — a dynamic visible in the surge past $100 a barrel after pipeline attacks disrupted Saudi and regional exports.

The mission's report goes to the Human Rights Council for formal presentation in the coming weeks, where member states will debate a response that could range from a strongly worded resolution to nothing at all — the Council has no enforcement power of its own. The more consequential test is whether any government or court with actual jurisdiction picks up the evidentiary trail the mission has laid down. Until then, the war grinds on largely as before, with one more entry on the growing list of grievances neither side is likely to forget at the negotiating table.
