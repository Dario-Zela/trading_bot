FED RATES · By M. Halloway · 2026-09-18 · 6 min read

Fed hikes rates for first time in three years as oil-driven inflation bites; yields ease, stocks rally

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C., where the FOMC voted unanimously to raise rates. — AgnosticPreachersKid / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
The Fed hiked rates to 3.75%-4% for the first time in three years, blaming Iran-war oil inflation rather than economic overheating, and signalled more hikes to come.

The Federal Reserve raised its benchmark interest rate on Wednesday for the first time in more than three years, ending a long easing cycle and opening a new one — but not for the reason textbooks describe. The move was a response to a war, not an overheating economy.

The Federal Open Market Committee voted 12-0 to lift the federal funds rate a quarter point to a target range of 3.75%-4%, its first increase since July 2023. The unanimity mattered as much as the number: two months earlier, at the Fed's July meeting, only three of the committee's members had favoured a hike. By September, all of them did. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh, who took the gavel in May after one of the most divisive confirmation fights in the central bank's history, framed the decision as necessary even though the Fed itself has little power over its root cause.

"We cannot affect any individual price," Warsh told reporters after the meeting, citing oil and groceries specifically. "But what we can do and will do is ensure that any change in relative prices don't broaden out, don't have second and third order effects on the economy."

A war, not a boom, is driving the cycle

The proximate cause is the war between the United States, Israel and Iran, now in its ninth month, and the fuel-price spiral it has fed. Crude has traded above $100 a barrel for weeks on fears over the Strait of Hormuz, through which more than a fifth of the world's oil passes, and on repeated attacks on Gulf energy infrastructure. US pump prices have risen more than 40% since the war began; AAA put the national average above $4.30 a gallon in mid-September, and diesel has set records near $5.85. By the Fed's own informal reckoning, the war is now costing the average American household roughly $700 in added fuel spending.

That is the distinction traders need to sit with. The last hiking cycle, in 2022 and 2023, answered demand that had outrun supply — stimulus-flush consumers, a labour market running hot, goods and services prices rising broadly. This one answers a supply shock in a single, geopolitically volatile commodity. Core inflation excluding energy has stayed closer to target than the headline number suggests; August data released the week before the meeting showed core prices rising faster than expected, but the visible driver at the pump is oil, not wages or rents. Warsh's own language — distinguishing a one-off "relative price" move from a broader inflationary drift — was as much a signal to markets as the rate decision itself: the Fed is trying to keep expectations anchored around an oil shock, not fight a 2022-style overheating with a 2022-style playbook.

The committee's updated projections underline that this is not a one-and-done move. Of the 18 FOMC participants, 16 pencilled in at least one more hike this year, and four saw room for two. The message: as long as oil stays elevated and the war unresolved, the Fed intends to keep leaning against it.

Markets flinch, then recover

The immediate market reaction ran through the standard hawkish-shock playbook before reversing. Stocks sold off on the announcement Wednesday afternoon — the Dow closed down 0.17% at 52,002.01 — as the 10-year Treasury yield spiked to 5.041% intraday, its highest level since July 2007, before settling just above 5%. That yield move was less about the 25-basis-point hike itself, which was fully priced in, than about the dot plot: a Fed that expects to keep raising rates competes harder for capital against a swelling supply of corporate debt, Warsh noted, and Treasuries had already been climbing for weeks on that basis.

By Thursday, the mood had flipped. The Dow rose 316 points, or 0.61%, to 51,778.04; the S&P 500 gained 1.14% to 7,637.76; the Nasdaq added 1.69% to close at 26,418.30. The 10-year yield eased back from its multi-year peak, and crude fell alongside it — Brent lost roughly $1 to settle at $104.82 a barrel, WTI shed 52 cents to $101.91 — as Saudi officials signalled the East-West pipeline damaged in recent attacks would resume operations within days and offered Asian refiners additional cargoes via ship-to-ship transfers near Oman.

That combination is the one worth pausing on. A hawkish Fed, falling yields, falling oil and rallying equities do not normally arrive in the same 24 hours. Higher rates are supposed to support the dollar and weigh on commodities priced in it; instead, oil fell mainly because a supply fix appeared to be materializing on the ground in Saudi Arabia, not because the Fed made crude more expensive to hold. Stocks, in turn, rallied less on the rate decision than on relief that the geopolitical premium in energy might be starting to unwind — with the Fed's hike, once delivered, no longer an overhang of uncertainty.

What the pivot means for the curve

For traders used to reading Fed tightening as a signal about domestic demand, that decoupling is the real story. A hiking cycle driven by an oil shock behaves differently across the curve than one driven by an overheating labour market. Short-end rates rise on the Fed's reaction function, as they always do. But the long end is being pulled two directions at once — up by concerns over debt supply and persistent headline inflation, down by any hint that the oil shock itself might fade, whether through a ceasefire, a supply fix like the Saudi pipeline restart, or demand destruction from $4-plus gasoline. That tension explains why the 10-year touched a 19-year high one day and eased the next without any change in the Fed's own guidance.

It also complicates the read-through to other asset correlations. Historically, Fed hikes strengthen the dollar and pressure energy and gold; a war-driven hike, by contrast, can coincide with a weaker dollar case if markets believe the Fed will have to reverse course quickly once the geopolitical shock passes, or if a ceasefire would do more to cool inflation than another quarter point ever could. Warsh's insistence that the Fed's job is to stop the oil shock from "broadening out" — not to bring oil down itself — is effectively an admission that the central bank is a secondary actor in this cycle. The primary variable is the war.

What comes next

The Fed's own dot plot points to another hike before year-end, most likely at the late-October or December meeting, contingent on inflation data that will keep reflecting pump prices for as long as the war continues. The more decisive catalyst sits outside the Fed's control: any sign of a durable ceasefire, or of Gulf producers restoring enough supply to break crude's grip above $100, would do more to reshape the rate path than anything the FOMC votes on. Until then, traders pricing the curve are effectively pricing two wars at once — one in the Fed's statement language, and one in the Strait of Hormuz.
