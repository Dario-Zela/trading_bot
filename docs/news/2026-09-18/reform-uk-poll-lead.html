Reform UK holds strong poll lead as Burnham's Labour struggles to stabilise

Andy Burnham's official portrait as Prime Minister, taken at 10 Downing Street in July 2026. — Alecsandra Dragoi / Crown Copyright (Open Government Licence v3.0)
In one sentence
Reform UK and Labour are essentially tied in the polls, while PM Burnham and Chancellor Healey register some of the weakest personal ratings on record.

Andy Burnham has been prime minister for exactly nine weeks, and the polling verdict on his premiership so far is: not disastrous, but not the recovery Labour needed either.

The latest PollCheck seven-poll moving average, updated 17 September, puts Labour on 25.4% against Reform UK's 23.3% — a lead of barely two points, well inside a general sense of statistical noise. YouGov's most recent voting-intention poll, fielded 13-14 September, has the two parties tied at 23% apiece. Other pollsters tell a different story: Ipsos fieldwork earlier this year had Reform ahead by anywhere from two to eight points. Which party is "winning" now depends almost entirely on which house's methodology a reader trusts — an unusual state of affairs for a governing party that spent the past year trailing by double digits.

A weak start, then a weaker one

Burnham's personal numbers explain why nobody in government is celebrating the tightened race. His first Ipsos satisfaction rating after taking office in July — 29% satisfied — was worse than Keir Starmer's first reading as PM (37%), and nowhere close to Theresa May (54%), David Cameron (57%) or Tony Blair (65%) at the equivalent point in their premierships. It was, in other words, one of the weakest opening satisfaction scores Ipsos has recorded for an incoming prime minister.

The trend since hasn't reversed it. YouGov's September favourability tracker shows Burnham's unfavourable rating climbing six points to 39% — the highest since he entered Downing Street — even as his favourable rating held flat at 46%. Ipsos's own job-satisfaction question, a stricter measure, now finds only around three in ten Britons satisfied with his performance, with roughly four in ten saying they simply don't know.

Chancellor John Healey fares no better. YouGov puts his favourability at 16%, unchanged since August, while his unfavourable share has risen five points to 24% — and six in ten voters still have no opinion of him at all, a sign the Treasury has yet to make much of an impression either way. Ipsos separately puts overall government satisfaction at just 18%, against roughly seven in ten dissatisfied.

Why it matters for markets

None of this amounts to a crisis signal on its own — a two-point polling gap is not a mandate crisis, and Burnham's net favourability, while sliding, remains positive by the standards of recent premierships. But a government with thin personal capital and a razor-thin lead over an insurgent opposition has less room to spend on unpopular fiscal choices, whether that's the tax rises needed to fund the defence-spending path NATO's Mark Rutte has been pressing for, or further gilt issuance at yields already sitting near three-decade highs. Sterling and gilts have so far shrugged off the polling noise, largely because no single pollster commands consensus and no election is imminent. That calculus changes quickly if the averages converge on a clear Reform lead.

The next test is due before month-end, when Ipsos and YouGov both refresh their trackers; a by-election in Makerfield, watched closely inside Downing Street as a proxy for Burnham's personal standing, will offer the first real-world check on whether the polling divergence reflects genuine volatility or just sampling noise.
