Nike adds LVMH heir Alexandre Arnault to board after $200bn market-cap slide

Alexandre Arnault, the LVMH executive and Bernard Arnault's son, now joins Nike's board. — Sedentaire / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Nike named LVMH heir Alexandre Arnault to its board days after dropping out of the S&P 100 following an $200bn-plus market-value collapse since 2021.

Nike has added Alexandre Arnault, the 34-year-old son of LVMH chairman Bernard Arnault, to its board of directors — a move that lands eight days after the sportswear giant was dropped from the S&P 100 index of America's largest blue-chip companies.

The appointment, announced September 17, expands Nike's board to twelve members. Arnault currently serves as deputy chief executive of Moët Hennessy, LVMH's wines and spirits arm, a post he has held since early 2025. Before that he spent four years running product, communications and industrial strategy at Tiffany & Co. — where he helped broker a streetwear collaboration with, of all companies, Nike — and four years as chief executive of the German luggage maker Rimowa, where he pushed collaborations with Supreme and Off-White. He also sits on LVMH's own board and is a trustee of New York's Museum of Modern Art.

"His experience across innovation, digital transformation and brand building will be an asset," Nike chief executive Elliott Hill said in a statement announcing the appointment.

A board seat against a $200bn backdrop

The timing is not incidental. Nike's stock has fallen from a November 2021 peak near $177 to roughly $36 this week, a decline of almost 80% that has erased more than $200bn in market capitalization — from a peak value near $290bn to about $54bn today. That slide dragged Nike out of the S&P 100 on September 21, ending an 18-year run in the index of the market's hundred largest companies. Nike remains in the broader S&P 500.

The underlying business has been sliding in parallel. Fiscal 2026 revenue came in at $46.4 billion, down 2% on a currency-neutral basis, with fourth-quarter Nike Direct sales off 7% and digital sales down 12%. The company has spent much of the year trying to claw back control of its own distribution, pulling online-sales rights from wholesale partners it had leaned on during the pandemic boom, while contending with eight straight quarters of falling sales in China.

David Swartz, an analyst at Morningstar, told Fortune that Nike would not make a board move like this without expecting a tangible payoff, though he noted the arrangement could just as easily be read as a finishing-school posting for Arnault ahead of a bigger role at LVMH someday. Either reading points to the same underlying fact: a company built on performance footwear and marketing muscle is now borrowing credibility from a conglomerate whose luxury labels have weathered the same consumer pullback far better than Nike's own.

The next marker is Nike's fiscal first-quarter earnings, due in early October, which will show whether Hill's turnaround — tighter inventory, direct-distribution clawback, a renewed push in China — is bending the sales trend before the holiday quarter.
