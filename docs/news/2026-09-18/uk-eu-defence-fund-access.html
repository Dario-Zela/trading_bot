Chancellor to press EU finance ministers on UK access to joint defence-industry fund

Chancellor John Healey, who took over the Treasury from Rachel Reeves in July's reshuffle, is renewing the UK's bid for access to the EU's SAFE defence fund. — Lauren Hurley / No 10 Downing Street (Open Government Licence v3.0)
In one sentence
Chancellor John Healey asked EU finance ministers in Dublin to admit the UK to the €150bn SAFE defence fund, reopening a bid Rachel Reeves shelved over a €6bn entry fee.

John Healey spent Thursday in Dublin doing something his predecessor at the Treasury tried and abandoned: asking the European Union to let Britain into its flagship defence-industrial financing scheme. The Chancellor raised the UK's case for access to the Security Action for Europe fund — SAFE, the EU's €150bn loan facility for joint military procurement — on the sidelines of the informal ECOFIN meeting hosted by Ireland's Tánaiste and finance minister, Simon Harris, as part of Dublin's EU Council presidency.

It is, on its face, a rerun. Rachel Reeves's Treasury negotiated over SAFE membership through much of 2025 and walked away in December, after the European Commission and France pushed for an entry fee of up to €6bn — a figure Yvette Cooper, then at the Foreign Office, said failed the government's own "value for money" test. Canada, by contrast, secured its SAFE association agreement for a reported €10m, a gap of more than 500-to-1 that UK officials called indefensible and MPs on the Commons foreign affairs committee queried directly with ministers.

Same fund, different pitch

What has changed since December is less the fund's terms than the Treasury occupant making the case. Healey took over from Reeves in Andy Burnham's first reshuffle on 20 July, arriving from the Ministry of Defence with a working relationship with EU counterparts built over eighteen months of Ukraine-related procurement talks. His pitch in Dublin leans on geopolitics rather than accountancy: a Europe rearming against Russia, an unpredictable US security guarantee under Trump, and a UK defence industry that argues exclusion from SAFE-funded joint procurement locks British suppliers out of contracts EU rivals can bid for freely.

The financial argument runs through the City. SAFE loans are raised by the Commission on capital markets against the EU's balance sheet and then lent on to member states — a pipeline of debt issuance, structuring and advisory work that UK banks want a share of, much as they lobbied for a foothold in the EU's post-2020 pandemic recovery bonds. Treasury officials frame renewed SAFE access as a way of keeping London-based underwriting inside the tent rather than watching Frankfurt and Paris capture the mandates.

A crowded field of defence banks

Complicating the pitch is that Healey is simultaneously exploring alternatives to SAFE rather than relying on it alone. The UK has been building a Multilateral Defence Mechanism with the Netherlands, Finland and Poland — sovereign-governed, unlike SAFE's Commission-run allocation — and is weighing membership of the Canada-led Defence, Security and Resilience Bank, backed by JPMorgan, RBC, Commerzbank, ING and LBBW. Reeves rejected the DSRB idea when it was first floated; Healey has reopened that file too, according to people briefed on the talks. Reconciling overlapping memberships across three defence-financing tracks — one EU-run, one UK-led, one transatlantic — is now as much a diplomatic exercise as a financial one.

Dublin's talks continue into Saturday, with a press conference expected to close the informal session, though no SAFE decision is likely this week — Brussels typically takes such requests through full Council process. The clearer marker to watch is the spring UK spending review, where Healey has promised to lay out, for the first time, a costed path to the NATO target of 3.5% of GDP on defence by 2035. Whatever SAFE access looks like by then will shape how much of that spending flows through EU-run channels rather than British or transatlantic ones — and how much of the underwriting fee income lands in London.
