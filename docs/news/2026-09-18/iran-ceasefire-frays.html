Iran war ceasefire still fraying past the 200-day mark

In one sentence
A Pakistan-brokered Iran ceasefire is fraying past 200 days, with fresh strikes in September and Israel's Lebanon buffer zone still holding dozens of villages and tens of thousands displaced.

Two hundred and one days after Israeli and American jets opened the war on Iran, the ceasefire that ended the shooting is still standing — barely, and mostly on paper. The latest reminder came on September 2, when Iran's Revolutionary Guard Corps fired missiles and drones at American positions in Jordan, Bahrain and Iraq, one of the heaviest single barrages since the truce took hold. Washington called it a ceasefire violation. Tehran called it retaliation for earlier US strikes. Both statements can be true, which is the problem.

In one sentence: A Pakistan-brokered ceasefire is seven months old and still fraying, with disputed strikes continuing and Israel's Lebanon buffer zone leaving tens of thousands displaced and dozens of villages under occupation.

A truce built in Islamabad, tested everywhere else

The war began on February 28 with joint US-Israeli strikes on Iranian nuclear and military sites. Pakistan, which shares a 900-kilometre border with Iran and had every incentive to see the fighting stop, brokered a two-week ceasefire on April 8; Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif announced it personally, saying the truce covered "everywhere, including Lebanon." President Trump extended it indefinitely on April 21. Two months later, Sharif and Field Marshal Asim Munir hosted US and Iranian delegations at Islamabad's Serena Hotel, producing the Islamabad Memorandum: Iran would reopen the Strait of Hormuz and abandon any pursuit of nuclear weapons; Washington would lift its naval blockade, grant sanctions waivers and unfreeze Iranian assets.

None of that fully happened. The memorandum collapsed in July over control of the Strait, which remains effectively shut to Western-flagged shipping — Iran now runs its own transit regime, charging vessels as much as $2 million a passage in yuan or crypto. Munir flew to Tehran on August 24 to try again, seeking to "prevent further escalation" and revive talks on reopening Hormuz. A ministerial meeting between Iran and Gulf states, planned for Oman on September 14, was postponed at the last minute for lack of "conditions conducive to constructive dialogue." Casualty counts, though contested, run into the thousands: estimates of Iranian dead range from 1,200 to 3,600, alongside at least 13 US troops and 21 Israeli soldiers killed since the war began.

The buffer zone that won't shrink

The clearest sign the ceasefire never actually stopped the fighting sits in Lebanon. Israel's declared "security" or "forward defence" zone — the same one mapped in the IDF's own release, running 8 to 12 kilometres into Lebanese territory — displaced more than 1.2 million people, roughly a fifth of the country's population, when evacuation orders went out in March. Amnesty International has called the mass evacuation orders an unlawful transfer under international law. By September the registered displaced figure had eased to around 330,000, but aid officials describe the population movement as "highly fluid and often reversible": people go home, a strike or a new order sends them back out. More than 50 Lebanese villages remain under Israeli occupation, including the Ali al-Taher ridge, a position Israeli commanders consider strategically vital and have shown no willingness to give up. Lebanese casualties from the campaign now exceed 4,000 dead and 12,000 wounded.

That is the pattern across the whole conflict: a ceasefire that holds for the big set-piece strikes but never quite extends to the grinding, lower-intensity operations underneath it — the Lebanon occupation, the tanker seizures in the Gulf of Oman, the missile exchanges each side frames as self-defence. Pakistan's diplomacy has repeatedly bought pauses, not endings.

What's next

Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian said on September 1 that Tehran would "immediately reciprocate" if Washington honoured the defunct Islamabad terms, keeping the door open despite the September 2 strikes. Whether that door stays open likely hinges on Hormuz: a real reopening would ease the oil-supply anxiety that has already pushed crude past $100 a barrel, while another closure attempt would test the ceasefire past its breaking point. Munir's shuttle diplomacy continues; the rescheduled Gulf-Iran talks in Oman are the next concrete date to watch.
