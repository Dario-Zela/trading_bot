Watchdog says ministers lack credible plan as British Steel rescue costs soar

Blast furnaces at British Steel's Scunthorpe works, the plant at the centre of the government's rescue. — Gareth James / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
MPs say ministers still can't explain how British Steel becomes viable, even as the bailout bill heads toward £1.5bn by 2028.

Britain is now spending £1.3 million of public money every day keeping British Steel's furnaces lit, and Parliament's spending watchdog says ministers still cannot say when — or whether — that will stop.

The Public Accounts Committee (PAC) has found that the Department for Business and Trade has no credible plan to return British Steel to a sustainable footing, more than a year after the government took emergency control of the Scunthorpe plant to stop its Chinese owner, Jingye Group, shutting it down. By mid-June, £555m of taxpayer money had already gone into wages and raw materials. The PAC's own projection puts the total bill at up to £1.5bn by 2028 — a figure ministers have not disputed, but have also not budgeted for in any published form.

"British Steel is unable to wash its own face, and government is now in charge," said Clive Betts, the committee's deputy chair. "Having brought British Steel onto the taxpayers' books, it is now up to government to explain its plan." The committee's report is blunter still: officials could not give MPs "even indicative estimates" of the final cost or the timeline for reaching one, and taxpayers "remain exposed to significant and growing costs and uncertainty — it is not clear whether the money will ever be recovered."

No route back to private hands

The emergency intervention in April 2025 was framed as a rescue, not a takeover — but British Steel was formally nationalised on 16 July 2026, some 15 months after ministers first floated the idea. Jingye, for its part, argues it is owed roughly £1bn in compensation and has begun proceedings to recover it; Beijing has called the nationalisation "strongly dissatisfying." The government has since gone further, nationalising Speciality Steel UK and its 1,300 jobs in mid-September — extending the same open-ended exposure to a second site just as the PAC was publishing its verdict on the first.

What the committee wants is unglamorous but specific: a published plan setting out the intended production model, a decarbonisation pathway for the plant's ageing blast furnaces, a route to financial sustainability, and a costed timetable showing where the money comes from and when it stops. None of that exists yet in public form.

For a government already fielding pressure over gilt yields sitting near three-decade highs and a fractious Bank of England vote, an open-ended industrial commitment with no defined exit is not a reassuring signal to bond markets watching how the Treasury handles contingent liabilities. The next marker is the government's formal response to the PAC report, due within two months under standard select committee procedure — and whether it contains a number ministers are willing to be held to.
