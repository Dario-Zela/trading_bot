Huntington Bancshares shares tumble on cut NII and 2027 EPS outlook

Huntington Center in Columbus, Ohio, headquarters of Huntington Bancshares. — Wikimedia Commons / Ɱ, CC BY-SA 4.0
In one sentence
Huntington Bancshares fell 5.6% after cutting its 2026 net interest income and 2027 EPS guidance, citing deposit-cost and loan-pricing pressure from higher-for-longer rates.

Huntington Bancshares shares fell 5.6% on Wednesday, closing at $15.82 after the Columbus, Ohio-based lender told investors at the Barclays Global Financial Services Conference that its earnings trajectory through 2027 will be flatter than promised. Roughly 77.5 million shares changed hands, well above normal volume, as the market repriced a stock that had leaned heavily on a bullish post-merger growth story.

Management cut its 2027 earnings-per-share target to a range of $1.75 to $1.83, down from the $1.90 to $1.93 it had guided to in the spring. It also trimmed 2026 net interest income growth to roughly 35%, from a prior 39–43% range, and cut projected 2026 revenue growth to about 34% from 37%. Those percentages look enormous next to typical bank guidance because they are: Huntington closed its $8.3 billion acquisition of Cadence Bank in February and folded in Texas lender Veritex Holdings last October, so the underlying base is still absorbing two large deals. The cuts describe how much of that combined growth is now expected to show up in the numbers — and it's less than advertised.

Deposit costs outrunning the model

The culprit is pricing, not credit. Chairman and CEO Steve Steinour told the conference the environment "is materially different and more challenging than we had anticipated at the beginning of the year," adding: "We got caught with a different expectation in the interest-rate environment and the competitive dynamic." Short-term rates have stayed higher for longer than Huntington modeled, and deposit costs have followed: total deposit costs rose roughly 30 basis points between January and August, while the rate paid on interest-bearing deposits reached 2.29% in the second quarter, up 8 basis points sequentially. Net interest margin slipped 3 basis points on the quarter to 3.21%, even after Huntington's prime rate moved to 7.00% following the Federal Reserve's rate increase.

CFO Zach Wasserman pointed to a second front: loan competition. He cited "meaningful acceleration in terms of the competitive environment in indirect auto" that pushed Huntington to pull back originations — auto balances fell 4.3% in the first half of the year — while commercial real estate loans shrank $800 million in the second quarter as borrowers refinanced away faster than expected. Steinour was explicit that the response won't be to chase volume: "What we won't do is change our credit discipline, and we're going to be very disciplined about our risk-adjusted returns." Management still points to a 2027 return on equity above 17%.

Some analysts read the reset as overdue rather than alarming — Keefe, Bruyette & Woods described it as a potential "clearing event" that removes an overhang investors had already priced in after BofA downgraded the stock earlier this year on similar concerns. Huntington's shares now trade around 8.8 times the midpoint of its new 2027 EPS estimate, a discount that will either look cheap or prove sticky depending on what regional-bank peers say about deposit competition when they report third-quarter results in mid-October — the next real test of whether this is a Huntington problem or a sector one.
