Washington presses Mexico to cut Chinese content from North American vehicles

An auto assembly line: North American content rules determine which components count toward tariff-free treatment under the USMCA. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The U.S. is using USMCA talks with Mexico to push a 50% American-content floor for vehicles and close the Chinese-parts backdoor into North America.

Washington is negotiating to rewrite the rules governing what goes into a North American vehicle — and the central target is Chinese-made components flowing through Mexican factories into U.S. showrooms. The first formal bilateral round of the USMCA joint review concluded in Mexico City on May 29; a second round is scheduled for Washington on June 16-17, and a third for Mexico City the week of July 20. The July 1, 2026 treaty deadline — mandated under Article 34.7 of the agreement — will pass without a concluded deal. U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer has said plainly that he will not "rubber-stamp an extension" and that the administration "probably will not resolve all the issues by July 1."

What happens after July 1 depends on whether all three parties — the U.S., Mexico, and Canada — agree to extend the pact, which would add 16 years to its life. If any party declines, mandatory annual reviews begin, running for a decade before expiration. Canada, largely sidelined from the bilateral U.S.-Mexico track, has its own complications: Prime Minister Mark Carney's recent agreement admitting 49,000 Chinese-built electric vehicles at reduced tariffs drew a sharp reaction in Washington. For now, Mexico is the priority partner, and the agenda is explicit.

Rules that remake the supply chain

The core U.S. demand is a higher U.S.-specific content threshold for vehicles built in Mexico. The current USMCA requires that 75% of a vehicle's value originate within North America, with at least 40% of passenger car content (45% for pickup trucks) coming from higher-wage facilities — primarily in the U.S. or Canada — covering core parts like engines, transmissions, and body panels. Washington is now pushing for a 50% minimum for U.S.-specific content alone, a condition the Trump administration also sought, unsuccessfully, during the original USMCA negotiations.

On steel, negotiators want a "melted and poured in North America" requirement for any steel receiving preferential tariff treatment — a provision aimed at blocking Chinese steel processed in Mexico from qualifying for USMCA benefits. Steel industry executive Barry Zekelman, who has been briefed on the talks, described the approach as a systematic effort to "close all of the loopholes that still exist."

Greer has framed the objective broadly: "Over the course of these negotiations, we are going to be talking about rules of origin in a way that enhances U.S. content in these goods." The U.S. also wants to expand the definition of "core parts" to include major electronics modules — a category where Asian supply chains dominate — and is seeking Mexico to align its tariffs on non-USMCA steel with U.S. rates.

The talks are proceeding without congressional trade promotion authority, which means any substantive changes to the USMCA's legal text would require a congressional vote. The practical consequence: officials on both sides expect outcomes to take the form of side letters or protocols rather than formal treaty amendments.

The China problem inside North American cars

The animating concern behind the content push is straightforward: more than 30 Chinese auto-parts manufacturers now operate in Mexico, and the number is rising. In 2024, Chinese-origin vehicle body parts and accessories imported into Mexico were valued at $899 million, representing 13.9% of Mexico's total imports in that category. Major Chinese EV makers — BYD, Geely, Chery, and Great Wall Motor — have been evaluating or bidding for manufacturing capacity in Mexico, seeing the country as a potential springboard into the U.S. market.

U.S. officials and trade analysts describe Mexico as a potential "backdoor" through which Chinese components, or vehicles assembled with Chinese supply chains, can enter North America duty-free under USMCA rules that were written before Chinese automotive investment in Mexico became significant. Research from Brookings and the Rice Baker Institute has found evidence that Chinese products have been circumventing U.S. tariffs via third-country manufacturing — a practice the revised content rules are designed to close off.

Mexico has begun moving in Washington's direction. On January 1, 2026, Mexico imposed a 50% tariff on auto companies without operating plants in Mexico and levied new duties on approximately 1,400 Chinese-origin products. USMCA utilization among Mexican exporters jumped from 44.8% in January 2025 to 88.7% by November 2025, signaling that manufacturers are scrambling to qualify under existing rules — and will need to re-qualify if the thresholds tighten.

What the market is reading into this

Equity markets registered the trade pressure on publication of the FT's reporting Thursday. Ford fell 3.94%, Stellantis 3.28%, and General Motors 1.27%; the iShares MSCI Mexico ETF dropped 2.77%, and the iShares China Large-Cap ETF shed 1.80%. The moves reflect two distinct risks: for the automakers, compliance costs and supply-chain restructuring if U.S.-content thresholds are raised; for Mexico-linked plays, the possibility that tighter origin rules raise effective costs for manufacturers assembled south of the border.

The deeper implication is what this negotiation signals about other sectors. Auto supply chains are the highest-profile test case for Washington's broader reshoring agenda, but the logic — raise North American content thresholds, restrict Chinese-affiliated inputs, align partner tariffs — is transferable to electronics, batteries, critical minerals, and steel. CSIS analysts have described the review as a potential foundation for "Fortress North America," a systematic reduction of the continent's supply-chain dependence on China. The U.S. International Trade Commission launched its own full review of USMCA automotive rules in February 2026, with a final report due July 2027 — ensuring the pressure on manufacturers persists well beyond any near-term deal.

The next concrete data point is the Washington round on June 16-17. Neither side expects a breakthrough — but movement on the steel and electronics provisions would signal how far Mexico is willing to go before the July 1 clock runs out.
