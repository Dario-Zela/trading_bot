White House bars national security AI from censorship and surveillance as Trump plans industry summit

The White House, where Trump signed NSPM-11 restricting AI use in the national security enterprise — Cezary Piwowarczyk / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Trump's NSPM-11 bans AI censorship and surveillance across 13+ national security agencies—but lacks independent enforcement just months after the Pentagon blacklisted Anthropic for drawing the same lines.

On Thursday, the White House signed a presidential memorandum that formally prohibits the national security enterprise from using artificial intelligence to censor speech, embed ideological bias, or conduct surveillance outside constitutional limits. The document — National Security Presidential Memorandum 11, or NSPM-11 — is the most direct statement yet from the Trump administration on what government-linked AI systems are and are not permitted to do.

The timing is not coincidental. Three months ago, the Pentagon blacklisted one of the country's largest AI companies for drawing exactly those lines.

What NSPM-11 Actually Says

The memorandum lays out four pillars for how the national security enterprise — a term that covers at least thirteen departments and agencies, from the CIA and FBI to the Departments of State, Energy, and Homeland Security — is meant to handle AI. The administration calls them Adoption, Adaptation, Assurance, and Accountability.

The accountability language is the sharpest. Section 2(d) states that "American AI technologies shall neither be developed nor used by the national security enterprise to censor free speech, embed ideological bias, or conduct unauthorized or unlawful surveillance activities." Civil liberties and constitutional protections, the document says, are "non-negotiable."

Enforcement is a different question. The primary mechanism is a chain of command: commanders, directors, and agency heads are made personally accountable for compliance at every level. There is no independent watchdog or inspector-general mechanism named in the text. Section 3(b) does give agencies the authority to terminate contracts with vendors that have "repeatedly demonstrated a pattern of conduct inconsistent with" the stated policies, but that language is permissive — it allows such terminations rather than mandating them.

The document replaces the Biden administration's NSM-25, which Trump officials describe as having burdened AI adoption with "ideological mandates" and fostered "dangerous single-vendor dependencies." Key implementation deadlines fall at 90 days — when the Department of War must update its directive on autonomous weapons — and 120 days, when procurement reviews and a new computing roadmap are due.

The Anthropic Backdrop

There is an awkward precedent sitting behind all this declared principle. On February 27, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth designated Anthropic a supply chain risk to national security. President Trump followed the same day, directing every federal agency to cease using Anthropic's technology. The company was penalized, according to court filings and reporting, because it refused to let the Pentagon use its Claude models without restrictions — specifically, Anthropic had drawn two redlines: no use in autonomous lethal weapons systems, and no use in mass surveillance of American citizens.

In other words, Anthropic was blacklisted for trying to enforce something very close to what NSPM-11 now instructs the government itself to observe.

A California federal judge later blocked the Pentagon's designation as unconstitutional. The Ninth Circuit declined to reinstate the blacklist pending appeal, leaving a split outcome: Anthropic can work with civilian agencies but remains locked out of Defense Department contracts while litigation continues. A spokesperson for Anthropic did not respond to requests for comment on the new memorandum.

The Anthropic case illustrates the gap between stated policy and actual enforcement. The administration's AI procurement decisions have operated largely through executive and military authority — quick, difficult to challenge — while the new memorandum relies on diffuse accountability structures rather than any external check. Absent a dedicated oversight body, whether NSPM-11's censorship and surveillance restrictions will constrain procurement choices in practice remains an open question.

A Summit, and Something Bigger

Also on Thursday, Trump told reporters that he planned to host the leading AI companies at the White House "maybe next week," saying "all of them" would have representatives present. He named no specific companies or agenda items.

The meeting arrives against a broader policy push. On June 2, Trump signed a separate executive order inviting major AI developers to voluntarily submit frontier models to the government for up to 30 days of security testing before public release — a measure the administration describes as a national-security screening tool. Critics note the word "voluntarily" does most of the work; there is no compulsion mechanism.

A more significant idea is now in circulation. According to reporting by The Washington Post and Notus, Trump and his advisers are discussing the possibility of the federal government acquiring equity stakes in major AI companies, framed as a way to give ordinary Americans a share of the industry's profits. OpenAI's Sam Altman has reportedly discussed voluntarily ceding shares to the government. Anthropic, by contrast, is described as not engaging in those conversations — hardly surprising given the ongoing litigation.

The equity-stake concept has no legislative basis yet and faces obvious governance complexities. But its emergence alongside NSPM-11 suggests the administration is thinking about AI in national security terms that go beyond procurement standards — as a strategic asset to be co-owned, not merely regulated.

The next concrete marker is the 90-day deadline for the Department of War's weapons-autonomy update, which falls in September. The courts will continue to shape the Anthropic dispute in parallel. How the AI industry summit goes — and whether the government equity idea moves from White House corridor talk into formal proposal — will do more than any declared principle to reveal what this administration actually intends.
