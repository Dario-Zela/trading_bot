Trump says he wants lower rates, may leave October decision to Warsh

Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh delivers remarks at his swearing-in ceremony in the East Room of the White House, May 22, 2026. — Daniel Torok / White House / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Trump said he'd like lower rates but will defer the October call to Fed Chair Kevin Warsh — an unusual act of patience that runs straight into hot jobs data and markets now pricing a hike.

President Donald Trump said Friday that he would like to see lower interest rates but will leave the rate decision to Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh when the Federal Open Market Committee meets in October — a notably hands-off formulation from a president who has spent much of his political career haranguing the central bank.

"Housing's all about interest rates," Trump said at a community college event. "They can pass all the bills they want, it's about interest rates. You get the interest rates down, everybody's going to be very, very happy." Asked separately on Fox Business whether he still expects rates to fall, he answered: "When Kevin gets in, I do... interest rates should be much lower."

The pivot to deference — 'I'll leave it to Warsh' — is the unusual part. Trump has long preferred the direct approach, publicly pressuring Jerome Powell for cuts and, after years of frustration, replacing him. Installing Warsh was itself meant to signal a dovish shift. Saying he will now stand aside and let the new chair handle October reads less as restraint and more as expectation management: Trump is telling the housing market, the bond market, and his own political base that relief is calendared, if not guaranteed.

The Math Is Not Cooperating

The problem is the economic data, which has turned sharply in the wrong direction for rate-cut advocates. The May payrolls report released Friday showed 172,000 jobs added — more than double the consensus forecast of roughly 85,000 — while March and April were revised up by a combined 93,000. The three-month average now sits at 188,000. Unemployment held at 4.3%. Consumer prices are running at 3.8% year-on-year, the hottest reading since May 2023 and nearly double the Fed's 2% target.

Markets processed this quickly. CME FedWatch data now puts the odds of at least one rate hike in 2026 above 57%, with October specifically pricing better than 60% for tightening. The 2-year Treasury yield — the most sensitive instrument to near-term rate expectations — jumped to 4.15%, while 20- and 30-year bonds crept above 5%.

Warsh, who was sworn in at the White House on May 22 as the first Fed chair to take the oath there since Alan Greenspan in 1987, has been deliberate about keeping distance from the White House's preferred outcome. He testified during his confirmation hearing that Trump had not asked him to "predetermine, fix or decide on any interest rate decision." He holds one of 12 FOMC votes. Jerome Powell, whom Trump sought for years to remove, remains on the board as a sitting governor through 2028 and retains the ability to influence committee deliberations.

The October 27–28 FOMC meeting carries an additional layer of complexity: it falls less than a week before the November 3, 2026 midterm elections, at which all 435 House seats and 35 Senate seats are up. The Fed's informal tradition of avoiding rate moves that could be read as politically timed will make any October cut — or hike — a harder lift to narrate as purely technical.

For now, the specific catalyst to watch is Warsh's first meeting as chair on June 16–17, when the FOMC will release updated economic projections alongside its rate decision. Markets are pricing no action there. What the 'dot plot' signals about the second half of the year will set the frame for whether October is even in play.
