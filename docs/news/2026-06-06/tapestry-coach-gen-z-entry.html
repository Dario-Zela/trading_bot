Tapestry's post-earnings dip offers an entry into Coach's Gen Z franchise

A Coach retail store at Barton Creek Mall, Austin, Texas. The brand's $300–$700 price tier has made it a dominant first-luxury-purchase for Gen Z consumers. — Kurt Kaiser / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
Tapestry fell ~10% despite a Q3 earnings beat on 31% Coach growth—a valuation reset after a 100%+ run, not a brand stumble, with analysts pricing 17%+ upside.

Tapestry reported the kind of earnings quarter that usually rewards shareholders: $1.92 billion in revenue, up 23% year-over-year, a 62% surge in earnings per share to $1.66, and full-year guidance lifted to roughly $7.95 billion. The company's flagship Coach brand added 2 million new customers in a single quarter, 800,000 of them from Gen Z. The stock fell roughly 10% anyway.

That gap between operational momentum and market reaction is the signal worth examining.

The Goldilocks brand

Coach has spent several years engineering what its chief executive Todd Kahn calls "expressive luxury" — the Goldilocks position between LVMH-tier aspiration and mass accessibility. Most of its handbags are priced between $300 and $700, reachable for a young professional buying a first luxury piece but still carrying the cachet of a heritage New York label founded in 1941. The strategy is working with a demographic that other affordable-luxury names have struggled to capture. North American Coach revenues rose 27% in the fiscal third quarter; Greater China surged 58%; Europe added 27%. Core leather goods volumes grew over 20%, and footwear — driven mainly by sneakers — expanded roughly 20% as well. Coach brand revenue crossed $1.7 billion for the quarter, up 31%, representing approximately two-thirds of Tapestry's group sales.

Kahn's assessment of the addressable opportunity is expansive to the point of near-evangelical: "We can literally add millions of new customers every quarter for the next 10 years, and we'll just scratch the surface." CEO Joanne Crevoiserat frames the target simply: Coach becoming "a $10 billion brand over time, with best-in-class margins."

Against that backdrop, the selloff requires an explanation that goes beyond the brand.

The stock entered results day having already doubled from its 52-week low of $75.48, touching an all-time high of $161.97 in late February. A trailing P/E north of 55 meant perfection was priced in, and perfection is a high bar. What the quarter delivered was close — but not uncomplicated. Kate Spade, the group's second brand, posted a 10% revenue decline to around $220 million and absorbed 180 basis points of tariff-related margin pressure. The company framed the Kate Spade contraction as a deliberate promotional pullback and repositioning effort. The market, reading two-thirds growth and one-third drag, settled on a "sell the news" verdict after an enormous run. A February disclosure that Crevoiserat sold $7.6 million in stock added a note of caution that lingered.

The post-earnings price around $140 is where analysts see value. Fifteen of 21 covering the stock rate it a buy, with a mean 12-month price target of $163.63 — approximately 17% above current levels. Evercore ISI trimmed its target to $175 on May 8 while maintaining an Outperform rating, implying 25% upside from the post-selloff print. GlobalData managing director Neil Saunders notes that Tapestry has effectively become a Coach pure-play: fixing Kate Spade matters, but it no longer makes or breaks the investment thesis.

The case against buying the dip is essentially a valuation argument: even post-correction, Tapestry is not cheap relative to traditional luxury retail multiples, and any further Kate Spade deterioration would test margin guidance. The case for it is that 31% brand revenue growth and accelerating Gen Z penetration at a $300–$700 price point represents a structural, multi-year story — one that a single quarter's profit-taking doesn't invalidate.

The next hard data point is Tapestry's fiscal fourth-quarter report, due in August. Kate Spade's trajectory in that print — whether the promotional discipline produces a revenue stabilization or a continued slide — will determine whether the current entry point looks obvious in hindsight or merely early.
