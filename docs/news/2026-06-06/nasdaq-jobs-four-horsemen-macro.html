MACRO RISK · By M. Halloway · 2026-06-06 · 6 min read

Jobs surprise hands Nasdaq its worst day since the tariff shock as four market risks converge

The Marriner S. Eccles Building in Washington, D.C. — Federal Reserve headquarters — as rate-hike bets swing sharply upward — Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA 3.0
In one sentence
May payrolls of 172k — double consensus — sent rate-hike bets to 96%, tipped the Nasdaq 100 to its worst day since the tariff shock, and pushed bitcoin below $60,000.

For most of the spring, the story on Wall Street was simple: AI chips were infallible, the Federal Reserve was finished tightening, and a nine-week winning streak for the S&P 500 suggested investors had decided to move on from last year's tariff turbulence. Friday's May jobs report demolished all three assumptions before the opening bell.

In brief: Strong May payrolls doubled Wall Street's consensus estimate, sent rate-hike bets surging, and triggered the Nasdaq 100's worst single session since the April 2025 Liberation Day shock — dragging semiconductors, AI proxies, and bitcoin down with it.

The number that flipped the script

U.S. employers added 172,000 jobs in May, roughly double the ~85,000 consensus forecast. Prior months were revised upward by a combined 93,000. The unemployment rate held at 4.3%. On its own the report might have been read as a sign of resilience; layered on top of an April CPI print of 3.8% year-over-year — the hottest reading since May 2023 — it was enough to shift the entire rates narrative.

The bond market responded immediately. The 10-year Treasury yield jumped to 4.54%; the 30-year crossed 5%. Money markets, which had spent weeks pricing in a prolonged Fed pause or even cuts, swung sharply in the other direction: by Friday afternoon, futures implied a 96% probability of at least one rate hike before year-end. That is a material repricing from where consensus sat just a week earlier.

Hotter-than-expected hiring alongside stubbornly elevated inflation removes the Fed's principal reason to stand still. The question is no longer whether it cuts — it is whether it hikes.

The Nasdaq's worst session since Liberation Day

The rate shock detonated squarely in the corner of the market most exposed to it. The Nasdaq 100 fell 4.7%, its worst single-day loss since the April 2025 tariff shock that briefly erased trillions from global equities. The Nasdaq Composite dropped 4.18%, closing at 25,709. The S&P 500 shed 2.6%, snapping a nine-week winning streak — the longest such run since 2023. The Dow held up comparatively, losing 1.4%.

The damage concentrated in semiconductors. The VanEck Semiconductor ETF (SOXX) fell roughly 9% on Friday and is now down more than 10% over two sessions. Marvell Technology lost 16%; Micron declined 13%; AMD, Arm, Intel, and Qualcomm each shed more than 10%.

Broadcom (AVGO) illustrated the difficulty of beating numbers when the macro frame has shifted. The company reported earnings that exceeded expectations on Thursday and reiterated AI revenue guidance of "in excess of $100 billion" — a figure that would have been received as bullish news a month ago. Instead, shares collapsed 12.6% Thursday and gave up another 7% on Friday. When a company posts strong results and still falls double digits across two sessions, the problem is not the fundamentals; it is the discount rate applied to every future dollar of profit.

The broader growth-stock selloff reflects a straightforward valuation mechanic: higher long-term interest rates compress the present value of distant earnings. Technology and AI names carry more of their value in years five through twenty than, say, a regulated utility or an energy major. A 50-basis-point move in the 10-year yield is a modest number in fixed-income terms but a meaningful one in equity discounting arithmetic.

Crypto's worst week since FTX

Bitcoin fell below $60,000 on Friday for the first time since Donald Trump's election victory in late 2024 — a level the market had treated as psychological bedrock. The intraday low reached $59,100 before a partial recovery. The weekly decline stands at roughly 17-19%, the worst seven-day performance since the FTX collapse in November 2022.

Coinglass data showed $1.75 billion in liquidations in the 24-hour period, with 351,233 traders wiped out across crypto markets. Ether performed even worse, losing more than 17% on the week. Spot bitcoin ETFs recorded outflows exceeding $3.2 billion.

Strategy Inc. (formerly MicroStrategy), whose stock serves as a leveraged proxy for bitcoin, dropped roughly 25% on the week. Separately, CEO Michael Saylor sold 32 bitcoin for approximately $2.5 million — his first disclosed sale since December 2022 — a move that attracted attention given its timing.

Analysts at Presto Research attributed the crypto selloff partly to competition: as AI equities and gold both reassert themselves as inflation hedges and growth bets respectively, the narrative justification for crypto premium valuations weakens. The rate-hike repricing compounds the problem; risk assets that performed well under an easy-money thesis are the most exposed when the thesis reverses.

Four risks converging on a concentrated market

The week's events did not arrive in isolation. A separate analysis of S&P 500 structural vulnerabilities identified four compounding pressures that had been building before Friday's data point provided a spark.

First, inflation: the April CPI reading and a commodity backdrop with structural upward pressure mean the Fed's job is not finished, even if the market spent weeks pretending otherwise. Second, technology speculation: the VanEck Semiconductor ETF entered the week up approximately 60% year-to-date and trading 50% above its 200-day moving average — extended by any historical standard. Third, credit stress: private-credit funds have begun restricting redemptions, a signal that liquidity is tightening at the margin in corners of the financial system that do not show up in public spreads. Fourth, concentration: the top ten stocks in the S&P 500 now represent roughly 40% of the index's total market capitalisation — one of the highest readings in modern market history, meaning index-level volatility amplifies any rotation out of mega-cap names.

The parallel being drawn in some corners of the market is to late 1999, when investors dismissed Warren Buffett's preference for quality companies during the dot-com bubble. Berkshire Hathaway, Procter & Gamble, and PepsiCo are currently trading at multi-year relative lows versus the broader index — a defensive rotation waiting, in that framing, to happen.

The key question is whether Friday was a corrective shock that clears excess or the beginning of a more durable derating of growth assets. The answer depends almost entirely on what comes next in the data. June's CPI print, due in the second week of July, will be the first real test of whether April's 3.8% reading was a seasonal blip or a trend re-acceleration. The Federal Open Market Committee meets July 29-30 — the meeting at which a rate hike would first be live. Between now and then, the market will have four weeks of data, a string of Fed speaker appearances, and whatever surprises the geopolitical calendar delivers. The earnings season and AI investment cycle are not over. But the era of pricing in cuts and ignoring inflation is.
