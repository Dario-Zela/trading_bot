Two lawmakers exit Berkshire as Abel settles into the CEO seat

Berkshire Hathaway's understated Omaha headquarters at Kiewit Plaza — unchanged under the new regime, even as the portfolio is not. — Wikipedia / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Two Congress members made routine BRK/B sales in early 2026; the real Berkshire story is Greg Abel's sweeping Q1 portfolio overhaul — 16 positions out, big bets on Alphabet and Delta in.

Congressional stock-trade filings drop every week — a dozen sales, a handful of buys, a disclosure lag of 30 to 45 days — and most carry roughly the same informational weight as a recycled bin. Two recent sales of Berkshire Hathaway Class B shares fall squarely in that category, whatever headline link is drawn to Warren Buffett's retirement.

Rep. Josh Gottheimer, Democrat of New Jersey, reported two sales of BRK/B on 4 May, each in the $1,000-to-$15,000 range — the lowest mandatory disclosure band. The trades are barely a rounding error for Gottheimer, one of Congress's most prolific stock traders: more than 3,500 transactions on the books, representing north of $340 million in total value. He bought Berkshire shares twice in 2024 and sold them twice in 2025. Nothing in the rhythm suggests a verdict on the new management.

Rep. Christian Menefee, freshman Democrat of Texas, sold three tranches of BRK/B on 10 February totalling between $31,000 and $115,000. These were his first disclosures since entering Congress in January — meaning the stock was already in hand before he cast a single vote. He sold; he hasn't bought back. But the absence of a follow-on purchase tells us very little when the opening position arrived as pre-existing inventory, not a conviction trade.

Two lawmakers, no shared timing, no coordination, different transaction sizes. The pattern is congressional routine, not a post-Buffett referendum.

Abel's Q1 was the real story

While the disclosure filings generated heat, the more substantive Berkshire news lay in the company's 13F filing for Q1 2026 — the first full quarter with Greg Abel in the chief executive's chair. Abel was decisive. Berkshire exited 16 positions outright: the entirety of its Visa and Mastercard stakes, its holdings in UnitedHealth Group, Amazon, Domino's Pizza, and Charter Communications, among others. The conglomerate spent roughly $16 billion acquiring stocks while selling around $24 billion — a net-seller posture that trimmed the equity portfolio from 42 holdings to 29.

The buys carried a different disposition from Buffett's late-career caution. Berkshire more than doubled its Alphabet stake, lifting its Class A position to 54.2 million shares valued at roughly $23 billion — vaulting it to the seventh-largest holding. A new position in Delta Air Lines arrived at 39.8 million shares, about $2.8 billion. The company nearly tripled its stake in The New York Times Company and added to its positions in homebuilder Lennar.

All this played out against a backdrop of mild underperformance: BRK shares are down about 1.3% in 2026, while the SPDR S&P 500 ETF has gained 8.3% over the same stretch. Some of that gap may reflect genuine investor uncertainty about the post-Buffett era; some is likely mean reversion after decades of outperformance. Neither congressional seller will help settle that question.

The next meaningful read on Abel's evolving approach comes with the Q2 13F filing, due in mid-August. That will show whether Q1's concentrated reshuffling — tech-heavy, airline-curious, media-friendly — was a one-off clearing exercise or the opening chapter of a substantially different investment philosophy.
