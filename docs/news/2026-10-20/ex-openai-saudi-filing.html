Ex-OpenAI worker accused of planting device had Saudi support, court filing says

In one sentence
US prosecutors say a former OpenAI engineer charged over a hidden recording device appears to have had Saudi backing; the claim is an allegation in a detention filing.

US prosecutors say a former OpenAI engineer accused of hiding a recording device in the company's offices appears to have had backing from the Saudi government. The claim is in a court filing. It is an allegation, and nothing in it has been tested at trial.

The defendant is Abdulrahman Alfozan, 35, a Saudi national. He was due in federal court in San Francisco on 19 October. Prosecutors filed a detention request that day, arguing he is a flight risk and should be held in US custody until trial.

What prosecutors allege

A criminal complaint says Alfozan installed a "surreptitious audio recording device into a USB port on the back of a television in an executive OpenAI conference room" on 28 September. It says the device stayed there for "at least multiple days". He is charged with using an electronic device to intercept oral communications.

The detention filing goes further. Prosecutors say investigators found evidence that he may have planned to place more devices in OpenAI offices. They also say he used AI tools to help develop encrypted recording devices and to identify meeting rooms.

The Saudi link

The state-backing claim rests on two specific assertions. Prosecutors say Alfozan was in frequent contact with an unnamed member of the Saudi royal family. They also say a member of the Saudi military paid for a one-way plane ticket for his wife and son to return to Saudi Arabia.

The filing says he "appears" to have Saudi backing. That is cautious wording, and it fits a document written to argue he might flee. Prosecutors have not, in what has been reported, charged anyone with acting as a foreign agent. They have not named the royal family member or the military official. The reporting also doesn't say whether Saudi authorities have responded. I did not find a defence response to the detention request.

Why it matters

The case is unusual because the alleged target was a conference room at a leading AI lab, not a data centre or a code repository. Executive meetings at such companies cover model plans, funding talks and security policy. An audio bug picks up all of it.

The case also touches a worry the industry has voiced for years: that insiders are the easiest route into a well-defended company. There is a precedent. Two former Twitter employees were prosecuted over accessing user data for Saudi Arabia, and one was convicted, though prosecutors dropped the case against another pair of ex-Twitter workers in 2020.

What to watch

The immediate question is whether the judge grants detention. After that, the next test is whether prosecutors add charges that reflect the foreign-state claims, or whether the Saudi link stays in the filing as context for a flight-risk argument. OpenAI's own account of what was said in that room, and whether it was sensitive, has yet to emerge in the reporting reviewed here.
