North Sea strike threat could severely disrupt UK fuel supplies, union warns

In one sentence
Unite says Apache North Sea workers back strikes that could threaten the Forties pipeline, but no dates are confirmed and the price impact is so far only inference.

A union has warned that industrial action in the North Sea could disrupt UK fuel supplies. On the evidence so far, the warning is a bargaining move and not a stoppage. The scale of the risk depends on whether talks fail and on how far a walkout at one platform would spread through shared infrastructure.

The Guardian's report, as summarised in the headline, names no union, employer, dates or workforce. Other coverage fills some of that in, but the picture is incomplete. The material below comes from a Reuters report carried by Baird Maritime on 5 October, plus search summaries of other outlets. The Guardian piece and the Scotsman's could not be read directly.

What the union says

Unite says more than 160 offshore workers at seven North Sea assets operated by Apache have "overwhelmingly backed" industrial action. The reported vote counts and turnout are not given. The workers rejected a pay offer that Unite called "a real-terms pay cut". The Reuters report said a strike could come in October. It gave no specific dates, though a related headline mentions a planned 24-hour stoppage. That detail is unconfirmed.

The supply argument rests on geography. Unite says disruption at Apache's Charlie platform could bring it to a standstill and potentially shut the Forties pipeline system. Ineos operates the system, which carries crude from dozens of fields to Scotland. Unite puts its share at about 29% of UK oil production and 30% of UK gas. It also exports Forties Blend, one of the crudes that underpin the Brent benchmark.

Those shares are the union's figures, and they were not checked against official production data. Ineos declined to speculate on the effect of any action. One headline suggests Apache has played down the pipeline risk, but its statement was not available. A union has an interest in stating the stakes at their widest, and a 24-hour action by a small workforce is a different order of event from a prolonged shutdown.

A separate dispute

A second dispute is running alongside. Unite and the RMT are in conflict with Wood Group over proposed changes to pay and conditions, including possible fire-and-rehire notices. Technicians face six strike days in October and November. The reported dates are 20 and 21 October and 3 and 4 November, on a Wood contract at Harbour Energy platforms. Wood has said it received notice of action on one Harbour contract. This is a different employer and a different set of assets from the Apache dispute, and the two should not be treated as one.

What to watch

The link from strike to pump or wholesale prices is inference at this stage. No supply volumes, price moves or government response have been reported. A market reaction would show first in Brent and Forties differentials and in UK wholesale gas, ahead of any retail effect. Three things will settle the question:

  • whether Apache and Unite agree a revised offer before any dates are formally served;
  • whether Ineos or the operators say how much output a one-platform stoppage would actually put at risk;
  • whether the walkouts at Harbour platforms on 20 and 21 October pass without a measurable dent in output.

Until dates and scale are confirmed, the story is a union lever and a pipeline-dependency argument, with no stoppage yet.
