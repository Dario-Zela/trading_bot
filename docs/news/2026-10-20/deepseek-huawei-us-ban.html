DeepSeek reportedly works with Huawei on chip design as the US bars it from government systems

In one sentence
The Information reports DeepSeek is deepening chip-design ties with Huawei to cut reliance on US silicon; the report is single-sourced and unconfirmed.

A leading Chinese AI lab is reportedly tying its hardware plans to a Chinese chipmaker. The Information says DeepSeek is working more closely with Huawei on the design of future AI chips. The stated aim is to loosen China's dependence on US silicon. Neither company has confirmed it. Benzinga's account relays The Information's reporting, so the claim rests on one outlet's sourcing, and the public detail is thin.

What is reported

The reporting says DeepSeek and Huawei are deepening their ties on chip design, with the goal of reducing reliance on US chips. The public versions do not say what is being designed, how many engineers are involved, or when any chip might ship. They do not say whether this is a formal co-development deal or the next step in an existing collaboration. Those gaps matter. "Co-design" can mean a model lab and a chipmaker sharing performance data, or it can mean a lab shaping a processor's architecture. The first is routine. The second would be a bigger step.

The report fits a pattern. In April 2026, The Information reported that DeepSeek's V4 model would run on Huawei's latest chips. It said DeepSeek had spent months with Huawei and Cambricon rewriting and testing parts of V4's core code. It also said Alibaba, ByteDance and Tencent had placed bulk orders for Huawei's upcoming chip, reportedly in the hundreds of thousands of units. Those claims were also relayed secondhand, and the companies did not confirm them at the time. A Substack analysis citing SemiAnalysis went further and described V4 as partly co-designed for Huawei's Ascend inference chips. That is a secondary account of a paywalled report, and it should be treated as less certain.

So the new report is better read as the next step in an existing relationship than as a sudden pivot. The direction is consistent with earlier reporting, but each piece of it rests on the same kind of sourcing.

Why the hardware matters

Most frontier AI work depends on Nvidia's chips and the software built around them. US export controls limit which of those chips can be sold to China. A Chinese model lab that designs its models around Huawei hardware is effectively building a second supply chain. If the effort works, the controls lose some of their force, because the chips they restrict become less essential.

The obstacles are real. Huawei's chips are generally regarded as behind Nvidia's on performance per chip. Chinese chipmakers also face limits on advanced manufacturing capacity. Software is a further hurdle. Developers have spent years tuning their code for Nvidia's tools, and moving to another platform costs time. That is why the reported work on rewriting core code matters. A lab that adapts its models to Huawei hardware, and perhaps shapes the hardware in return, narrows that gap from both sides.

For Nvidia, the risk is to its China business. Chinese customers that commit to domestic chips are customers it is unlikely to win back. How large that effect is depends on how many labs follow DeepSeek, and the reporting does not establish that.

Washington's patchwork

The report arrives amid a long run of US restrictions on DeepSeek itself. These are a patchwork that began in early 2025. The US Navy barred personnel from using DeepSeek in late January 2025, citing security and ethical concerns. NASA and the Pentagon also restricted it around the same period. Texas Governor Greg Abbott banned it on state-issued devices and networks in February 2025.

In Congress, the bipartisan No DeepSeek on Government Devices Act (H.R. 1121) was introduced on February 6, 2025 by Reps. Josh Gottheimer and Darin LaHood. It would direct the Office of Management and Budget to set standards for removing the DeepSeek app from federal agency IT. A Senate companion, S.765, was also introduced. I found no evidence that either bill has passed. That means any claim of a new, government-wide federal ban in October 2026 is not supported by what could be confirmed, and the earlier restrictions remain a mix of agency and state measures. The headline's reference to the US barring DeepSeek from government systems should be read in that light.

The two threads connect without being the same story. The bans address data security: whether an app tied to a Chinese company should run on government devices. The Huawei report concerns industrial capacity: whether China can train and run advanced models without American components. Both point to a widening separation between the US and Chinese AI ecosystems.

What to watch

The first test is confirmation. A statement from DeepSeek or Huawei, or a second outlet with independent sourcing, would move this from a report to a fact. The second is evidence in the product itself: whether DeepSeek's next models list Huawei hardware as the primary platform, and how they perform there. The third is policy. Congress could act on H.R. 1121 and S.765, and the Commerce Department could revisit export rules if domestic chips look like a working substitute. Until one of those happens, the report is a signal about direction, not proof of a result.
