Putin proposes a Trump–Xi summit in China to discuss a Ukraine deal

In one sentence
The WSJ reports Putin has proposed a Trump–Xi–Putin summit in China on a Ukraine deal; it is an offer, with no confirmed date, venue or response from Washington or Beijing.

The Wall Street Journal has reported that Vladimir Putin has proposed a three-way summit with Donald Trump and Xi Jinping, to be held in China, with a settlement of the war in Ukraine as the agenda. As reported, it is an offer and not an agreement. The pieces of reporting available at the time of writing give no confirmed date, venue or programme. They also give no public acceptance from Washington or Beijing. The WSJ report was picked up by Benzinga, and the Journal remains the primary source.

What is on the table

The idea is simple to state. Moscow wants a deal on Ukraine, and it is suggesting that China host the talks and that both the American and Chinese presidents attend. For Putin the appeal is plain. A summit in China puts Russia's largest economic partner in the room and gives the Kremlin a venue it regards as friendly. It also moves the conversation away from European capitals, where Russia's position has little sympathy.

The proposal has precedents, though not identical ones. In February 2025 the Journal reported that China had floated a Putin–Trump summit as a way to end the war, a notion the White House did not embrace. In August 2025 Putin telephoned Xi, Narendra Modi and other leaders to brief them on his contacts with Washington. Trump, for his part, has said he would consider a meeting with Putin and Xi "when things calm down". None of these amounted to a format in which Beijing sat at the table as a co-broker. This one would, if it were taken up.

Why Beijing's role matters

China has cast itself as a neutral party on Ukraine while buying Russian energy and supplying goods that sustain Russian industry. It has not condemned the invasion. A summit on its soil would bring that ambiguity into the open and give Xi a part in settling Europe's largest war since 1945. It would also create a direct US–China channel on the subject, running alongside the trade and technology disputes that dominate relations between the two.

There are reasons for caution. Kyiv is not named in the reported proposal, and a deal on Ukraine reached without Ukraine's government would be hard to make stick. European governments, which carry much of the financial burden of supporting Kyiv, are likewise absent from the reported format. Washington has also been wrangling over its own role: a Zelensky adviser said this week that Ukrainians are dying because of US curbs on intelligence sharing, a sign of how contested American policy remains.

What markets would watch

The economic stakes are indirect but real. A credible route to a settlement would bear on three things:

  • Energy: expectations about sanctions on Russian oil and diesel feed into fuel prices. Russian diesel supply is already uncertain, and the Treasury Secretary has blamed September inflation on an "energy shock".
  • Grain: a settlement affecting Black Sea shipping would matter to wheat and corn.
  • Sanctions: any bargain would raise the question of what the West would lift, and in what order.

None of that follows from a proposal alone. Traders tend to price in announcements and discount them when nothing follows.

What to watch

The first test is whether the White House or Beijing responds in public, and in what terms. A Chinese foreign ministry statement would be the clearest early signal. After that come the practicalities: a date, a venue and whether Ukraine and European governments are offered a seat. Until a response arrives, the offer is best read as a diplomatic opening move whose weight depends on Trump's and Xi's replies.
