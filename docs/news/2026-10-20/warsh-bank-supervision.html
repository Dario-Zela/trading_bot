Warsh admits 'I have failed' on bank supervision in first 148 days

In one sentence
Fed chair Kevin Warsh is reported to have said he 'failed' to give bank supervision enough attention in his first 148 days, though the exact wording is unverified.

Kevin Warsh has told an international audience that he has fallen short on bank supervision since taking over the Federal Reserve. The remark came about five months into his chairmanship. Only a thin trail of reporting supports it so far, and the exact wording has not been checked against a transcript.

Benzinga's headline quotes him as saying "I have failed". Central Banking, a trade publication, carries a related report under the title "Warsh: I've focused too much on monetary policy". A search summary of that report places the remarks at the IMF and World Bank annual meetings in Bangkok on 16 October, in conversation with the IMF's managing director, Kristalina Georgieva. It has Warsh saying he had "failed" across his first 148 days because he had not given enough attention to supervision, regulation and payment systems. The Central Banking page itself could not be loaded for this article, so that account is second-hand. Neither the Fed nor the IMF has published a transcript that was available to check. The day count and the first-person phrasing should be treated as reported rather than established.

What he is said to have conceded

The Central Banking framing is more specific than the Benzinga headline. It suggests the self-criticism was about attention, not a particular lapse: a chair who spent his first months on interest rates and the balance sheet, and who now says he will turn to the other parts of the institution. Those parts are bank supervision, payments plumbing and consumer affairs. Whether he said "I have failed" or used a looser phrase such as "I've failed to" matters for how far the admission goes. As reported, it reads as a confession of priorities, not of wrongdoing.

Why it lands awkwardly

Supervision was already the sore point of his confirmation. Americans for Financial Reform urged the Senate Banking Committee to oppose his nomination, citing his past resistance to strong supervision and to Dodd-Frank-era rules. In April, Senator Elizabeth Warren sought Fed records on his role in the 2008 crisis response ahead of his hearing. Her office also raised concerns about efforts to scale back supervision, including smaller examiner staffing and softer ratings.

Against that record, a public statement that supervision has been neglected cuts two ways. Critics can read it as confirmation. Supporters of lighter regulation can read it as a prelude to a reorganised, not necessarily tougher, approach. The remarks as reported do not say which.

What to watch

The first test is the text. A Fed or IMF transcript, or a recording of the Bangkok session, would settle the wording and context. After that, the test is behaviour: any announcement on supervisory staffing, the ratings framework or the Fed's vice chair for supervision, and how Warsh answers questions on the subject at his next congressional appearance. Until then, the admission is better read as a signal of where he says his attention will go than as evidence of any change in policy.
