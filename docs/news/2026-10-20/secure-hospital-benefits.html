Criminals detained in hospital to lose benefits

In one sentence
The government plans to end Universal Credit for offenders held in secure hospitals, but the legal route, start date and number affected remain unstated.

The government intends to stop paying benefits to offenders who are held in secure hospitals rather than prisons. Prisoners already cannot claim most state benefits. Ministers argue that the same logic should cover people detained by a court after a serious offence, because their accommodation, food and care are already paid for by the state.

The details below come from the Department for Work and Pensions (DWP) announcement of 15 January 2026. The primary BBC report behind today's coverage could not be reviewed, and no later decision was found. Any October development is therefore not reflected here.

What is proposed

The DWP's focus is Universal Credit. Under the current rules, it says, a single offender over 25 who is held in hospital and receives the higher rate for limited capability for work and work-related activity could be paid more than £800 a month. The department describes the saving over a long period of detention as "thousands" of pounds per person. It gives no overall figure and no estimate of how many people are affected.

The department says the change would reach offenders detained in hospital under court order, including those convicted of violent or sexual offences such as manslaughter and rape. It says people who have been detained under civil mental-health powers, with no offence, would not lose benefits. That distinction is the one the policy depends on, and it will need precise legal drafting. Court-ordered and civil detentions sit side by side in the same wards.

Prime Minister Keir Starmer said: "It cannot be right that offenders convicted of the most serious crimes continue to receive benefits they don't need." Work and Pensions Secretary Pat McFadden added: "we don't reward acts of violence."

The release also notes that Personal Independence Payment already stops after four weeks in hospital, and that Carer's Allowance is generally time-limited. The announcement therefore concerns Universal Credit, not the wider benefits system.

Unanswered questions

The release does not name a legal vehicle, whether regulations, primary legislation or something else. It gives no start date. It describes the plan as early-stage, with the Work and Pensions Secretary first gathering views from experts, and says the department will co-ordinate with the Scottish, Welsh and Northern Irish governments. Health and justice powers are devolved in different ways, so the territorial reach is not settled.

The campaign behind the plan was driven by victims' families. Reports have highlighted the case of the Nottingham attacker, who was reported to be eligible for Universal Credit of up to £360 a month while held at a secure hospital. The parents of one Nottingham victim welcomed the proposal.

Why clinicians will watch

In secure mental-health care, benefits can cover personal spending, and sometimes support for family members. Whether that holds for this group is not established. Removing it could affect rehabilitation and discharge planning, because patients approaching release often need money, and an address, in place before they leave. Mind published a response to the announcement, but its page could not be loaded here, so its position is not reported. Evidence on how the change would affect discharge outcomes was not found, so that remains an open question rather than a finding.

The next marker is the outcome of the expert consultation. It should show which benefits are covered, how civil and court-ordered patients will be distinguished, and whether any money is protected for dependants.
