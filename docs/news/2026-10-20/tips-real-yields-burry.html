TIPS pay 2.8% above inflation as Burry warns of years of poor real equity returns

In one sentence
A reported 2.8% real yield on TIPS raises the bar for equities, and Michael Burry's unverified forecast of 15–20 weak years sharpens the question, but neither is yet backed by index-level numbers.

A Treasury inflation-protected security now pays about 2.8% a year above inflation, according to a Benzinga report, and that figure has set off a familiar argument. If an investor can lock in a guaranteed real return of that size, what must the S&P 500 offer to justify its price?

The 2.8% number comes from a single source and carries no date or maturity. It is at least plausible. A tracker of Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis data put the 10-year TIPS yield at 2.92% on 2 October, up from 2.88% on the prior reading. The same tracker showed readings of about 2.35% to 2.41% in July. Those are secondary readings, not Treasury's own series, and the maturity behind the Benzinga figure is unknown. On any of them, real yields have risen by roughly half a percentage point since summer. They remain below the 3.15% record set in November 2008.

Why the hurdle matters

A TIPS yield is the return above measured inflation that the Treasury promises if the bond is held to maturity. Equities offer no such promise. Their expected real return is a forecast, usually built from the earnings yield (profits divided by price) plus growth. When the risk-free real rate climbs, the premium that stocks must offer over it narrows unless prices fall or earnings rise.

That is the logic behind the claim that high real yields "challenge valuations". It is a claim about relative attractiveness, not a measurement. The triage material includes no index-level earnings yield or forward price-to-earnings ratio, and none is asserted here. Without that number, the gap between stocks and TIPS cannot be calculated.

History offers some guidance on horizons. An Advisor Perspectives analysis of long TIPS yielding about 3% finds that US stocks have rarely returned less than 3% real over 30 years. Over 15 or 10 years, a shortfall against 2.5% to 3% real becomes more likely, though stocks still beat TIPS in most such periods.

Burry's longer view

That shorter-horizon risk is where Michael Burry's comments fit. Burry, who made his name betting against the housing market before 2008, has reportedly forecast 15 to 20 years of poor real equity returns. His argument, as relayed by Benzinga, is that speculation has overrun investing.

Three caveats apply. The only source is that one write-up, and the original post and wording have not been checked. The 15 to 20 year figure is a forecast, not an outcome. And Burry no longer runs outside money. He closed Scion Capital to outside investors and deregistered it in late 2023, and he now publishes mainly on Substack. His remarks are commentary, not a fund disclosure or a trade.

Still, the two items point the same way. A 2.8% real yield sets a floor under what long-horizon investors should demand from stocks. A decade-plus of weak real returns would be the case for taking that guaranteed return instead.

What to watch

The claim needs two numbers to carry weight: the S&P 500's forward earnings yield and the Treasury's own daily real yield curve for the relevant maturity. The earnings season now under way supplies the first. The gap between them, rather than the 2.8% in isolation, is what would show whether equities are still paying for the risk they carry.
