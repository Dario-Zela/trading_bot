Kenya cult leader guilty of 191 murders after telling parents to starve children

In one sentence
A Kenyan High Court convicted cult leader Paul Mackenzie and 28 others of murdering 191 people, mostly children, at Shakahola, rejecting the claim that victims fasted voluntarily.

A Kenyan High Court has found Paul Mackenzie, the self-styled pastor who led the Good News International Church, guilty of murder in the deaths of 191 people at Shakahola, a patch of bush country inland from the coastal town of Malindi. According to Kenyan reports of Monday's judgment, 190 of the victims were children and one was an adult. Justice Diana Kavedza convicted Mackenzie and 28 co-accused on all counts. Sentencing is set for 6 November.

The ruling is the first murder verdict in a case that has hung over Kenya since April 2023. It also answers a question the defence had put at the centre of the trial: whether people who starved had chosen to.

The forest and the fast

Mackenzie, a former taxi driver turned preacher, founded his church in 2003. He had run into the law before over his apocalyptic preaching. He said he closed the church in 2019 and moved to Shakahola, where followers bought or occupied plots of forest land. Prosecutors allege he told them to fast to death in order to "meet Jesus", and that children were starved first.

Police found the first graves in April 2023, and Mackenzie surrendered on 14 April. Exhumations went on for months. Wikipedia's tally, as of August 2025, lists 457 deaths and more than 600 people reported missing. Autopsies found that most victims had starved. Some bodies, including children's, showed signs of asphyxiation, strangulation or blows.

Those figures are larger than the 191 counts on which the court has now convicted. The gap reflects how the case was built. Prosecutors could charge only the deaths they could tie to named victims and to the accused. Mackenzie also faces separate cases over terrorism, radicalisation and child cruelty. One outlet reported a finding of radicalisation linked to 429 deaths. Reports differ on some details, including the number of accused (29 in most accounts) and which court sat, and these should be checked against the written judgment.

What the court decided

The central defence claim was that the victims, all under 16 according to reports, fasted voluntarily. The judge rejected it. Reports say the convictions rest on Section 203 of the Penal Code, which defines murder. Children cannot meaningfully consent to starvation, and the court was apparently willing to treat parents and leaders who enforced a fast as responsible for the deaths it caused.

That is the point with reach beyond this case. Kenya's constitution protects freedom of religion strongly. After the exhumations, a government inquiry and a parliamentary committee looked at how a man with earlier run-ins with the authorities, including arrests over his preaching, was able to gather hundreds of followers on remote land. Registration rules for churches have been debated since. A murder conviction shows prosecutors can reach a leader through ordinary criminal law, without any new regulation of belief.

A slow case, and what comes next

The trial has been slow even by the standards of Kenya's courts. Mackenzie and others pleaded not guilty in early 2024. At an earlier stage the court ruled the murder charges properly drafted but directed the prosecution to reduce the number of counts, a ruling reported at the time as cutting 191 to 12. The convictions on 191 counts suggest that is not how the case ended, though the reports reviewed do not explain the change. Separately, the state closed its child-cruelty case in September 2025 after 14 months and 75 witnesses. For families, many of whom waited a year or more for bodies to be identified and released, the pace has been a grievance in itself.

Three things now matter. The first is sentencing on 6 November, when the convicts are due to be produced in court. The second is the defence's likely appeal, which will test the reasoning on voluntariness. The third is whether the remaining terrorism, radicalisation and child-cruelty cases proceed more briskly. Regulators of unregistered churches will be watching the last of these, since a conviction that takes years to secure deters less than one that follows quickly.
