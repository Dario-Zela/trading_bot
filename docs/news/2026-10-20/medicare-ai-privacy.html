Trump's Medicare AI push raises patient data privacy concerns

In one sentence
Bloomberg reports privacy worries over the administration's push to use AI in Medicare, but the specific systems, data flows and critics could not be independently verified.

The Trump administration's effort to bring artificial intelligence into Medicare is drawing privacy objections, according to Bloomberg. The program covers tens of millions of older and disabled Americans. The report is the only source for the claim as it reached this desk. The paywalled text could not be read in full, so the specific applications, data-sharing arrangements and critics it cites are unverified here.

What is known about the push

The administration's best-documented Medicare AI effort is the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services' WISeR model, short for Wasteful and Inappropriate Service Reduction. It is a six-year pilot that uses AI and machine-learning tools to support prior authorization for selected services in Original Medicare. [^1] Reporting indicates it covers six states: Texas, New Jersey, Oklahoma, Ohio, Washington and Arizona. [^2]

CMS named the technology companies that would take part as model participants. They include Cohere Health, Genzeon, Humata Health, Innovaccer, Virtix Health and Zyter. [^1] One analysis has warned that vendors would be paid a share of the expenditures they avert, which critics say could create an incentive to deny more claims. [^1] CMS has said it will monitor participants' performance to guard against abuse. [^1]

It is not confirmed that the Bloomberg privacy report concerns WISeR specifically. It may refer to this pilot, to other initiatives, or to both.

Where the privacy worries come from

Much of the criticism so far has been about the fairness of coverage decisions rather than data handling. Bloomberg has reported objections to the Medicare administrator Mehmet Oz's call for AI to speed coverage decisions. [^3] House Democrats have pressed CMS for data on how well WISeR works. [^2] They also introduced legislation to repeal the model the day after the vendors were named. [^1]

Privacy concerns have a separate, related track. CBS News reported on a White House initiative involving major technology companies and health-data access for older and disabled Americans. The physician Céline Gounder said in that coverage that health providers are tightly regulated, but that apps outside those rules may sell personal data. She also noted that administration-held data could be shared with other federal agencies. She cited a lawsuit by more than 20 states over Medicaid data being given to Immigration and Customs Enforcement. [^4] That is a different programme from the one Bloomberg describes, and it should be read as context, not as confirmation of the Bloomberg claims.

What would settle the question

Several things would show whether the concern has substance. One is the contract terms and data-use agreements between CMS and the vendors, including whether beneficiary records can be retained or used to train models. Another is which privacy rules bind the vendors, and whether any sit outside the regulations that govern conventional providers. A third is whether any data flows to agencies beyond CMS. None of these was verifiable from the material available.

The next concrete checkpoints are any CMS response to the Bloomberg report and the congressional oversight already under way, including Democratic requests for WISeR performance data and the repeal bill. Those would show whether privacy joins coverage denials as a formal line of inquiry.

[^2]: Reporting on Democratic lawmakers' June 2026 request to Oz for WISeR data.
[^3]: Bloomberg Government, "Oz's Call for AI to Speed Coverage Decisions Draws Objections".
[^4]: CBS News, "Top tech companies to work with Trump administration on improving health care data access for older, disabled Americans".
