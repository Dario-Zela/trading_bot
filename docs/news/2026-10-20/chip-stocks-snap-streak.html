Chip stocks snap a Lehman-era losing streak as the Nasdaq 100 adds nearly $500bn

In one sentence
Chip stocks reportedly rebounded sharply, adding nearly $500bn to the Nasdaq 100, but the single-source figures are unverified and one session does not show a bottom.

Chip stocks bounced on Monday 19 October after a run of losses that Benzinga's report likens to the worst of the 2008 financial crisis. The rebound was big enough, the report says, to add nearly $500bn to the market value of the Nasdaq 100. AMD and SanDisk were named among the leaders. Several figures behind that account could not be confirmed independently, and this article flags them as it goes.

What is reported

The account rests on a single source. Benzinga says AMD led a semiconductor bounce on Monday, reversing a selloff in the sector. The Lehman-era comparison and the roughly $500bn gain in Nasdaq 100 market value come from the headline of the triage candidate. They are not backed by index levels, percentage moves or a stated cause.

The length of the losing streak is not given. Neither is the date range over which the $500bn was added. A search for corroboration turned up nothing that matched this episode. It returned only pieces from other dates about different sessions, including sharp swings in memory-chip names such as SanDisk and Micron. A figure of that size is plausible for a single strong session in an index where a few semiconductor companies carry heavy weight. Until the numbers are confirmed against exchange data or a market wrap, they should be read as reported rather than established.

Why a chip move moves the index

Semiconductors are among the largest weights in the Nasdaq 100. Nvidia, Broadcom, AMD and a group of equipment and memory makers sit alongside the mega-cap software and internet names. When the sector falls for days on end, the index falls with it. When it turns, the index recovers a large amount of value quickly, which is the pattern the $500bn figure implies.

The sector is also the most direct market proxy for spending on artificial intelligence. Many investors hold that trade through index funds and exchange-traded products without choosing it deliberately. A sell-off in chips therefore reaches well beyond specialist funds.

AMD's role as a leader fits that picture, since it is a high-beta name that tends to swing hard in both directions. SanDisk, a flash-memory maker, has also been volatile. Recent reporting describes violent moves in memory stocks, with one-day drops of more than ten percent in the group.

Bottom or bounce?

The angle that matters is whether this is a bottom or a bounce. The evidence available does not settle it.

A 2008 comparison describes how severe the decline was, not how it ended. After large drops, sharp one-day recoveries are common and often fade. Short covering and bargain hunting can lift prices without any change in the underlying case for the sector. The triage material identifies no catalyst for the reversal, such as earnings, a policy shift or a change in demand. Without one, a single session is thin evidence of a durable low.

A lasting turn would need to show up in several places. Breadth would have to widen beyond a few leaders. Follow-through would have to hold over several sessions. Earnings guidance from the companies themselves would need to support the rebound. A flat or falling next session would point to a bounce.

What to watch

The nearest test is this week's earnings calendar, which includes Meta and Tesla (see related coverage). Their capital-spending comments feed straight into expectations for chip demand. Close attention should also go to whether the Nasdaq 100 holds Monday's gains, and to whether the semiconductor sector's recovery spreads beyond AMD and SanDisk. If Benzinga's figures are borne out by closing data, the rebound is real. Whether it is a low remains open.
