Ukrainians are dying because of US curbs on intelligence sharing, Zelensky adviser says

In one sentence
Zelensky adviser Serhii Beskrestnov told the BBC that lives have been lost because US curbs on air-raid alert intelligence leave too little warning; the White House denies any change.

A senior voice in Kyiv's defence circle has accused Washington of costing Ukrainian lives by restricting the intelligence that lets the country warn its citizens of incoming missiles. Serhii Beskrestnov, an adviser to President Volodymyr Zelensky who is widely known by the call-sign "Flash", told the BBC in Kyiv that civilians had died because there was not enough time to alert them to ballistic missile strikes. "People have already died," he said. The White House says nothing has changed.

The claim rests on one interview and on Kyiv's own account of events, and no casualty figures, locations or dates have been given for the deaths he describes. It arrives, though, alongside a separate push by Zelensky himself, and it turns a dispute over American policy into a question about who is, and is not, getting a warning.

What the adviser said

Beskrestnov, speaking to the BBC's Dan Johnson and in an interview published on 19 October, described the intelligence situation as "very strange". American warnings, he said, sometimes arrived before an attack and sometimes did not. In one case a warning came about a minute before impact, which is far too little time for people to reach shelter.

He drew a narrow line around the problem. Other intelligence cooperation with the US continues, he said; the difficulty is confined to the air-raid alert component. He also said the US had not explained who was responsible for the disruptions, and described the simultaneous problems as "probably" a deliberate American move to pressure Ukraine into negotiations with Russia. That last point is his inference, offered without evidence in the interview as reported, and Washington rejects it.

He added that Ukraine is looking at alternative communications systems, and that its own radar data for early warning is limited, which is why it leans on partner intelligence for ballistic threats that are fast and difficult to track.

A week of disputed interruptions

The BBC said Ukraine first reported an interruption to US intelligence sharing the previous week, amid a dispute over a controversial deal to import Russian oil. Zelensky told NBC that sharing was restored after a three-day pause. Zelensky has since reported two overnight ballistic missile attacks on Kyiv, one with no warning at all and one that gave residents minimal time to take cover. The BBC article gave no casualty figures for those strikes.

Axios reported on 19 October that Zelensky lobbied US senators, including Richard Blumenthal and Thom Tillis, to press the White House over alleged intelligence and weapons curbs. According to that account, Ukraine had gone without early warnings on three occasions in the past week, and Zelensky said Kyiv had "received no clear explanation from Washington". Axios's page could not be retrieved in full for this article, so those details come from a search summary and should be read with that caveat.

There is a second strand. Restrictions on Starlink and its military counterpart, Starshield, are said to be continuing. Starshield mobile units were reportedly limited to 100 mph (160 km/h) while connected, which hampers Ukraine's ability to intercept fast drones and to run mid-range drone strikes into occupied territory. Elon Musk has said the US government ultimately controls Starshield access. Trump has denied involvement. The BBC said it had contacted Starlink for comment.

Washington's response

The White House said there had been no changes to intelligence sharing with Ukraine. An official, speaking on background, said President Trump is focused on ending the war and that the US has given more aid than any other country. Trump himself put it more bluntly: "We've given a lot to Ukraine. They've given us nothing."

Secretary of State Marco Rubio denied that there had been a "pause" and attributed a recent problem to a "software problem involving their early warning system" — that is, a fault on Ukraine's side rather than a decision in Washington.

The two accounts are not easy to reconcile. Zelensky has acknowledged a three-day pause that was later lifted; the White House denies any change; Rubio points to Ukrainian software. All three could be partly true, since a technical fault and a policy decision are not mutually exclusive, but nothing public yet establishes which explanation covers which gap in warnings.

Why it matters

The episode has precedent. In March 2025, after the Oval Office confrontation between Trump and Zelensky, the US briefly halted military aid and intelligence sharing, and CIA director John Ratcliffe confirmed the pause. It was lifted within days once Kyiv signalled willingness to accept a ceasefire proposal. Kyiv has therefore long understood that American intelligence can be used as leverage, and Beskrestnov's reading of events follows that template.

The human stakes are visible in the same news cycle. On Monday a drone strike near a park café in the north-eastern Kharkiv region killed at least six people, including a toddler and an 11-year-old girl, and injured 14. Zelensky called it an "absolute atrocity". Drones are a different threat from ballistic missiles and nothing reported ties that attack to any intelligence gap, but it illustrates how thin the margin for warning is.

For European allies the question is whether they can substitute for what Washington supplies. Early warning against ballistic missiles depends on space-based and radar assets that few countries other than the US possess. The dispute also lands as Moscow courts Washington diplomatically; Vladimir Putin has proposed a Trump–Xi summit in China to discuss a Ukraine deal.

What to watch

Three things would settle the matter. The first is documentation: dates, locations and casualty counts for the strikes that Kyiv says came without adequate warning, which would let outsiders test the claim. The second is whether the senators Zelensky lobbied press the administration for a formal answer on whether any directive restricted alert-related sharing. The third is whether the warning gaps recur. If they do, the "software problem" explanation becomes harder to sustain; if they stop, the dispute may be filed as another short-lived episode of leverage that nobody in Washington will admit to.
