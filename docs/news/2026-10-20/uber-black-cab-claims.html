Uber wins bid to throw out £200m black cab claims

In one sentence
Uber has had a £340m black cab lawsuit, including a roughly £200m drivers' claim, thrown out as time-barred, but the ruling is open to appeal and the claims' merits were never tested.

Uber has won the first round of a fight that, at its largest, put about £340m at stake. The High Court has thrown out two linked lawsuits over how the company obtained its London private hire licence. The ruling rests on timing, not on the merits, so the claimants' appeal options matter as much as the win.

The headline figure of about £200m refers to the largest strand: roughly 13,000 black cab drivers, with individual claims worth up to £25,000 each. A second claim, from two defunct minicab firms, Kabbee and Iride, covered the rest. The company claims were valued at about £199m and £141m, so the £340m total is the figure most outlets use. The drivers' share is the one the angle singles out.

What the court decided

The drivers alleged that Uber gave Transport for London misleading information when it applied for its operator licence, so the licence should never have been granted. They said Uber then undercut black cab fares. The claims covered the period from 2012 to 14 March 2018. Uber denied the allegations and says it operates lawfully in London with a full TfL licence. It has called the claims "completely unfounded".

The judge did not reach any of that. The ruling found the claimants had waited too long to bring their claims. Under the Limitation Act 1980, most such claims must be brought within six years. Uber argued each claim was time-barred. The drivers argued the clock should start later, because they could not reasonably have discovered the alleged deception until a June 2018 court hearing over Uber's licence. That hearing followed TfL's 2017 decision not to renew it.

The court ordered a separate trial on this question in October 2025, using 20 sample claimants, 10 chosen by each side. That hearing took place in late June. The judgment was announced on Monday 19 October. Reuters noted that a copy of the judgment was not yet available when it reported the result. Reports differ on the judge's name, so none is given here.

Why a strike-out is not a clean exit

A limitation ruling is final only if it survives appeal. The court will not examine the substance of the allegations unless the claimants win an appeal. If they do, the case revives and Uber's exposure returns in full. RGL Management, which ran the claims, was reported to be "significantly disappointed" and to be considering its options with its legal team. No appeal has been reported as filed. The grounds for any challenge are visible, though. The ruling turns on when the drivers could reasonably have discovered the alleged deception, and the date of that discovery is exactly what the claimants contest.

There is also a reason the case stayed alive for so long. The claimants' case for a later start date depends on a factual finding about what drivers knew and when. Appeal courts tend to defer to trial judges on such findings, which favours Uber. That is an inference from how appeals generally work, not something reported about this case.

What it means for Uber

A claim of this size is a contingent liability, not a cost already incurred. Removing it does not change Uber's London fares or licensing directly. It does remove a large, unquantified risk that would have hung over its UK business for years, and it takes away a line of argument for those who say the licence was obtained improperly. Uber's licence position with TfL is not affected by this ruling.

The next marker is whether RGL Management seeks permission to appeal, and whether the Court of Appeal grants it. Until the deadline for that application passes without a filing, or an appeal fails, the £340m remains in the "won, not closed" category.
