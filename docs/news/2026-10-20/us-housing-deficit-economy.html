US housing sentiment hits 2012 lows as the $1.99tn deficit pushes interest costs above defence

In one sentence
The NAHB builder index fell to 29, its lowest since June 2012, as 7%-plus mortgage rates meet a $1.99tn deficit that puts interest costs above defence.

US homebuilders are more pessimistic than at any point since the housing bust was ending. The NAHB/Wells Fargo Housing Market Index fell three points to 29 in October, its lowest reading since June 2012, according to the National Association of Home Builders. Economists polled by Bloomberg had expected 31. Readings below 50 mean more builders rate conditions as poor than good, and the index has now been under that line for 30 consecutive months.

The same week, preliminary Congressional Budget Office figures put the fiscal 2026 federal deficit at $1.99tn. At that level, interest on the national debt costs the Treasury more than the Pentagon. The two stories are linked, and the link is the cost of money.

What the survey shows

All three components of the index fell. Current sales conditions slipped two points to 33. Expectations for sales over the next six months dropped five to 32. Buyer traffic fell five to 18, which Robert Dietz, NAHB's chief economist, said is the weakest since April 2012 if the pandemic-hit April 2020 reading is set aside.

Builders are paying to keep deals alive. Some 43% cut prices in October, the highest share in the 2020–2026 period, and the average cut held at 6% for a seventh straight month. Some 65% used sales incentives such as mortgage-rate buydowns.

The regional picture is weak but uneven. On three-month averages, the Northeast stood at 38 and the Midwest at 40. The South, where speculative building is heaviest, was at 30, and the West at 26.

NAHB chairman Bill Owens pointed to the cause: "Mortgage rates have climbed well above 7% in recent weeks." Dietz added that "demand persists, but higher mortgage rates" are keeping buyers on the sidelines, along with elevated construction costs and weaker consumer confidence. Higher fuel prices and inflation also hurt traffic. Coverage of the release also cites labour shortages tied to tighter immigration enforcement, though that is not in NAHB's own summary.

Testing the 'Covid' comparison

The discovery brief for this piece described homebuilder stocks as trading "like the Covid period". That claim could not be supported. No volatility reading, breadth measure or drawdown figure was found to back it, and the NAHB release is a sentiment survey, not a market price. The comparison is therefore left out.

The historical comparison the data does support is narrower. Buyer traffic is at its lowest since 2012, apart from the brief collapse of spring 2020. That makes this a slump in demand driven by financing costs. The pandemic drop was a shutdown that reversed within months. Whether builder shares are pricing the slump is a separate question. It should be answered by the sector's performance against the S&P 500 over a stated window, which is not yet in hand.

The deficit and the price of money

The fiscal 2026 deficit of $1.99tn is a CBO preliminary estimate. It is up about 12% on the roughly $1.8tn of fiscal 2025 and is the largest since 2021. Reports put spending at about $7.4tn, up around 6%, and revenue at about $5.4tn, up around 3%. Spending is growing about twice as fast as receipts. Treasury's final monthly statement could revise the figure.

The interest-above-defence comparison is not new. Net interest outlays passed defence spending in fiscal 2024, and CBO's baseline has interest exceeding defence in every year of its outlook. Estimates of this year's interest bill differ, from about $1.0tn in CBO's baseline to above $1.1tn in some press tallies, and the source reports conflict on detail. In the first half of the year interest reached roughly $529bn against about $461bn for defence. The news is the size of the gap, not that it exists.

The connection to housing runs through long-term yields. A larger deficit means more Treasury issuance, and mortgage rates are priced off the 10-year Treasury yield. The causal weight of deficits against inflation and monetary policy is contested, and this analysis does not apportion it. What can be said is that borrowing costs rose and builder sentiment fell in the same month.

What to watch

The next hard tests are the September housing starts and permits data from the Census Bureau, and the November NAHB index. Starts and permits show whether sentiment is turning into lower construction. Mortgage rates around 7% are the variable most likely to change that reading. Treasury's final fiscal-year statement will settle the deficit and interest totals. Until those arrive, the survey is the clearest evidence of a housing market constrained by the cost of money.
