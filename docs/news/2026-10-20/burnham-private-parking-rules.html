Burnham sets out rules to outlaw 'rip-off' private parking charges

In one sentence
Andy Burnham has announced rules to curb 'rip-off' private parking charges, a consumer-facing move that also bears on his political profile; the detailed terms remain to be seen.

Andy Burnham has announced new rules intended to curb what he calls "rip-off" private parking charges. The move targets the operators who issue penalty notices in car parks at retail parks, hospitals, stations and housing estates, and it is aimed squarely at motorists' sense of being unfairly fined.

This article rests on the headline announcement as reported by the BBC and the Guardian. The detailed terms had not been independently checked at the time of writing. The size of any cap, the start date and the enforcement mechanism are therefore not set out here.

Why private parking is a political issue

Private parking enforcement sits in an unusual corner of British regulation. Councils run on-street and public car-park penalties under statutory rules. Private firms instead rely on contract law: a motorist who overstays or fails to pay is said to have broken the terms displayed on a sign, and the operator pursues a "charge". Those firms obtain keeper details from the DVLA, and unpaid charges can end in county court claims.

Parliament tried to impose order on this system with the Parking (Code of Practice) Act 2019. The Act envisaged a single statutory code and a single appeals service in place of the competing trade-body schemes that operators currently use. Implementation stalled. An earlier draft code, which proposed lowering the maximum charge, was withdrawn by the incoming Labour government in 2024 for further review. Any new set of rules therefore revisits an unfinished piece of business that has changed hands several times.

The politics

Burnham's announcement matters for two reasons, and only one of them is about car parks. For motorists and for the operators, what counts is whether the rules bind: whether charges are capped, whether signage standards are mandatory, and whether appeals are independent of the industry. Those details will decide whether the "rip-off" label sticks to the practices that remain.

For Burnham, the choice of subject is itself a signal. Parking fines are a cheap, widely felt grievance that cuts across party lines, and a policy aimed at them is easy to explain and hard to oppose. His recent interventions, including a call for officials to find more radical options for Thames Water, point to a politician building a public case around everyday costs and corporate behaviour. How far that serves his own standing is a matter of inference rather than evidence, and nothing in the announcement itself settles it.

What to watch

The test is the text of the rules. Operators' trade bodies are likely to argue that lower charges would weaken deterrence and leave landowners unable to manage their spaces. Consumer groups will look for a single independent appeals body and a ban on the more aggressive add-on fees. Those documents, and the response to them, will show whether this is a durable change or another announcement in a long-running saga.
