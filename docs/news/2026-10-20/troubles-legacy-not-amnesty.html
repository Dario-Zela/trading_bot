Troubles legacy bill 'not an amnesty for soldiers', NI secretary insists

In one sentence
The Northern Ireland secretary insists the Troubles legacy bill is not an amnesty for soldiers, but critics dispute that and the real test is its practical effect on prosecutions.

The Northern Ireland secretary has rejected the charge that the government's Troubles legacy legislation amounts to an amnesty for former soldiers, telling critics it is "not an amnesty for soldiers". The claim is contested. The question underneath it is whether a state can close off prosecutions of its own former troops while describing the result as something else.

The secretary's remarks were reported by the BBC. The coverage available at the time of writing does not set out the bill's current stage or every provision. Those details are therefore not asserted here.

A dispute with a long history

The argument is not new. Successive governments have tried to deal with the roughly 3,500 deaths of the Troubles, many of them still unresolved, without reopening every case in court. Ministers have repeatedly drawn a line between an amnesty, which would formally pardon or immunise people, and other mechanisms such as a statutory bar on prosecutions or a body that offers conditional immunity in return for information. Reporting on earlier plans noted that the government distinguished a prosecution bar from an amnesty, and that previous convictions would stand.

Critics have not accepted the distinction. Amnesty International warned about an earlier version of the legislation that it would give "impunity to murderers and those responsible for torture". Unionist politicians have made a different objection: that protections framed as being for veterans are generic ones, equally available to paramilitaries and former police officers. Keir Starmer, answering that point, said the provisions were for veterans.

The Irish government has also been a critic. It considered legal action over the 2023 legacy law, and the European Convention on Human Rights frames the argument over whether the state must investigate deaths in which its agents were involved.

What the secretary's wording does

Saying a measure is "not an amnesty for soldiers" is a narrow denial. It can be true of the label while leaving open the effect. If a mechanism ends investigations or prosecutions for a defined group, families and lawyers will ask whether the practical outcome differs from a pardon. If the same mechanism applies to everyone, including paramilitaries, the soldiers-only description fails in a different way.

The test critics are likely to apply is therefore functional. Do the provisions allow cases involving former soldiers to be investigated and, where evidence supports it, prosecuted? Or do they close them off? The answer will turn on the text and on any amendments, which this report has not been able to verify.

What to watch

The next marker is the bill's parliamentary passage, including any amendments tabled and how peers and the Northern Ireland parties respond. Reaction from victims' families and from Dublin will indicate whether the secretary's distinction persuades anyone beyond the government benches. A court challenge, if one follows, would test the same question on legal rather than political ground.
