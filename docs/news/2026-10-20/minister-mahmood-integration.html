Minister says Mahmood's migration policy will 'prolong integration'

In one sentence
Energy Secretary Miatta Fahnbulleh appeared to warn that Mahmood's longer settlement wait would hamper integration, then pulled the post, saying it misstated her views.

A cabinet minister appears to have broken ranks with the Home Secretary over migration, then moved quickly to retreat. The Guardian reported on Monday that Miatta Fahnbulleh, the energy secretary, had seemed to criticise Shabana Mahmood's plans to lengthen the route to permanent settlement. She argued that making people wait longer would prolong the process of integration rather than aid it.

The remark did not come in a speech or an interview. It sat in a write-up of a community meeting in Peckham, her constituency, held in September and posted on her constituency website. The Telegraph first drew attention to it. The page quoted her as saying: "If we continually prolong the time people have to wait for settlement…" The rest of the sentence is not available in the version seen. Fahnbulleh has since taken the post down, saying it did not accurately reflect her views. Her office described the remarks as paraphrased and said the account would be rewritten.

That qualification matters. The words are the website's summary of her comments, not a verified transcript. Her denial concerns the accuracy of the write-up, so the extent of any disagreement with Mahmood is less clear than the headline suggests.

The policy at issue

The plan under discussion is the Home Office's "earned settlement" scheme. It would raise the standard qualifying period for indefinite leave to remain from five years to 10, with migrants expected to earn settlement through their contribution to the country. Refugees would face a longer route still, of up to 20 years, with reviews every 30 months.

The government has not yet published its final response to the consultation. Concessions are thought likely for some professions, care workers among them. Mahmood has told MPs that decisions will come later this year, without naming a date. Prime Minister Andy Burnham has said he backs most of the changes.

A wider pattern of unease

Fahnbulleh's comments are not the only sign of discomfort. Angela Rayner, the housing secretary, has said that unamended, the changes would unfairly penalise care staff who "moved their lives here to look after our loved ones." Fahnbulleh herself, who came to Britain as a child after fleeing the civil war in Liberia, told the Guardian's Politics Weekly podcast that her family would have found Mahmood's changes "tough." She called the immigration debate toxic and argued for a system built on "control and compassion", with more weight on integration.

The argument has a logic of its own. Mahmood's case is that a slower pace of settlement lets communities absorb newcomers and that the right to stay should be earned. Critics, Fahnbulleh apparently among them, counter that a decade of uncertainty keeps people in a provisional status that makes them harder to integrate, not easier. Both sides claim integration as their aim, which is why the dispute is awkward for a government that wants to be seen as firm and fair.

What to watch

The test is the government's formal response to the consultation. Reports suggest Mahmood may be reconsidering parts of her plans. If the final version carves out care workers or softens the refugee route, the Peckham post will look like an early marker of where cabinet opinion sat. If it does not, Fahnbulleh's retraction and Rayner's objection will stand as unresolved disagreement that ministers have so far kept short of open confrontation. Both outcomes are due later this year.
