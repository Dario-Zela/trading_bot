No 10 says Hegseth 'wrong' on non-US Nato troops in Afghanistan

In one sentence
Downing Street has publicly rebuked Pete Hegseth for claiming only one or two Nato countries were fighting by the end of the Afghan war, a conflict that cost 457 British lives.

Downing Street has publicly told the US defence secretary he is wrong. Pete Hegseth, speaking at the University of Tennessee, said that although dozens of countries joined the Nato mission in Afghanistan, only "one or two" were still fighting by the end of the war. At a Westminster briefing on Monday, a spokesperson for the prime minister, Andy Burnham, rejected the claim in terms that British officials rarely use about a senior member of an allied government.

The row is narrow in its immediate substance. It is also a sign of how little the argument over who carried the burden in Afghanistan has settled, more than five years after the last Western troops left.

What was said

Hegseth, who served in Afghanistan as an Army captain, drew on his own time there. He recalled that American troops joked that the ISAF patch, the badge of the International Security Assistance Force, stood for "I Saw Americans Fighting." The reporting available does not give the date of the speech. It was reported on 19 October.

The No 10 spokesperson answered at the regular briefing. Hegseth was "wrong" to diminish the role played by international forces in a conflict that cost the lives of 457 British personnel. "We are incredibly proud of our armed forces," the spokesperson said, adding that their service and sacrifice "will never be forgotten, and it is wrong to diminish their role." The spokesperson also said that more than 450 British personnel "never returned." The Pentagon had not responded to a request for comment from the Daily Beast.

A familiar argument

Hegseth has made a version of this point before. In June 2025 he told the Senate Armed Services Committee that ISAF had been "a lotta flags" but "not a lot of on-the-ground capability." He added: "I recognize that there were lives lost from other countries. But the bulk of the effort was Americans." Senator Chris Coons answered him on the record. Coons pointed to the allies who served and died after Nato invoked Article 5, the alliance's collective-defence clause, for the first and only time after the September 2001 attacks. He noted that Denmark, with about six million people, suffered some of the highest per-capita losses of any coalition member, behind only the United States.

In January this year Donald Trump said Nato sent "some troops" that "stayed a little back, a little off the front lines." Keir Starmer, then prime minister, called the remarks "insulting and frankly appalling" and said Trump should apologise. Trump later praised British forces on Truth Social, citing the 457 dead and calling the bond "too strong to ever be broken." This week's remarks are therefore the second such claim from the administration in nine months, and the third occasion on which the point has been made in public.

What the record shows

The figures cut against the claim. The United States lost about 2,461 service members in Afghanistan, the largest toll of any country. More than 1,100 troops from other Nato countries were also killed, among them the 457 Britons. That makes Britain's the largest allied loss, and the largest British death toll in any conflict since Korea. Thirty-one countries besides the United States had personnel killed. The House of Commons Library puts the cost of the UK contribution above £32.8bn in 2024-25 prices.

Hegseth's underlying point has some basis, but it is a narrower one than the one he made. The United States supplied most of the troops and most of the fighting. Some allies operated under national caveats that limited where their forces could go, a grievance that American commanders voiced during the war. Even so, British, Canadian, Danish, Dutch and Estonian forces took heavy casualties in Helmand and Kandahar. The deaths are the best evidence that these were combat roles, and the figures leave little room for the idea that only one or two countries fought.

Why it matters

Washington has not reversed itself, and it has not yet issued a clarification. Without either, the exchange could fade as a one-day spat. The substance behind it will not fade. Article 5 is the foundation of Nato's deterrence, and it rests on a belief that each member will treat an attack on another as an attack on itself. The only time it has been invoked, the allies who answered were those whose sacrifice is now being discounted. A US defence chief who publicly questions that sacrifice invites allied governments to ask what the guarantee is worth.

Britain's response also shows how far Downing Street's position has moved. Starmer's criticism of Trump in January was unusual. A formal rebuke of Hegseth from a new prime minister's office, delivered in public, suggests No 10 sees little political cost in speaking up, and some cost in staying silent.

The next test is whether the Pentagon responds, and whether Hegseth's remarks come up when Nato defence ministers next meet. Allied governments will also be watching for any sign that the White House repeats the line, as it did in January.
