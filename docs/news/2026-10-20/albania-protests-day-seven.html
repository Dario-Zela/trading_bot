Seventh day of violent clashes between Albanian protesters and police

In one sentence
A week of violent clashes between protesters and police in Tirana shows no sign of easing, but the cause, casualty counts and staying power of the movement remain unclear.

Albania has now gone a week with violent clashes between protesters and police in its capital. The BBC's World report describes the seventh consecutive day of confrontation, and the unrest shows no sign of easing. Whether this hardens into a sustained political crisis is not yet clear. Early reporting points to a mix of grievances rather than one clean demand.

What is known

The demonstrations appear to have begun around 13 October in Tirana, when several hundred people gathered outside the education ministry. Reporting from the first days describes students angry about conditions and tuition fees at universities. The crowds grew and the demands widened to include the resignation of Prime Minister Edi Rama.

Police have answered with tear gas and water cannon. Protesters have thrown stones and set fire to rubbish bins to block roads. Some accounts describe attacks on government buildings, banks and businesses. Student boycotts of lectures and smaller demonstrations have been reported in other cities.

Casualty figures differ by outlet. One account puts the toll at 89 injured, 61 of them officers. Another cites 51 injured officers and 65 arrests. The BBC brief gives no figures. The counts should be treated as provisional until a single official tally is published.

Rama's response

Rama has refused to step down. Quoted in coverage of the unrest, he argued that governments in Albania change through elections, and that the country held one last year. He has also blamed unspecified "foreign hands" for the escalation. That claim has been reported without supporting evidence, and it echoes a pattern in which governments in the region attribute street protest to outside meddling.

The EU delegation in Tirana has called for a proportionate response from the authorities. Albania is a candidate for EU membership, and it has been negotiating its accession. Sustained street violence and heavy-handed policing would complicate that process.

Why this might not be a one-issue protest

The students are not the only group on the streets. Some of those taking part belong to a broader anti-government movement that has also objected to a planned resort development linked to Jared Kushner, the son-in-law of US President Donald Trump. Earlier demonstrations against the project reportedly led to clashes outside parliament. If those strands have merged with the student protests, the movement would be more political and more durable than a dispute over tuition fees.

That is the open question. Sustained unrest usually needs organisation, a unifying demand and a government unwilling or unable to defuse it. Rama has a recent electoral mandate, which makes a forced exit unlikely in the short term. A week of daily violence that includes arson and mass injuries is nevertheless unusual even by the standards of Albanian politics, which has seen bitter confrontations before.

What to watch

Three things will show where this is heading. The first is whether the daily crowds grow or shrink and whether other cities join in. The second is whether the government offers concessions on university fees or conditions, which would split the students from the wider opposition. The third is whether the police response escalates, since a death in custody or on the streets has often turned protest movements in the Balkans into something larger. The next round of demonstrations, and any official statement of casualties and arrests, will be the first test.
