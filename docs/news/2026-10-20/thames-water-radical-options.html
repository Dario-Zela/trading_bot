Burnham urges officials to find more radical options for Thames Water

In one sentence
Burnham is urging officials to find more radical options for Thames Water, but no specific option is named, so creditors, bills and the sector model hinge on a proposal yet to appear.

Pressure on ministers to find a bolder answer for Thames Water has resurfaced, with Andy Burnham urging officials to look at "more radical" options for Britain's most heavily indebted water company. The Guardian's politics coverage is the only source for the call itself. It names no specific option, and the full text could not be checked for this article, so the substance depends on what officials put forward.

What has been on the table

The plain reading of "more radical" is a step beyond the creditor-led recapitalisation that has dominated the company's story. Press reports since July, mostly citing unnamed officials and The Times, describe a spread of routes:

  • Special administration. This is a temporary form of state control, and it is the option most often described as the radical one. Reports in late August said it had been shelved after officials warned a rescue could cost taxpayers about £2bn over 18 months, and raised the risk of legal challenge. A government source was quoted as saying it was "not off the table", with further work to be done before any decision.
  • Mutual or localised public control. Reports say Burnham has favoured a mutualised model for water companies over full nationalisation. He has also floated a more localised public-control option modelled on his Manchester bus franchising.
  • The creditor plan. A consortium called London & Valley Water has put forward a rescue that reportedly offers the government a "golden share" and a restructuring spread over about a decade. It is still live, according to the same reports.

None of these figures has been confirmed in an official statement that this desk has seen, and they should be read as press-reported.

Why the choice matters beyond one company

The three constituencies at stake pull in different directions. Creditors want to be treated as lenders to a regulated monopoly, with claims honoured in line with their seniority. Bill payers want to avoid funding a rescue twice, once through higher charges and once through tax. The regulated-utility model, in which private capital funds infrastructure in return for a predictable, regulator-set return, depends on the first being credible and the second being tolerable.

Special administration would put that bargain to the test. It would hand control to a court-appointed administrator with a statutory objective of keeping services running, and would leave open how much of the debt is written down and who absorbs the loss. The reported £2bn cost estimate is one reason the idea has stalled. A mutual or public-control structure raises a different question, namely who would lend to such a vehicle and at what price.

What would move markets

As it stands this is a political-pressure story rather than a market-moving one. A call for something bolder, without a named option, changes little in the pricing of the company's debt or in how other listed and privately held utilities are valued. That would change if officials set out a specific proposal with a cost, a treatment of creditors and a route for setting bills. The assessment is reportedly expected to take several months, so a decision within the week is unlikely.

The next evidence will be whether the government publishes its options work, whether the creditor consortium's plan is formally engaged or rejected, and what the regulator, Ofwat, says about bills under any of the structures.
