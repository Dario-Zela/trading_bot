China's growth accelerates as the AI boom lifts exports

In one sentence
China's economy grew 4.5% in the third quarter, up from 4.3%, helped by AI-linked exports, though weak retail sales and property suggest the boost is narrow.

China's economy grew faster in the third quarter than it did in the second, and the main push came from abroad. Output expanded 4.5% in the July–September period compared with a year earlier, according to the National Bureau of Statistics, up from 4.3% in the spring, which had been a low for more than three years. A Reuters poll had pointed to 4.4%. Reporting by the BBC and others attributes much of the pick-up to global demand for chips and data-centre hardware. The statistics bureau's own release does not mention artificial intelligence, so that link is the press's reading of the trade data, not an official finding.

The question is how far the boom reaches. A 0.2-point acceleration that leans on one category of exports is a different thing from a broad recovery.

What the numbers show

The trade data, published a week earlier, carry the story. Exports rose 36.3% year on year in September to $447.18bn, up from about 25% in August and the largest monthly rise since the pandemic distorted comparisons in 2021. Semiconductor exports jumped more than 108%, and shipments of automatic data-processing machines, the category that includes servers, rose nearly 55%. Over the first nine months, exports rose 21.3% to $3.37tn, leaving a surplus of $938.1bn.

The pattern was not confined to one market. Shipments to ASEAN, where much electronics assembly sits, rose more than 52% to $81.7bn. Sales to the EU rose 25.3% and to the US 39.6%, to $47.88bn.

Taylor Wang of HSBC called high-tech demand, "supported by the global and domestic AI upcycle," the key driver. Imports also rose, by 31% in September, which fits a supply chain in which China imports components, assembles or processes them, and re-exports the result. Some of the export value is therefore recycled foreign content, not domestic value added.

The domestic side

The home economy looks different. September retail sales rose just 1.1% from a year earlier. Industrial output grew 5.5%, a respectable figure that mostly reflects factories working to fill export orders. Property investment kept falling, and households remain cautious after years of falling home prices.

Lynn Song of ING put it plainly: "Growth remains quite unbalanced, with domestic data quite soft in comparison." The pattern also shows up within exports. Some traditional lines are shrinking; ceramics shipments, for example, have fallen by more than a quarter. The gains are concentrated in electronics and computing hardware, which employ fewer people per dollar of output than the labour-intensive goods they are displacing.

The evidence so far therefore supports the narrower reading. The AI boom is lifting a set of exporters, their suppliers and regional assemblers, and it is lifting headline GDP. There is little sign yet of it showing up in wages, retail spending or construction.

Why it matters beyond China

For global markets the data cut two ways. They confirm that AI infrastructure spending is large enough to move the national accounts of the world's second-largest economy, which bears on the same demand that drove the chip rally covered elsewhere in this edition. They also show how much of the supply chain for that spending runs through China and its neighbours.

For readers trying to judge China's recovery, the composition is the caution. A 4.5% print sits at the bottom of Beijing's 4.5%–5% target range for the year. Analysts quoted in coverage of the release said the target remains reachable but would need strong fourth-quarter growth. That leaves the economy dependent on a cycle in which it is a supplier, not a decision-maker: if AI-related orders slow, or trade barriers rise in response to a widening surplus, there is little domestic demand to take up the slack. A surplus of nearly $940bn in nine months is also the sort of figure that tends to draw political attention from trading partners.

What to watch

The next tests come in November, when October trade data will show whether September's 36% export surge was a peak or a plateau, and when retail sales and property data will show whether any of the strength is spreading inland. Beijing's policy response, whether more support for household spending or continued reliance on exports, will matter as much as the next quarterly print. Full-year GDP, due in January, will settle whether the 4.5% target was met.

Note: The BBC article itself was not retrieved; figures come from the National Bureau of Statistics release as reported by syndicated outlets, and from customs data reported by Al Jazeera.
