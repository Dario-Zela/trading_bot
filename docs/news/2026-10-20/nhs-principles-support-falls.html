Public support for NHS founding principles is falling, survey finds

In one sentence
King's Fund analysis of British Social Attitudes data finds support for the NHS's three founding principles fell between 2021 and 2025, though majorities still back each one.

Public support for the three ideas on which the National Health Service was founded — that care should be free when used, available to everyone, and paid for out of general taxation — has weakened over the past five years. That is the finding of new analysis by the King's Fund, a health think tank, reported by the Guardian on 20 October 2026. Backing for each principle remains high. The direction of travel is what makes the finding of interest to ministers weighing funding and reform.

The analysis draws on British Social Attitudes (BSA) survey data and compares 2021 with 2025. The coverage seen for this article does not give a sample size, fieldwork dates, question wording or margin of error, and it refers only to the "UK population". The BSA is a long-running survey, but the full King's Fund tables should be checked before the size of any movement is treated as settled. Several figures below are incomplete for the same reason.

What the figures show

Support fell between 2021 and 2025 for all three principles, with one exception: backing for tax funding rose slightly among millennials, born 1981–1996.

The steepest reported fall was on universality, the idea that the NHS should be available to everyone. Among people born before the Second World War, agreement fell from 87.1% in 2021 to 73.2% in 2025, a drop of 13.9 percentage points. Among baby boomers (born 1946–1964) it fell from 83.1% to 71.2%, a drop of 11.9 points. Figures for the other generations were not given in the coverage.

Support for free care fell in all five age groups, by smaller amounts. Between 85% and 92% of the population still hold that view, depending on age group. Per-group numbers were not published in the account seen.

On tax funding, the sharpest change is among Generation Z (born 1997–2012). Their support fell by 11.2 points over the period, to 74.1%, against at least 80% in every other group. The fall was larger than among Generation X, boomers or the prewar cohort.

These numbers need careful reading. A fall of 11 to 14 points is large for an attitudes series, but the levels are still majorities. Nobody has shown that any group now opposes the principles. Whether the movements are statistically robust, particularly for Gen Z, whose subsample is likely to be small, cannot be judged without the sample sizes.

Possible explanations

The King's Fund offers explanations as possibilities, not established causes. Danielle Jefferies, a senior analyst there, said public backing remains strong 76 years after the NHS's creation, but warned that "politicians and the public should not take that support for granted". She described "a generational fault line in attitudes to NHS funding".

On the fall in support for universality, Jefferies linked it to changing attitudes towards migration, while acknowledging that "it is hard to unpick exactly why" support is falling. The survey data as reported do not test that link.

On Gen Z, she pointed largely to economics: high housing costs, student debt and stagnant wages, and the experience of coming of age after austerity and the Covid-19 pandemic. She also cited frustration with long waits and with access to mental health care. The Guardian's report states there is no single explanation.

Ciarán Devane, chief executive of NHS Alliance, said it is "natural that people may question the funding model", but noted that "a large majority" of Gen Z still back the current approach. He called any erosion of support "a concern".

Why it matters for policy

Public attitudes set the limits of what governments can propose. Free-at-the-point-of-use care funded from taxation is the settlement that every major party says it defends. Evidence that the settlement is losing ground among younger adults, who will be its future taxpayers and patients, bears on any later argument about charging, insurance-style models or larger tax rises to fund the service.

The Guardian's report notes that healthcare takes up a growing share of public spending as the population ages, which may make it harder for ministers to justify larger budget increases. That argument sits alongside the wider fiscal pressures facing the Treasury, covered elsewhere in this edition, including the Chancellor's ruling out of a capital gains tax rise ahead of the Budget.

Earlier BSA results offer a useful comparison. In 2024 the survey found 90% said the NHS should definitely or probably be free at the point of use, 77% said it should be available to everyone and 80% said it should be funded from general taxation. The 2024 survey also recorded a fall in those saying the NHS should "definitely" be available to everyone, from 67% in 2023 to 56%. Satisfaction, which is a separate question from support for principles, hit a record low of 21% in 2024 before recovering to 26% in 2025, according to the King's Fund and Nuffield Trust. The authors cautioned that the rebound is not a solid signal of a turning point. Only 16% expected NHS care to improve over the next five years.

The distinction matters. Dissatisfaction with how the service performs is not the same as rejecting what it stands for, and the existing data show the two moving separately. The new analysis suggests that the second may now be softening too, though the evidence for that rests on a five-year comparison between two survey waves.

What to watch

The first test is the full King's Fund publication: the sample sizes, question wording and confidence intervals will show whether the generational gaps are statistically meaningful. The second is the next BSA release, which will show whether the 2025 readings were a step down or part of a continuing slide. The third is the Budget, where the government's treatment of NHS funding will be the first practical sign of how it reads the public mood.
