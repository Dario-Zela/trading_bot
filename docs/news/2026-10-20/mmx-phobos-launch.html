Mission launches to Mars to collect a sample from its moon

Phobos, the larger of Mars's two moons, in a colour-processed image. — ESA/DLR/FU Berlin (G. Neukum), via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Japan's MMX probe has launched toward Mars to land on Phobos, take at least 10 grams of surface material and return it to Earth around 2031.

Japan's space agency has launched a probe to Mars with an unusual destination: not the planet itself, but its larger moon, Phobos. The Martian Moons eXploration mission, MMX, lifted off on an H3 rocket from the Tanegashima Space Center on 20 October Japan time (19 October in Europe and the Americas). If it works, it will be the first spacecraft to bring back material from a moon of Mars.

The plan is to land on Phobos, collect at least 10 grams of surface material and return it to Earth. The capsule is due back in 2031. It will come down in Australia and then go to the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency's (JAXA) Institute of Space and Astronautical Science in Sagamihara.

Why a moon, not the planet

Phobos is a lumpy body about 22 km across on average. It orbits only a few thousand kilometres above the Martian surface and has almost no gravity. That is the main reason it makes a practical target.

Landing on Mars and flying a sample back off it is a hard engineering problem, because the spacecraft has to fight through an atmosphere and climb out of a deep gravity well. Phobos offers some of the same skills at far lower cost: navigating to a small body, touching down, taking a sample and making the long trip home. The mission leaves out the hardest part, the ascent from a planet.

The lander can take advantage of the weak gravity. The 25 kg rover would weigh about 15 grams there. That makes the landing gentle, but it also means loose material can drift off the surface.

The science is a real puzzle in its own right. There are two main ideas about where Phobos came from:

  • It is an asteroid captured by Mars's gravity. Its makeup would then differ from Mars.
  • It formed from debris thrown into orbit when a large object hit the young planet. Its material would then preserve conditions from early Mars.

Remote observations have not settled the question. Samples analysed in laboratories on Earth could.

The spacecraft and its instruments

MMX has three main parts: a propulsion module, an exploration module and a return module. The return capsule is about 60 cm across and has a heat shield.

The sampling gear includes two systems. A corer will drill into the surface, and a pneumatic sampler will collect loose material. The spacecraft also carries instruments to study the moon's composition from orbit. They include MEGANE, a gamma-ray and neutron spectrometer, a near-infrared spectrometer, and several cameras.

Also on board is a small rover called Idefix, built by France's space agency CNES and the German Aerospace Center, DLR. It will be released before the main craft lands. Its job is to scout the surface, with a radiometer to measure temperatures, a Raman spectrometer to identify minerals and cameras for imaging and navigation.

JAXA leads the mission. NASA, CNES, DLR and the European Space Agency are partners.

The long road ahead

The journey is slow. The probe is expected to arrive at Mars about a year after launch. Then comes roughly three years of observations around Phobos, including the sampling itself, according to ESA's mission factsheet. Several flybys of the smaller moon, Deimos, are also planned.

Published timelines differ on details. Some sources put the Phobos landing in 2027 and others in 2030. The 2031 return date is consistent across them.

The first milestone is Mars orbit insertion, expected around the middle of next year. The riskiest moment comes later, when the craft touches down on a moon with almost no gravity.
