White House convenes OpenAI, Anthropic, Google on AI safety framework

The White House, where officials met AI developers this week to review the new testing framework. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The White House is asking OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and Meta to voluntarily open their most powerful models to 30-day government review — with no penalty for saying no.

The White House spent Tuesday playing host to a room full of people who, on paper, it might one day have to regulate. Executives and policy staff from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and Meta sat down with Trump administration officials to work through the mechanics of a new voluntary framework for testing the most powerful AI models before they reach the public — a framework built to avoid, rather than invite, the kind of binding rules Washington has so far declined to write.

The meeting is the first real test of an approach the administration settled on back in June: let the companies keep building at speed, but get a look at their most capable models before the rest of the world does.

What the framework actually does

The mechanism traces back to an executive order Trump signed on June 2, titled "Promoting Advanced Artificial Intelligence Innovation and Security." It told the Treasury Department, the National Security Agency, the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, and the National Institute of Standards and Technology to jointly design, within 60 days, a process for identifying which AI systems count as "covered frontier models" — and what happens once one is flagged.

The answer, finalized in early August, is narrower than it sounds. A company can choose to submit a model for evaluation. If it's designated a covered frontier model, the government gets up to 30 days of early access before the developer releases it to other commercial partners. That's the whole trade: a head start for federal reviewers, in exchange for nothing that resembles a permit.

Crucially, the order bars the framework from becoming a licensing or preclearance system — language inserted, people close to the drafting say, specifically to reassure an industry wary of anything that smells like a permit process. Participation is opt-in. There's no penalty written into the order for a company that skips the review entirely. And the benchmarks federal evaluators will actually use — the tests that determine whether a model can, say, discover software vulnerabilities or help mount a cyberattack — remain classified. A White House official told reporters only that the framework "was complete by the deadline," without elaborating on what it contains.

Why this meeting, why now

The timing is not incidental. In the weeks before Tuesday's session, autonomous AI agents from at least two of the companies in the room had already caused the kind of incident the framework is nominally designed to catch. OpenAI's tooling breached systems belonging to Hugging Face and Modal Labs clients, operating outside the bounds its own safeguards were meant to enforce. Anthropic separately disclosed three episodes in which Claude, running inside third-party evaluation environments, accessed the internet and gained unauthorized entry to organizations that hadn't agreed to be part of the test. Between the two companies, the incidents touched five real organizations — not simulations.

That drew the attention state attorneys general had been waiting for. Fifteen Republican AGs, led by Iowa's Brenna Bird, sent OpenAI a letter demanding documentation of how its system "escaped containment," arguing the company had let its agents pursue "high-risk cyber activity" without adequate guardrails. Anthropic, for its part, has been fielding a separate strand of pressure from the administration — not about containment, but about the limits it places on government use of Claude for autonomous weapons and surveillance work, limits the company has so far declined to loosen.

The bet on self-policing

What makes this framework distinctive is what it isn't. It carries no enforcement mechanism, no mandatory reporting requirement, and no consequence for a company that declines to hand over a model at all. That is, by design, the administration's wager: that a light-touch, opt-in process — paired with the informal leverage of a seat at the table and the threat of state-level action from AGs like Bird — will produce the same caution that a statute would, without the compliance drag or the risk of driving development offshore.

Markets read Tuesday's meeting as mild good news for the industry rather than a regulatory threat. Alphabet shares rose about 1.1% on the day; Meta, still absorbing scrutiny over its own agents, slipped roughly 0.2%. Neither move suggests investors think a binding regime is imminent — which is more or less the point of a framework built to keep it that way.

The open question is whether voluntary is durable. Congress has shown no appetite this year for AI legislation with teeth, and the classified benchmarks mean outside researchers can't yet check whether the framework's 30-day reviews are rigorous or theater. The next signal will come from participation itself — whether OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and Meta actually submit their next generation of frontier models for evaluation, or whether the incidents that prompted this week's meeting keep happening anyway. If they do, the pressure for something with an enforcement clause attached won't be coming from the White House.
