Labour minister signals push for 'deeper relationship' with EU

Hamish Falconer, Minister of State for Intergovernmental and European Relations — House of Commons / Roger Harris via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A junior Foreign Office minister said Burnham's government wants a 'deeper relationship' with the EU, offering warm words but no policy detail.

The first hint of Andy Burnham's foreign policy came not from the new prime minister but from one of his junior ministers, who used a round of broadcast interviews to say the government wants a "deeper relationship" with the European Union. It was a notable phrase from a government not yet three weeks old — and one still working out how far it is willing to move from the Brexit settlement it inherited.

The minister in question is Hamish Falconer, promoted when Burnham formed his cabinet on 20 July to the post of Minister of State for Intergovernmental and European Relations, a joint Foreign Office and Cabinet Office role that makes him Britain's day-to-day contact with Brussels. He replaced Nick Thomas-Symonds, and one of his first acts was a call to Maroš Šefčovič, the European Commissioner who has led the EU side of the post-Brexit "reset" talks. Falconer's comments echoed language used by continental leaders when Burnham took office: Emmanuel Macron said he hoped for a relationship carrying "new momentum," while Ursula von der Leyen spoke of bringing the two sides "closer and closer."

Warm words, no worked-out policy

What Falconer did not do is say what a deeper relationship would actually involve. There was no mention of the customs union, the single market or freedom of movement — the three red lines Keir Starmer's government drew around the reset and which Burnham has, so far, left untouched. Nor did he attach numbers, sectors or a timetable to the sentiment. That gap between tone and substance is precisely what fuelled a separate stir the week before, when a clip of a Labour minister discussing EU relations was read by some commentators as leaving the door ajar to rejoining talk — a reading Downing Street will be keen to shut down rather than encourage.

The substantive UK-EU work, such as it is, sits elsewhere: a sanitary and phytosanitary agreement on food and agricultural standards that Brussels and London have targeted for 2027, parallel talks on linking emissions trading schemes, and a proposed youth mobility scheme still being negotiated over caps and duration. None of that moved this week. What moved was rhetoric, delivered by a minister two rungs below the Foreign Secretary, at a moment when a new government is naturally inclined to signal continuity and goodwill to its neighbours.

For now, that is the whole of the story. A shift in tone from Downing Street is worth noting after a decade of Conservative governments that treated any warmth towards Brussels as a concession, and it fits with Burnham's own long-stated instinct toward closer European ties. But instinct is not policy, and goodwill is not a trade deal. The reset's actual test comes at the UK-EU summit expected this autumn and in the 2026 review of the Trade and Cooperation Agreement, when there will be texts to read rather than adjectives to parse. Until then, "deeper relationship" is a mood, not a mechanism.
