UK 30-year gilt yields still climbing toward 1998 highs

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, London. — David Iliff / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
UK 30-year gilt yields climbed to 5.70% — the highest since 1998 — as investors price in fiscal slippage ahead of October's Budget.

Britain's longest-dated government debt is trading at prices last seen when Bill Clinton was in his first term and the Bank of England still answered to the Treasury. The yield on the 30-year gilt touched 5.70% on Tuesday, its highest since 1998, as investors continued to demand a bigger premium for lending to a government whose fiscal arithmetic won't be clarified until autumn.

In one sentence: UK 30-year gilt yields hit 5.70% — the highest since 1998 — as bond markets price in fiscal slippage weeks before the government's autumn Budget.

The move extends a selloff that has been building since Andy Burnham replaced Keir Starmer as prime minister in July and installed John Healey at the Treasury. Burnham's early promise to seek "flexibility" within the government's fiscal rules landed badly with bond investors, who read it as code for looser borrowing constraints at a moment when the UK already pays more to borrow for 30 years than any other G7 government. Healey's appointment briefly calmed nerves — sterling and gilt futures recovered on the day he was named chancellor — but the reprieve has proven partial. Long-dated yields have since ground higher, and Tuesday's print puts the 30-year comfortably above the levels that forced Liz Truss's government to reverse course in 2022.

A widening gap between short and long

What makes the move notable is the divergence it exposes. The Bank of England cut its benchmark rate to 4.00% this week in a 6–3 split vote, easing the cost of short-term borrowing even as the long end of the gilt curve pushes further into 27-year-high territory. That split is the market's own verdict on two different risks: the Monetary Policy Committee is managing a slowing economy and a disinflation path it judges intact; gilt investors at the 30-year point are pricing something the central bank can't fix with a rate cut — the government's capacity to fund itself over three decades.

Healey has tried to reassure markets with technical fixes rather than outright loosening. The Treasury has been exploring the flexibility built into Public Sector Net Financial Liabilities, the balance-sheet-style fiscal metric Rachel Reeves introduced in 2024, which counts assets such as the student loan book and infrastructure holdings against government debt. Officials estimate that framework could generate £15bn–£25bn in headroom without breaching the letter of the fiscal rules. Investors have not obviously been won over: the pitch amounts to finding more room to borrow, not less, and it arrives against a backdrop of gilt issuance still expected to run close to £300bn a year for the next three years, keeping the Debt Management Office a heavy and continuous seller into a market already demanding higher compensation for duration risk.

The Budget as the next test

Healey has set his first Budget for October 28, pledging it will be built on "fiscal discipline." That date is now the market's fixed point — six weeks in which yields could ease if the chancellor produces credible detail on spending and tax, or grind higher still if he doesn't. The precedent from the November 2025 Budget under Reeves is instructive: ten-year yields dipped only modestly around that statement and settled without a decisive re-rating, suggesting investors want substance on the deficit trajectory rather than rhetoric about discipline.

For traders, 5.70% now functions as a technical marker — a level against which duration bets, gilt-swap spreads and sterling positioning will be measured until the Budget delivers a clearer signal. A break meaningfully above it would put 1998-era territory decisively behind and open discussion of levels not tested since gilts were a niche instrument for pension funds rather than a barometer of governing competence. A retreat, by contrast, would suggest markets are willing to give Healey the benefit of the doubt going into October. Until then, the gap between what the Bank of England controls and what bond investors are pricing looks set to keep widening.
