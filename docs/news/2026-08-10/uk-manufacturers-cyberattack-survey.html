Nearly a third of UK manufacturers hit by cyberattacks in past year

Vehicle production at Jaguar Land Rover's Solihull plant — the site at the centre of the UK's costliest manufacturing cyberattack to date. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A Make UK survey finds 30% of British manufacturers were hit by a cyberattack or supply-chain breach in the past year, a year after JLR's costly shutdown.

One in three UK manufacturers has been hit by a cyberattack — either directly or through a hacked supplier — in the past twelve months, according to a survey published this week by Make UK, the manufacturers' lobby group. The finding lands almost a year after Jaguar Land Rover's production lines fell silent for weeks following a ransomware attack that rippled through roughly 5,000 supplier and logistics businesses, at an estimated cost to the UK economy of close to £1.9bn.

The 30% figure covers incidents in the past year that either targeted a manufacturer directly or arrived via a compromised link in its supply chain. Among a subset of 123 firms hit specifically through supply-chain exposure, around 30% reported customer delivery delays or output cuts, and nearly a quarter reported component shortages caused by a supplier's own outage. Only half of affected firms said they had a formal incident-response plan in place before the attack landed.

Jonathon Ellison, the National Cyber Security Centre's director of national resilience, framed the stakes plainly: "In today's landscape, no manufacturer can afford to treat cybersecurity as anything other than a business-critical priority." The NCSC has spent the past year pushing smaller manufacturers — the tier-two and tier-three suppliers that rarely have dedicated security teams — to adopt baseline defences, precisely because JLR's shutdown showed how a single breach can cascade through a production network that has no slack built in.

A moving number

How alarming 30% is depends on which survey a reader trusts. A separate poll of 500 manufacturing decision-makers, commissioned by the cybersecurity vendor ESET and published in April, put the incident rate at 78% — more than double Make UK's figure — with over half of affected firms reporting six-figure losses and three-quarters reporting at least a day of shutdown. The gap likely comes down to definitions: ESET's questions captured a broader range of incidents, including attempted intrusions and near-misses, while Make UK's figure appears to track confirmed, disruptive breaches. Either way, the direction is the same, and the spread itself is instructive — it suggests manufacturers, insurers and investors are still working from inconsistent baselines when they try to price industrial cyber risk.

That matters for equity analysts covering exposed sectors. A production stoppage of the kind JLR suffered shows up in guidance cuts and working-capital strain months after the headlines fade, yet balance-sheet disclosure of cyber exposure remains patchy across UK industrials. The uncertainty cuts both ways commercially: it is a drag on manufacturers' own risk pricing, but a tailwind for the cyber-insurance and industrial-security vendors selling into the gap.

The next real test of these numbers arrives when the government publishes the full 2025/26 Cyber Security Breaches Survey later this year, which should offer a harder-edged, statistically representative read across the wider economy — including, this time, more granular manufacturing detail than in past editions.
