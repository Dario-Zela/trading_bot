Anthropic names Tino Cuéllar as first Chief Global Affairs Officer

Tino Cuéllar, former California Supreme Court justice and Carnegie Endowment president, is now Anthropic's first Chief Global Affairs Officer. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Anthropic hired ex-judge and Carnegie Endowment president Tino Cuéllar as its first global affairs chief as it fights a Pentagon blacklisting and preps a possible IPO.

Anthropic has never had a chief lobbyist. Until this month, the company that makes the Claude chatbot ran its government relations out of a policy team reporting up through the usual channels — no single executive whose job was to sit across the table from heads of state and explain what the company was building and why it should be trusted to build it. That changed on August 4, when Anthropic named Mariano-Florentino "Tino" Cuéllar as its first Chief Global Affairs Officer, a newly created role reporting directly to Anthropic president Daniela Amodei.

The hire is not a symbolic gesture. Cuéllar is a former justice of the California Supreme Court and, until this appointment, president of the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace — one of the world's oldest foreign-policy think tanks, with researchers in 20 countries. He has sat on the President's Intelligence Advisory Board, the State Department's Foreign Affairs Policy Board, and served in the White House and federal agencies across three administrations. He was already inside the Anthropic tent, having joined the company's Long-Term Benefit Trust — the body that oversees Anthropic's mission-focused governance structure — as a trustee in January. He gave that seat up to take the operating role.

"Democracies must set the terms on which this technology advances, and there is no more consequential place to be shaping that work right now than Anthropic," Cuéllar said in the announcement. Amodei's framing was similarly institutional: "Tino has spent his career helping public institutions respond to times of change with thoughtfulness, pragmatism, and deep commitment to the common good."

A company that picked a fight with Washington

The timing makes more sense once you look at what Anthropic has spent the past six months doing. In February, President Trump directed federal agencies to "immediately cease" using Anthropic's technology after the company refused to let the Pentagon deploy Claude for autonomous lethal targeting and domestic mass surveillance. The Defense Department then designated Anthropic a "supply chain risk" — a label historically reserved for firms tied to foreign adversaries, not homegrown AI labs. Anthropic sued. In March, US District Judge Rita Lin granted a preliminary injunction, calling the designation "likely both contrary to law and arbitrary and capricious." Anthropic's own finance chief, Krishna Rao, has said the dispute could still shave multiple billions of dollars off the company's 2026 revenue if federal agencies interpret the standoff broadly.

That is an unusual position for a startup to be in: suing the US government over a national-security designation while simultaneously trying to sell that same government — and dozens of others — on adopting its products. A chief global affairs officer, drawn from the judiciary and the think-tank world rather than from industry lobbying, is the kind of hire a company makes when it wants to project institutional credibility rather than just political access.

The IPO clock

The other piece of context is money, and a lot of it. Anthropic closed a $65 billion Series H round in late May at a $965 billion post-money valuation — co-led by Altimeter Capital, Dragoneer, Greenoaks and Sequoia Capital, with strategic backing from Amazon, Micron, Samsung and SK Hynix. Run-rate revenue has crossed $47 billion. On the same day the round closed, Anthropic confidentially filed a draft Form S-1 with the Securities and Exchange Commission, a formal early step toward a public listing that could come as soon as late 2026.

Going public means a different order of scrutiny than raising private capital from Sequoia and sovereign wealth funds. SEC reviewers will want to know how Anthropic characterizes its safety research, how concentrated its investor base is, and how it discloses the Pentagon dispute, ongoing copyright litigation, and export-control exposure as material risks to shareholders. The EU's AI Act has been layering on transparency obligations through 2025 and 2026 as well, adding a second regulatory front outside the United States. None of that is optional homework for a company preparing a prospectus — it's the kind of disclosure regime that punishes vagueness.

Seen against that backdrop, Cuéllar's appointment looks less like a PR flourish and more like risk management with a title attached. A sitting judge's instinct for how institutions justify their decisions, paired with a think-tank president's fluency in multiple governments' AI policy debates, is a specific kind of credibility — one that reads differently to regulators and prospective public shareholders than a career lobbyist would.

What it signals

Anthropic frames Cuéllar's mandate expansively — engaging heads of state, civil society and community groups on how AI's benefits get distributed, not merely fighting Washington's Pentagon designation. But the sequencing is hard to ignore: a company that sued its own government in March, filed confidentially for a public listing in May, and hired its first-ever global affairs chief in August is a company that has concluded regulatory and political risk now sits at the same table as product and revenue when it comes to strategic decisions.

The next marker to watch is the S-1 itself, or whatever public version of it eventually surfaces — its risk-factors section will show, in the company's own legal language, how it weighs the Pentagon dispute, export controls and AI-specific regulation against the growth story that justified a $965 billion valuation. Cuéllar's first public tests will likely come sooner: how Anthropic's government relationships evolve as that litigation continues, and whether a former judge can do for the company's Washington standing what its lawyers have so far only partially achieved in court.
