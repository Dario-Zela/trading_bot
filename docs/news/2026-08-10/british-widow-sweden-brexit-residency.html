BEYOND THE TAPE · BREXIT · By L. Bordeaux · 2026-08-10 · 3 min read

British widow, 78, ordered to leave Sweden after 21 years

A British passport — for pre-Brexit UK residents in the EU, the document that now sits at the centre of residency disputes like Joyce Thomas's. — Stratforder / Wikimedia Commons (public domain, UK Government work)
In one sentence
A 78-year-old British widow faces removal from Sweden after 21 years because her post-Brexit residency paperwork arrived late.

Joyce Thomas moved to Sweden in 2005, married her husband Gwynne there, buried him three years ago after 55 years together, and downsized into a flat in Hammarö, on the shores of Lake Vänern, to be near her son, her grandchildren, and his grave. In November she was told she had four weeks to leave the country.

The 78-year-old retired nurse is one of roughly 2,500 British nationals ordered to leave Sweden since Brexit — about a third of every removal order issued against Britons across the entire EU-27 since the transition period ended in 2021. No other member state comes close: Sweden's Migration Agency has refused 27.5% of British residency applications, against an EU average of 3-4%. Belgium refused 605 out of 13,000 applications; Bulgaria refused five out of 16,000. Sweden refused around 4,000 of 14,200.

Thomas's case turns on a technicality with an unforgiving edge. Britons living in Sweden before the end of the Brexit transition were required to apply for residency under the Withdrawal Agreement by a set deadline. Her husband, who handled the household's paperwork through their marriage, had not filed before he fell ill; Thomas said she only learned of the requirement on a visit to the UK. When she applied late, and again through a national immigration route after her husband's death, Swedish authorities rejected both, ruling that she had not shown "reasonable grounds" for missing the deadline.

"It is unbelievable at my age to be chucked out after 21 years when you've done absolutely nothing wrong," Thomas has said, describing the situation as an unbearable limbo.

A spokesperson for Sweden's Migration Agency said simply stating unawareness of the requirement is not sufficient grounds for an exception — a standard applied consistently regardless of age or bereavement.

A pattern, not an outlier

What makes the case notable beyond its particulars is how it fits a wider Swedish pattern that has drawn repeated complaint from London. The Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office says it "regularly raises concerns" about Sweden's approach to citizens' rights with both Swedish authorities and the European Commission, and Peter Kyle, the Labour MP for Brighton whose constituency includes one of Thomas's sons, called her treatment "deeply concerning," urging Swedish authorities to weigh her long residence and family ties with "fairness and compassion."

The Brexit withdrawal agreement was meant to grandfather in residents like Thomas, who arrived nearly two decades before the referendum. In practice, it left the mechanics of proving that status to national bureaucracies, and Sweden's has proven the least forgiving of missed paperwork of any in the bloc.

Thomas is now pursuing an appeal to a higher Swedish court with pro bono legal help. The outcome will matter well beyond Hammarö: with thousands of similar cases still working through Sweden's system, her appeal is likely to be read as a test of how much room, if any, Swedish courts will allow for age, bereavement and simple oversight in a regime built around fixed deadlines.
