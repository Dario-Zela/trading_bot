Meta ordered to pay $567m more in New Mexico child-safety case

Meta's headquarters in Menlo Park, California, where the company has said it will appeal the New Mexico ruling. — LPS.1 / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
A New Mexico judge fined Meta $567m and, for the first time in this case, ordered usage caps and notification curfews for under-18 users.

Meta has spent three years paying fines for how its apps affect children. Starting this week, in one state at least, it also has to change how those apps work.

First Judicial District Judge Bryan Biedscheid in Santa Fe ordered Meta to pay $567m into a fund for treating New Mexico children harmed by Facebook and Instagram — on top of the $375m a jury awarded the state in March. That brings the company's tally in this single case to roughly $942m. But the bigger news isn't the number. It's what comes with it: for the first time in this line of litigation, a court has told Meta how to run its products for anyone under 18, not just how much to pay for having run them badly.

What the order actually requires

The mandates are specific. Meta must cap usage for New Mexico users under 18 at 90 hours a month across Facebook and Instagram — about three hours a day. Push notifications to those accounts go dark from 10pm to 7am every night, and, during the school year, from 8am to 3pm on weekdays. "Like" counts disappear from minors' accounts unless a parent or guardian switches them back on. The platforms are barred from recommending any account belonging to a under-18 user to strangers, and adults with no existing connection to a minor can no longer message them. Meta also has to run daily safety banners and fund a public education campaign in the state on parental controls and reporting tools.

Judge Biedscheid's written findings, following on from a jury verdict in March that found Meta violated New Mexico's Unfair Practices Act, describe a business model rather than a defect. "Meta's platforms create a public nuisance because their purpose and effect is to optimize engagement, including in ways that are detrimental to teenagers' health and safety," he wrote, pointing to endless scroll, autoplay and recommendation algorithms as the specific mechanisms.

From damages to design

The case traces back to a 2023 lawsuit by New Mexico attorney general Raúl Torrez, built partly on an undercover operation in which investigators set up a fake profile for a 13-year-old girl and found it, in Torrez's telling, "inundated with images and targeted solicitations" from adults within days. That evidence anchored claims that Meta not only failed to protect minors from predators but understated the risk publicly while its own research flagged it internally — the same substance behind the Unfair Practices Act verdict in March.

What changed between March and now is the remedy. Civil penalties are a cost of doing business that a company the size of Meta — $164bn in revenue last year — can absorb without altering a single product screen. Usage caps and curfews are different: they cut directly against the metric Meta's advertising business depends on, engagement time. A three-hour daily ceiling and an eight-hour nightly blackout window, if they hold, would show up in New Mexico teens' usage data almost immediately.

The catch, and what's next

The order applies only to Meta accounts held by New Mexico residents under 18, and it does not touch the underlying design — infinite scroll and autoplay stay in place for everyone else, including adults in New Mexico. The judgment sets no implementation deadline, and Meta has said it will appeal. Spokesperson Andy Stone said the company remains "confident in our record of protecting teens online" and will keep contesting claims it says "misrepresent the facts."

Still, advocates are treating the ruling as a template rather than a one-off. "Now we will have someone in an official capacity checking the homework of these companies," said Brooke Istook of the Heat Initiative, one of the groups that pushed for the case. Similar suits are pending in California and Tennessee, and a Los Angeles court handed down a related judgment against Meta in March. Whether Biedscheid's specific mechanics — the 90-hour cap, the curfew windows — get echoed elsewhere will depend on two things: how Meta's appeal fares in New Mexico's courts, and whether other state attorneys general decide operational mandates, not fines, are now the more effective lever.
