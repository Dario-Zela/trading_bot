Trump's 50% Canada tariffs set to take effect August 19

Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney, who says Ottawa has not ruled out retaliation, addressing the World Economic Forum in January 2026. — World Economic Forum, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
A 50% US tariff on roughly $16-20bn of Canadian dairy, alcohol, furniture and auto imports takes effect August 19 under a never-before-used trade law.

Canadian exporters have nine days left before Washington's steepest tariff yet lands on their goods. On July 20, President Trump signed three proclamations imposing an additional 50% duty on a swathe of Canadian imports — dairy, alcoholic beverages, furniture and a long tail of agricultural and manufactured goods — set to take effect at 12:01 a.m. Eastern on August 19. The Tax Foundation puts the affected trade at roughly $16 billion; the U.S. Trade Representative's office, counting motor vehicles separately targeted in the same batch of proclamations, puts total exposure closer to $20 billion.

Either figure marks a step up in scale from the tariff actions of the past eighteen months. This is also a first: the White House invoked Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930, a Depression-era provision letting the president impose duties up to 50% on a country found to discriminate against U.S. commerce. No administration had used it before.

The case Washington is making

The administration's justification is narrower than the tariff's reach. Officials cite three specific grievances — Canadian quotas and tariffs on U.S. autos not applied to other trading partners, restricted provincial distribution of American alcohol, and dairy quotas that favour EU cheese imports over U.S. product. The USTR fact sheet cites a 22% drop in U.S. motor-vehicle exports to Canada (down $5.6 billion) between April 2025 and March 2026, and an 81% collapse in U.S. alcoholic-beverage exports (down $582 million) over a comparable window, as evidence of the imbalance.

The tariff lines themselves run wider than the headline categories suggest: 439 eight-digit lines for motor vehicles, 52 for dairy, 63 for alcoholic beverages, plus scattered entries covering hockey sticks, cement, plywood and swimming pools. Energy, potash, fish and critical minerals — goods Washington still needs — are carved out. Section 338 duties, unlike most of this administration's earlier tariff actions, carry no expiry date and apply even to goods that qualify under the USMCA.

Ottawa's calculus

Prime Minister Mark Carney has kept his options deliberately vague. "Everything is on the table depending on the outcome of the negotiations," he told reporters, declining to specify countermeasures on the grounds that signalling retaliation mid-negotiation would be counterproductive. Provincial premiers are less circumspect: Ontario's Doug Ford has pushed for withholding potash and oil exports outright, a step Ottawa has so far resisted. Trump's July 1 decision not to extend the USMCA for a further sixteen years — leaving it in force but subject to annual review rather than locked in — has raised the stakes on whatever comes out of the current talks track, which Washington has said is running behind its parallel negotiations with Mexico.

What it prices in

For markets, the deadline is unusually clean as these things go: a specific rate, a specific date, a specific list of tariff lines. Dairy processors and cheese importers, wine and spirits distributors, and furniture retailers with Canadian supply chains face a straightforward repricing problem — a 50% cost step-up on the affected share of goods entering the U.S. after August 19, with no transition period built in. Consumer-facing effects will lag; the more immediate hit falls on Canadian exporters, provincial revenue and the Canadian dollar, with the pass-through to U.S. shelf prices following as importers either absorb margin compression or push costs downstream.

The next marker is August 19 itself. Absent a negotiated carve-out before then, the tariffs take effect automatically — no further White House action required. Whether Ottawa responds with tariffs of its own, and on what, is the open variable traders are watching for in the interim.
