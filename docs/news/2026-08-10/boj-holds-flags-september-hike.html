Bank of Japan holds rates but flags possible September hike

Bank of Japan Governor Kazuo Ueda, who told reporters the bank would debate policy "from our next meeting onward" with upside inflation risks in mind. — Cabinet Public Relations Office, Government of Japan / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
The BoJ held rates at 1.0% in an 8-1 vote but flagged a possible September hike after warning inflation could overshoot its 2% target.

Japan's central bank left its benchmark interest rate unchanged at 1.0% on 31 July, but the vote and the language around it told a different story than the headline number. The Bank of Japan's nine-member board backed the hold 8-1, with Hajime Takata — the board's most consistently hawkish voice — dissenting in favour of an immediate quarter-point rise to 1.25%. Governor Kazuo Ueda then used his press conference to point directly at the bank's next meeting in September as the moment policy could move.

"Given underlying inflation is approaching our 2% target, we must be mindful of upside price risks more than ever," Ueda told reporters. "We will debate our policy from our next meeting onward with this point in mind."

A warning the BoJ hadn't given before

What moved markets was not the hold itself — economists had widely expected the bank to pause after raising rates to the current 1.0%, a 31-year high, only a month earlier. It was the inflation language. The BoJ said for the first time that underlying, or core, inflation was likely to run "clearly above" its 2% target from the second half of the current fiscal year, citing wage rises being passed through into selling prices, firmer crude oil costs and the yen's continued weakness. That is a materially more urgent framing than the bank's prior line, which treated a target overshoot as a risk rather than a likely outcome.

Takata's dissent reinforced the point. His argument, as summarised in the meeting record, was that the economy had entered "a new phase" requiring a nimbler policy response to demand-driven price pressure — language that reads as a direct challenge to the majority's preference for gradualism.

The yen problem in the background

The hawkish tilt did not emerge in isolation. Days before the meeting, Tokyo was suspected of intervening in New York trading to buy yen, after the currency had touched roughly ¥164 to the dollar — its weakest level since 1986 — before retreating to around ¥157.60. A yen that cheap has been pushing up import costs for households and retailers, and the political pressure to address it has been building for months. Slow, telegraphed rate rises have been blamed for letting the currency slide that far in the first place; the BoJ's sharper inflation language looks partly like an attempt to close that gap.

That matters well beyond Tokyo. An estimated $500 billion in yen-funded carry trades — positions built by borrowing cheap yen to buy higher-yielding assets abroad — remain outstanding, according to Morgan Stanley estimates. A September hike, if it lands as the market now expects, would raise the cost of funding those trades and could force a fresh round of unwinding, the same dynamic that briefly crashed risk assets in August 2024 after the BoJ's last surprise move.

What to watch

Most economists polled by Reuters now expect one more 25 basis-point increase to 1.25% before the end of the year, with the BoJ's 17-18 September meeting the leading candidate. The bank's quarterly outlook report, due at that same meeting, will carry updated inflation forecasts and is likely to be the clearest signal yet of whether Takata's dissent was an early call or a lone one.
