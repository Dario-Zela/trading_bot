Israel rejects Trump's Gaza plan as strikes continue

Trump and Netanyahu in the Oval Office, July 28, 2026 — eleven days before Netanyahu publicly rejected the Gaza plan the two men had discussed. — Daniel Torok / The White House
In one sentence
Netanyahu declared "Israel does not accept" Trump's 15-point Gaza plan as weekend strikes killed 26, unravelling the touted disarmament deal within days.

Benjamin Netanyahu stood before Israel's Sunday cabinet meeting and said, twice, what much of the region had already suspected: Israel is not going along with Donald Trump's Gaza plan. "I wish to be precise: Israel does not accept the 15-point document," he said, then repeated it for anyone inclined to doubt him. Within hours, Israeli strikes across Gaza had added to a weekend death toll that Al Jazeera put at 26 — a grim rebuttal to the White House's characterisation, just ten days earlier, of a "historic" breakthrough.

The gap between those two facts — a disarmament deal Trump called monumental, and a battlefield that never stopped producing bodies — is the story of Gaza in August 2026. It is also, for anyone pricing Middle East risk, the reason the ceasefire that briefly calmed oil markets can no longer be taken as settled.

"Genuine disarmament, not fictitious"

Trump's 15-point framework, drafted through his self-styled Board of Peace, sequences the endgame in Gaza in stages: Hamas surrenders its heavy weapons, production sites, depots and tunnel network first; Israeli forces then draw down in phases; administration of the strip eventually passes to a Palestinian technocratic committee under the Board's oversight. Hamas signed on to the broad terms in late July, and Trump presented the accord as the culmination of nearly two years of diplomacy.

Netanyahu's objection is about order, not just substance. "The IDF will not carry out any withdrawal until Hamas is disarmed," he told ministers, adding that Israel wants "genuine disarmament, not a fictitious one" — every weapon, heavy and light, before troops move. Hamas's position is the mirror image: it says it will give up its heavier armaments, but only after a full Israeli withdrawal and an end to hostilities, and it has linked the final stage of disarmament to progress toward a Palestinian state — the one outcome Netanyahu has ruled out entirely, telling his cabinet "no Palestinian state will arise" on his watch. Between those two sequencing demands sits the entire mechanism Trump spent political capital building.

The killing did not pause for the deal

What makes the rejection more than a rhetorical spat is that it was never accompanied by a pause in fighting. Al Jazeera's tally shows Israeli strikes killed at least 19 Palestinians on a single day the weekend before Netanyahu's cabinet remarks, part of a 26-death toll across Saturday and Sunday that hit Gaza City, Deir al-Balah and Khan Younis. Separate strikes days earlier destroyed two of the four medicine warehouses serving Al-Aqsa Martyrs Hospital. All of this occurred after Hamas and other factions had already signed the disarmament agreement Washington was calling a breakthrough.

The pattern — deal announced, strikes continue — has repeated often enough across this war that it now reads less as an aberration than as the operating rhythm of the ceasefire process itself. Israeli forces have, per US officials, been gradually pulling back toward the "Yellow Line," the provisional boundary drawn under the original Trump ceasefire plan that currently leaves roughly half of Gaza under direct Israeli control. That partial withdrawal is real. It has just not stopped the strikes, nor resolved who disarms first.

Washington's calculated shrug

What is notable is how little alarm the rejection has produced in Washington. A senior US official told reporters the administration has "no issue with the rhetoric as long as Netanyahu keeps restraining attacks on Gaza as agreed," and framed the public defiance as domestic theatre — "part of Israel's election season" — rather than a genuine break. The same official said Netanyahu had privately assured Trump's envoy, Jared Kushner, that he would give the plan real consideration despite the public posture. The White House itself offered no formal comment on Netanyahu's cabinet remarks.

That restraint reads as a deliberate choice to avoid forcing a rupture with an ally mid-negotiation, not as evidence the framework is secure. Analysts describe the situation as a "managed standoff rather than a real rupture" — for now. The distinction matters because it is precisely the kind of ambiguity markets dislike: enough friction to signal the deal is not self-executing, not enough clarity to say whether it collapses or limps forward.

Why the risk premium isn't going anywhere

Gaza is not the only flashpoint keeping the region unsettled. Houthi forces in Yemen claimed a drone strike on Saudi Aramco's Jazan refinery on August 9th, igniting a fire at the 400,000-barrel-a-day facility before it was extinguished; the attack landed the same weekend Netanyahu's rejection became public, and came as talks over reopening the Strait of Hormuz to shipping remained unresolved. Brent crude, which spent July climbing from $72 to above $100 a barrel before easing back toward $84 as the Gaza ceasefire's first phase took hold, is a fair proxy for how tightly these threads are wound together: withdraw the diplomatic momentum, and the premium has room to climb back.

None of this means renewed full-scale war is imminent — Israeli forces are still retreating toward the Yellow Line, and Washington is still, publicly at least, treating the disarmament deal as alive. But the sequencing dispute at the heart of Netanyahu's rejection is not cosmetic; it is the actual mechanism by which the plan is supposed to end the war, and it remains unresolved. The next marker to watch is whether the technocratic transition Trump's plan envisions for Gaza's administration gets any traction, or whether the Board of Peace remains, as one Al Jazeera analysis put it, an institution with limited power absent Israeli cooperation. Until that changes, the strip stays a live front, and the region's energy markets will keep pricing it as one.
