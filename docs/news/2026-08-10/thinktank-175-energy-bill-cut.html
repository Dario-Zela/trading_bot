Thinktank urges £175 energy bill cut for low-income households

A domestic prepayment energy meter — the payment method used by roughly four million UK households, disproportionately lower-income. — Taviso / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
The Resolution Foundation wants a £2bn, £175-a-year targeted energy discount ready by January, but the government hasn't committed to it.

The Resolution Foundation wants ministers to have a targeted energy discount ready to switch on by January — worth £175 a year to low-income households — even as the government insists its existing support is enough for now.

The thinktank's report, published Monday and titled Billing me softly: How to design targeted energy discounts, lands as Ofgem's price cap sits at £1,862 for a typical dual-fuel household on Direct Debit, up 13% since the spring after wholesale gas prices roughly doubled following the escalation of the Middle East conflict. Authors Mike Brewer, Alex Clegg and Jonathan Marshall warn the cap could jump again when it resets in January, and argue the government currently has no mechanism to shield poorer households from that.

The proposal

The headline option is a flat £175 annual discount for households where the highest earner takes home £24,000 or less, funded through a £2 billion scheme and reaching roughly 40% of households — including more than three-quarters of those in the four poorest income deciles. A more targeted, harder-to-administer alternative would tier support: around £220 for households with a top earner below £18,000, tapering to £85 for those between £18,000 and £24,000. Eligibility would combine an income test with passporting via receipt of means-tested benefits, assessed over the three months before the scheme launches.

"The government can get ahead of this, if it acts now," said Jonathan Marshall, principal economist at the Resolution Foundation. "A targeted energy discount could save eligible households £175 a year."

No policy movement, yet

Prime Minister Andy Burnham has already moved on bills once this year, cutting VAT on domestic electricity from October 1 — worth about £45 to a typical household — funded by scrapping the £1.8 billion digital ID programme. The Resolution Foundation's chief executive, Ruth Curtice, has been openly critical of that approach, arguing it is poorly targeted: "With bills still set to rise over £150 this October, this money would have been better spent on more targeted support."

So far, Downing Street has made no commitment to the £175 scheme or anything resembling it. That leaves the report as advocacy rather than policy in waiting — a marker laid down ahead of the autumn Budget and the January price cap reset, not a plan the Treasury has signed off. The Resolution Foundation says the design work, at least, should be finished well before winter bites; whether the money follows is a decision for the chancellor, not the thinktank.

The next concrete data point is Ofgem's Q1 2027 price cap announcement, expected in late November, which will show whether the winter jump the Resolution Foundation is warning about actually materialises.
