Ukrainian drones strike Russian refineries and shadow-fleet tankers

The Syzran oil refinery in Russia's Samara region, struck by Ukrainian drones on August 8, 2026 — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Ukraine struck refineries at Syzran and Ilsky and about a dozen shadow-fleet tankers, deepening a campaign that has disabled over 40% of Russian refining capacity.

The Ukrainian drones came in low over the Volga in the small hours of August 8, and within an hour the Syzran refinery — a Rosneft plant 750km southeast of Moscow that processes nearly 9 million tonnes of crude a year — was on fire. Local residents counted roughly thirty explosions between 2:41am and 3:45am; NASA's FIRMS satellite system logged the blaze at 3:07am. Sixteen hundred kilometres to the south, drone debris set the Ilsky refinery in Krasnodar region alight the same night, hitting one of southern Russia's largest privately-owned processing plants. Ukraine's General Staff said both facilities took direct hits on their primary distillation units.

The same week, Ukrainian naval and aerial drones struck about a dozen more vessels from Russia's sanctioned "shadow fleet" in the Black Sea and Sea of Azov — tankers that exist largely to move Russian crude around a Western price cap and export ban. Taken together, the two strands of the campaign mark a shift in what Kyiv is trying to break: not just the pipes and refineries that make fuel, but the ships that carry it to market.

From the grid to the export chain

For much of the war, Ukraine's long-range strikes concentrated on Russian power generation — substations, transformers, the grid that keeps factories and, not incidentally, air defence radars running. Over the past year that focus has moved decisively toward oil. Ukraine's Air Force says Russia has lost 42.7% of its refining capacity over the past year, at a cost it puts at $13.5bn in infrastructure damage. Refinery runs fell to about 3.8 million barrels a day in July, roughly 1.6 million bpd below the same month last year, according to the Kyiv School of Economics' Russian Oil Tracker. Omsk, Russia's largest refinery, went offline after a July 6 strike; Saratov followed a day later.

The domestic fallout has been substantial. Roughly 50 million Russians — about 35% of the population — have faced fuel restrictions this summer, with shortages reported in 78 of the country's 83 federal regions. Crimea and Sevastopol declared local states of emergency on June 26. Moscow has responded by banning exports of gasoline, diesel and jet fuel at various points since the spring, permitting refineries to drop fuel-quality standards from Euro-5 to Euro-3 through December, and rationing pump sales by licence plate in six regions.

The tanker war

The naval side of the campaign has its own name: Operation MoLoChKa, launched July 6 by Ukraine's Unmanned Systems Forces under commander Robert "Magyar" Brovdi. The acronym stands for "Moscow will fall through Crimea" — a statement of intent to cut the peninsula off from Russian supply by sea rather than take it by land. By early August the force said it had struck 218 shadow-fleet vessels since the operation began, 134 in the Sea of Azov and 84 in the Black Sea; the dozen hit this past week, claimed by Magyar's Birds Brigade and the K-2 Brigade, are the latest instalment. None of the figures have been independently verified, and Russia has not confirmed them.

The weapon of choice is the Sea Baby, an unmanned surface vessel developed by Ukraine's security service that has grown from a 1,000km range and modest warhead in 2023 to variants carrying up to 2,000kg of explosives out to 1,500km, alongside the underwater Sub Sea Baby and one-way FP-1/FP-2 attack drones. Earlier strikes this year hit tankers with names like Kairos, Virat, Louise 1 and Banda — vessels sanctioned by the EU, UK, Canada and Australia for moving Russian oil in defiance of the price cap. Globally, an estimated 1,140 such tankers, most elderly and irregularly insured, made up more than 18% of the world's oil-tanker fleet as of last August; by late July, sanctions authorities had designated 681 of them by name.

Squeezing the money

The two campaigns reinforce each other. Refinery strikes push Russia to export more unrefined crude rather than higher-value fuel, but that crude has to move on a shadow fleet that Ukraine is simultaneously trying to sink, damage or scare into idleness — about 180 loaded shadow tankers left Russian ports in June, KSE estimates, some 94% of them over fifteen years old. Russia's oil-export revenue fell roughly $5bn between May and June, to $15.8bn, and July's diesel and gasoil export ban puts up to 36% of remaining refined-product exports at risk, KSE analysts calculate. Independent oil-and-gas analyst Boris Aronstein has called the resulting squeeze "the most severe crisis in recent years," arguing that the scale and repetition of the strikes leave Russian operators unable to finish repairs before the next wave arrives.

What to watch

Russia's options are narrowing rather than disappearing — it can still reroute crude, lean harder on Chinese and Indian buyers, and draw down the fuel-quality standards further before winter driving season bites. The next data points worth watching are the Kyiv School of Economics' monthly Oil Tracker, due in early September, and whether Ukraine's claimed shadow-fleet tally holds up against ship-tracking data from analysts like Windward and Kpler. For markets already pricing geopolitical risk into Brent, the question is less whether Ukraine can keep striking — it plainly can — than whether Russia's sanctions-evasion architecture, built over three years, can absorb sustained damage to both ends of the supply chain at once.
