Burnham fast-tracks ban on 'subscription trap' practices

Prime Minister Andy Burnham's official portrait, taken at 10 Downing Street. — Alecsandra Dragoi / No 10 Downing Street (OGL v3.0)
In one sentence
PM Burnham is fast-tracking a UK ban on hard-to-cancel subscriptions to January 2027, part of a wider cost-of-living package.

Andy Burnham used his first big domestic policy push as prime minister to take aim at a target that polls well with almost nobody outside corporate finance departments: the subscription that is easy to start and maddeningly hard to stop.

Speaking as he kicked off a cost-of-living tour that begins in Port Talbot, Burnham said his government would fast-track consumer protection rules originally due next spring, bringing them into force in January 2027 instead. "People are sick and tired of rip-off discounts and subscription traps," he said, promising to make it "as easy to leave a subscription as it is to join."

The numbers behind the announcement are the most concrete thing in it. Officials estimate British consumers spend roughly £1.6 billion a year on subscriptions they no longer want but haven't got round to cancelling — streaming services nobody watches, gym memberships gathering dust, software licences renewed on autopilot. That figure, more than any regulatory detail, is doing the political work here: it reframes subscription fatigue as a hidden tax that fits neatly alongside the energy bill and bus fare measures Burnham unveiled in the same package.

What the rules actually require

The substance draws on subscription-contract provisions already written into the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024, which had been drifting toward an autumn 2026 start before Thursday's announcement pulled the timeline forward. Under the accelerated rules, businesses will have to give customers clearer upfront pricing information, send regular reminders before a contract renews, and build an exit process no harder to navigate than the sign-up flow. A new 14-day cooling-off window will let customers cancel after a free trial converts to a paid plan, or after a long-term contract auto-renews.

Separately, Burnham is opening a consultation on adding deceptive discounting — inflating a list price only to immediately mark it down — to the Competition and Markets Authority's roster of banned practices. That matters for enforcement teeth: it would let the CMA use powers under the same 2024 Act that already allow fines of up to 10% of global turnover for breaches, alongside "redress orders" that can force a company to contact every affected customer and offer a full refund.

Incrementalism, not a surprise

None of this is a change in direction so much as a change in pace. The DMCCA passed under the previous government in 2024; Thursday's announcement moves its subscription provisions up by a few months and adds a discounting consultation on top. No specific companies were named, and the government has not detailed transition costs or a compliance grace period for smaller subscription businesses — details that will matter more to streaming, SaaS and e-commerce operators than the headline itself.

For now the practical impact is limited to compliance planning: subscription-heavy firms have until January to rebuild renewal notices, cancellation flows and trial-conversion messaging, or face a regulator with meaningfully sharper teeth than it had a week ago.

The next marker is the CMA consultation on discount practices, which will set out how far the banned-practices list expands and give the clearest signal yet of how aggressively the fast-tracked regime will be policed once it takes effect.
