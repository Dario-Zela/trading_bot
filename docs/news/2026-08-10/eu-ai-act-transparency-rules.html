EU AI Act transparency obligations take effect

The European Commission's offices in Brussels, where the AI Office oversees implementation of the bloc's AI Act. — Satu Katja / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
The EU's AI transparency law took effect August 2 with €15m fines on paper, but many national regulators aren't yet staffed to enforce it.

From Sunday, any chatbot, virtual assistant or AI-generated video shown to someone in the European Union is legally required to say so. Article 50 of the EU's AI Act — the bloc's sprawling 2024 law governing artificial intelligence — became enforceable on 2 August, and it applies to a strikingly wide cast: not just OpenAI and Google, but freelance video editors, podcasters, advertising agencies and any company anywhere in the world whose AI product reaches an EU user.

The rule itself is simple enough to state. Providers of systems that interact directly with people — chatbots, voice assistants, AI avatars — must make clear, at the start of the conversation, that the other party is a machine, unless that's already obvious. Deepfakes and AI-manipulated audio, video and images need machine-readable marks so platforms can detect them. AI-generated text on matters of public interest has to be labelled unless a human has substantially edited it. Miss any of it, and the penalty ceiling is €15 million or 3% of global annual turnover, whichever is higher — the same scale of fine that has made GDPR a boardroom concern for a decade.

Who actually enforces this?

Here the picture gets murkier. Responsibility for policing Article 50 falls mainly to national market surveillance authorities, one per member state, with the EU's AI Office handling only systems built on general-purpose models and the European Data Protection Supervisor covering EU institutions themselves. The trouble is that a number of member states were still scrambling to designate those national authorities as the deadline arrived — more than a dozen missed an earlier appointment deadline, and by late last year most had not named a single point of contact. A rule with teeth on paper needs a regulator with a phone number to actually bite, and in several capitals that regulator barely exists yet.

The law's own language does the rest of the work in blunting it. Disclosure is only required if AI use isn't already "obvious" to a reasonably attentive person — a threshold vague enough that critics have described it as one a truck could drive through. And the obligation to mark synthetic content with technical watermarks, arguably the toughest part to engineer, has a grace period running to 2 December for anything already on the market.

A rule going live as the wider law loosens

The timing is awkward for anyone reading this as a sign of Brussels toughening up. Weeks before Article 50 kicked in, the EU finalised a "Digital Omnibus" package — drafted under sustained lobbying from Meta, Google and Amazon, and pressure from Washington over tariffs — that pushed back the AI Act's tougher high-risk system rules to December 2027 and August 2028. Article 50 survived that rollback intact, but it now takes effect into a regulatory climate visibly retreating from confrontation with big tech, not advancing on it.

That leaves the practical test still to come: not whether the rule exists, but whether a regulator anywhere in the EU actually uses it. The first fine, or the first high-profile case of a company simply ignoring the disclosure requirement without consequence, will tell compliance teams — and everyone else — what Article 50 is really worth.
