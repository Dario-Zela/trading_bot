Peter Murrell jailed over SNP embezzlement

Nicola Sturgeon with her husband Peter Murrell in 2014, before he stepped down as SNP chief executive and was later charged with embezzling party funds. — Scottish Government / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Peter Murrell, Nicola Sturgeon's estranged husband and the SNP's former chief executive, is serving five years and three months for embezzling £400,000 from the party.

Peter Murrell, the man who ran the Scottish National Party's machinery for more than two decades, will spend the coming years in prison rather than at its helm. Edinburgh's High Court sentenced the former chief executive to five years and three months on June 23 after he admitted embezzling £400,310.65 from the party over a twelve-year span — a sum first reported as high as £459,000 during the police investigation that eventually forced him from office.

The case has resurfaced this week not because of new legal developments but because of who is now talking about it. Nicola Sturgeon, Murrell's estranged wife and Scotland's former First Minister, told broadcaster Iain Dale at an Edinburgh Fringe podcast recording that she has not spoken to Murrell since his sentencing and does not intend to. "What a mug," she said, describing how she felt on learning he had been quietly negotiating a guilty plea for months while continuing to insist on his innocence to her. She will not be visiting him in prison.

A slow-motion scandal reaches its verdict

Murrell's embezzlement did not happen overnight, and neither did its unraveling. Police Scotland's Operation Branchform began in July 2021, examining what had happened to roughly £666,953 raised specifically to fund a second independence referendum campaign. Murrell was first arrested in April 2023, days after resigning as chief executive over an unrelated row about inflated party membership figures. He was arrested again the following year and finally charged with embezzlement in April 2024.

The charge sheet, once it emerged, read less like political intrigue and more like a shopping list: a £124,550 motorhome bought in 2020, a £57,500 Jaguar I-Pace, a £33,000 Volkswagen Golf, two Bremont watches worth £9,350, a £4,225 fountain pen, a silver wine coaster costing £3,500, plus assorted cosmetics and jewellery. Murrell pleaded guilty on May 25 this year and was remanded in custody immediately; his sentence was backdated to that date.

Passing sentence, Lord Young told Murrell he had committed a "calculated crime of dishonesty" involving a "large number of fraudulent acts" that grew in frequency and value the longer he went undetected. Reporting on the sentencing remarks noted the judge's evident scepticism that a scheme run through the party's own accounts, by its most senior administrator, over more than a decade, could go unnoticed by everyone around him — a note that has kept pressure on SNP leadership over who else knew what, and when.

A party still answering for it

Current SNP leader and First Minister John Swinney called the episode "agony" for the party, saying the sentence "recognises the severity and seriousness of the offences that have been committed against the Scottish National Party" and expressing hope it would offer "some closure for members who will feel deeply let down by the betrayal of trust." Police concluded their broader investigation in March 2025 without charging Sturgeon or the party's former treasurer, Colin Beattie, but the absence of charges has not been enough to close the question of institutional oversight — how a chief executive married to the sitting First Minister spent more than £400,000 of members' donations on himself without the party's internal controls catching it.

That is the harder legacy of the Murrell case for the SNP: not the criminal file, which closes with his imprisonment, but the governance file, which does not. A party that spent years insisting it had robust financial safeguards must now explain, to members who funded an independence campaign that never happened, how those safeguards failed for over a decade.

What's next

Murrell's earliest release, under standard Scottish sentencing rules, would fall roughly halfway through his term — likely sometime in 2028 — barring any parole complications. Sturgeon's memoir, Frankly, and her continuing round of public appearances are likely to keep drawing the marriage and its aftermath back into view, even as she insists her own conduct was separately and fully cleared by investigators. For the SNP, the more consequential date is whatever internal review, if any, the party commits to publishing on how its financial controls missed the fraud for twelve years — a report members have been promised but have not yet seen.
