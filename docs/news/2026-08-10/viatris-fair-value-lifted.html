Analysts lift Viatris fair-value estimates on pipeline progress

In one sentence
Barclays lifted its Viatris target to $22 on cost cuts and 2026 pipeline catalysts, but the stock fell after earnings and rivals value it lower.

Viatris shares slipped after last week's earnings, but at least one analyst on Wall Street thinks the market has it backwards. Barclays raised its price target on the generics and specialty-pharma maker to $22 from $17 on Friday, keeping an Overweight rating — a target that sits more than 25% above where the stock has traded since reporting second-quarter results.

The stock's reaction told a different story than the headline numbers. Viatris beat estimates on August 6, posting adjusted earnings of $0.69 a share on revenue of $3.8 billion, ahead of consensus forecasts of $0.61 and $3.67 billion. Management raised the midpoint of full-year guidance across the board — revenue to $14.75 billion, adjusted EBITDA to $4.40 billion, adjusted EPS to $2.52. Shares fell anyway, down more than 3% to $17.07, as investors focused on a roughly $150 million supply disruption and competitive pressure flagged for the second half.

Barclays analyst Glen Santangelo is betting the guidance raise matters more than the near-term noise. His note pointed to Viatris's cost-cutting program — the company is targeting roughly $400 million in savings by the end of 2028 through an enterprise-wide strategic review — alongside two regulatory catalysts due this year. UBS moved in the same direction, lifting its target to $20 from $18 and citing the same cost discipline. JPMorgan was more measured, raising its target to just $18 while keeping a Neutral rating.

Catalysts that are already largely known

The two pipeline events cited by bulls are not surprises. The FDA approved Viatris's once-weekly contraceptive patch, Gwyn Lo, on July 29; commercial launch is expected later this year. The other catalyst, a fast-acting, non-opioid formulation of meloxicam for acute pain (MR-107A-02), has an FDA decision date of December 27 after the agency accepted the application in May. Phase 3 data showed the drug reduced pain and cut opioid use compared with older formulations, and analysts at UBS and Truist have argued it could win "opioid-sparing" language on its label that positions it above older generic meloxicam. Whether that translates into meaningful share in a crowded acute-pain market — one already served by NSAIDs, opioids and newer non-opioid entrants like Journavx — remains the open question neither approval nor a PDUFA date answers on its own.

That gap between a well-telegraphed catalyst and an unproven commercial edge is roughly where the market's own skepticism sits. Simply Wall St's algorithmic fair-value estimate for Viatris has ticked up only modestly this year, to roughly $18.50 from $17.94, a fraction of the distance Barclays has now traveled. The spread between a $22 sell-side target and an $18.50 model-driven estimate is not unusual for a stock coming off a strong twelve-month run — Viatris shares have roughly doubled over that period — but it does suggest the bullish case rests more on execution than on any single catalyst surprising to the upside.

The next test arrives December 27, when the FDA rules on fast-acting meloxicam. Gwyn Lo's commercial rollout later this year will offer an earlier read on whether newly approved products can move the revenue needle at a company still leaning heavily on cost cuts to hit its targets.
