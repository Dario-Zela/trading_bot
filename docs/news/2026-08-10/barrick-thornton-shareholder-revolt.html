Barrick investors revolt against chairman Thornton's asset-split plan

NASA satellite image of Barrick's Goldstrike mine complex in Nevada, part of the Nevada Gold Mines joint venture Thornton wants to spin off. — NASA Earth Observatory / Jesse Allen (public domain)
In one sentence
Van Eck, Mackenzie and Franklin Equity are publicly fighting Barrick chairman John Thornton's plan to spin off its North American gold mines, with one investor calling for his resignation.

Barrick Mining's chairman wants to carve up the company. A growing bloc of his largest shareholders wants to carve him out first.

John Thornton, the 72-year-old former Goldman Sachs co-president who has chaired Barrick for 12 years, is pushing ahead with a plan to spin off the company's North American gold assets — including its dominant joint-venture stake in Nevada, the Fourmile discovery, and the Pueblo Viejo mine in the Dominican Republic — into a separate, publicly traded entity. Those assets generate more than half of Barrick's profit. Thornton's argument is that they are being dragged down in the market's eyes by the rest of the portfolio, and would command a richer valuation on their own.

A number of Barrick's biggest institutional holders disagree, and they are no longer disagreeing quietly. Portfolio managers at Van Eck Associates, Barrick's fourth-largest shareholder, have met company executives at least three times this year to push back on the plan, according to Bloomberg reporting. Mackenzie Financial and Franklin Equity Group have registered similar objections. At Mackenzie, portfolio manager Benoit Gervais put the frustration in blunter terms than fund managers usually allow themselves: "it would be nice to have a graceful exit of this current chairman."

Dilution, not diversification

The investor objection is mostly arithmetic. Under the proposed structure, Barrick would retain majority control of the new North American company but issue new equity to outside investors to fund the listing — a dilution current shareholders estimate at roughly 15%. In other words, existing holders would give up a slice of exactly the assets performing best, in exchange for shares in a still-troubled remainder and an equity stake in a newly public entity they didn't ask for.

Steve Land, a portfolio manager at Franklin Equity, described the resulting structure as simply confusing: "it just gives me a headache." Barrick still hasn't named a chief executive for the new company or settled where it will be domiciled, eight months after first floating the idea in December 2025. The company has targeted completion by the end of 2026 and has not said whether the transaction requires a shareholder vote — a gap investors have noticed.

The skepticism sits against a backdrop of weak relative performance. Barrick shares rose 4.2% when the spin-off was first announced but are little changed year-to-date. Newmont, the world's largest gold miner, is up 13% over the same period; Agnico Eagle has gained roughly 5.5% and overtaken Barrick to become the second-largest gold producer globally, pushing Thornton's company into third place. Nevada's operational numbers haven't helped the case for a re-rating either — analyst Martin Pradier noted plainly that "the numbers have not been good in Nevada," citing persistent turnover problems at the joint venture.

A boardroom already scarred

This is not the first governance flare-up of Thornton's tenure, and the pattern is what has investors reaching for the exit-clause language. Mark Bristow, Barrick's chief executive, was fired last September after missing internal production guidance for 14 consecutive quarters; he had also opposed the IPO plan at a contentious board meeting in London in August 2025. He was replaced by Mark Hill, previously the company's Latin America operating chief.

More tellingly, Ben van Beurden, the former Shell chief executive who joined Barrick's board as lead independent director in a role widely read as a succession track to replace Thornton, resigned after just three months when Thornton declined to step aside. Thornton's own standing with shareholders has been eroding in the open: he received only 81.1% approval at last year's annual meeting, below the chairmen of both Newmont and Agnico Eagle and well under the roughly 92% average for public-company directors. BlackRock's stewardship arm withheld its support for him entirely in 2025. In 2024, a shareholder revolt over his pay package cost him the title of executive chairman and $1.4 million in base compensation.

What the earnings call won't settle

Barrick reported second-quarter results on Monday, the same day this dispute reached print, and the IPO structure was expected to dominate the analyst call regardless of what the production numbers showed. Investors want three things Thornton hasn't yet provided: a named chief executive for the new North American entity, a confirmed domicile, and clarity on whether the transaction goes to a shareholder vote at all. Any one of those could become the flashpoint for a more formal campaign — Van Eck's repeated meetings and Mackenzie's public resignation call already have the shape of an activist opening, even without a filed proxy fight.

The deeper question is what a gold miner is supposed to look like in 2026, when peers are consolidating rather than splitting apart. Newmont's premium and Agnico's ascent to number two suggest the market is currently rewarding scale, not fragmentation — a reading that undercuts the core premise of Thornton's plan before it has even reached a vote.
