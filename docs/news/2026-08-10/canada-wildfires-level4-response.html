Canada wildfires burn over 3 million hectares

NASA MODIS satellite imagery showing burn scars spreading across Canada, captured 21 July 2026. — MODIS Land Rapid Response Team, NASA GSFC / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Canada's wildfires have burned over 3 million hectares and triggered the highest national alert level, with scientists finding climate change roughly doubled the odds.

Canada's wildfire season has moved from bad to historic. More than three million hectares had burned by mid-July, enough to push the country's national preparedness system to Level 4 — its highest rung, signalling that fire behaviour is severe in multiple jurisdictions at once and that the demand for crews and aircraft outstrips supply. By the second week of August, cumulative area burned had climbed past four million hectares across nearly 4,600 individual fires, according to Canadian Interagency Forest Fire Centre figures compiled on Wikipedia's running tally of the season. Five people have died, including four firefighters, and more than 24,000 residents have been evacuated.

Ontario and the Northwest Territories have taken the brunt of it. The Northwest Territories has lost roughly 1.29 million hectares, Ontario just under 740,000 — already more forest than has burned in any prior year on record for the province, according to provincial fire officials. The single largest blaze, the Thunder Bay 36 fire in the province's northwest, has consumed over 318,000 hectares on its own, drawing crews from Alberta, Nova Scotia and Mexico. Smoke from the fires pushed Toronto to the world's worst air quality among major cities on 15 July, and plumes drifting into the American Midwest and Northeast triggered alerts across sixteen states, exposing more than 100 million people to unhealthy air.

A once-in-40-years event, now every few years

What marks this season out is not just scale but speed: roughly 94 percent of the year's burned area accumulated in a five-week stretch between late June and late July, as prolonged heat and dryness took hold. A rapid-attribution study published this month by World Weather Attribution, the Imperial College London-led group, found that fossil-fuel-driven warming made the hot, dry, windy conditions behind the surge roughly twice as likely. Fire weather extreme enough to produce this season, the study estimated, would occur less than once every 40 years in a world without human-caused warming; it now recurs every two to six years in the Northwest Territories and every six to fifteen years in Ontario. "The impacts of climate change are very real, and we can quantify what those are and the extent to which they have become more serious," said Theodore Keeping, a climate scientist at Imperial College London and a co-author of the report.

What it tests in the pricing models

For risk desks, the interesting split is between severity and frequency. Canadian insurers have so far avoided large claims from the season, according to a Morningstar DBRS review, because the worst fires remain concentrated in sparsely insured boreal territory rather than dense property markets — direct underwriting losses look contained even as the headline hectare count breaks records. But that containment is partly luck of geography, not evidence the risk is being priced correctly: insurers around Thunder Bay have already begun pausing new coverage in fire-adjacent areas, a narrower, earlier signal than a claims event. Energy markets carry a parallel exposure — Alberta's oil sands, a large share of Canada's crude output, sit against the same boreal fire belt that has burned repeatedly in past seasons. The WWA findings argue that models built on twentieth-century return periods are already out of date; the open question for the rest of the season, which peaks through mid-August, is whether that recalibration shows up in premiums and forward curves before the next fire does.
