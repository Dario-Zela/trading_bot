Farage and Tice sue National Crime Agency over alleged data leak

Nigel Farage, whose High Court claim alleges the National Crime Agency leaked details of a £5m donation flagged to it under suspicious-activity reporting rules. — Laurie Noble / UK Parliament, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
Farage and Tice are suing the NCA for more than £25,000, alleging it leaked their bank details to the press during a donations probe.

Nigel Farage does not often find himself on the same side of a courtroom as the National Crime Agency, but not for want of trying. On Friday the Reform UK leader and his deputy, Richard Tice, lodged papers at the High Court in London accusing the agency of unlawfully leaking their private banking information to journalists — and are seeking damages north of £25,000.

The claim, filed as Reform UK rides high in the polls and braces for a general election within the next few years, turns the tables on an agency that has spent months examining the party's finances. Rather than defending themselves against the NCA's donations inquiry, Farage and Tice are now suing the investigator.

What the papers allege

The two MPs say NCA officers or staff disclosed to reporters that Suspicious Activity Reports — the confidential filings banks are legally required to submit when a transaction looks irregular — had been lodged over transfers linked to them. Court documents accuse the agency of misusing private information, breach of confidence and unlawful data processing, and demand an admission of responsibility, damages, and the names of everyone who received the leaked material.

The transactions in question have already been reported in detail. They include a £5 million donation to Farage from Christopher Harborne, a Thai-based crypto and aviation investor; an £80,000 bridging loan to Tice's property firm, Tisun Investments, from George Cottrell, a convicted fraudster and former Farage aide; and a separate £1 million donation from Cottrell's mother to a think tank run by Tice. Banks flagged each to the NCA as part of the standard suspicious-activity reporting regime, and the agency's Professional Standards Unit is now investigating both the underlying transfers and how details of them reached the press.

An agency on the back foot

Crucially, the NCA has taken no enforcement action over the transfers themselves — the complaint here is narrower, and aimed squarely at how the information became public rather than at the donations. The agency has confirmed it received complaints from both men and that the leak allegation is under internal review, but it has stopped short of accepting responsibility, saying only that further comment would not be appropriate while the matter is live.

That carefully hedged position reflects the bind regulators find themselves in when a target turns claimant. Suspicious Activity Reports sit at the heart of the UK's anti-money-laundering framework, filed in their hundreds of thousands each year and treated as strictly confidential precisely so banks will keep reporting without fear of reprisal. If a court finds the NCA's own staff broke that confidentiality, the reputational cost extends well beyond one political party's donor list — it goes to whether financial institutions and their compliance staff can trust the reporting system at all.

The wider donations story

Farage has faced scrutiny for months over Reform UK's fundraising, with a parallel parliamentary inquiry into his declarations currently paused because of an unrelated by-election. Tice, for his part, has been vocal in demanding the NCA be held accountable, framing the leak as evidence of a state agency weaponised against a party that unsettles the political establishment. Critics of Reform UK counter that the underlying question — where a party surging toward power is getting its money — has not gone away simply because the leak, rather than the donations, is now before a judge.

Both threads will likely run in parallel: the NCA's Professional Standards Unit examining its own conduct, and whatever separate scrutiny the donations themselves continue to attract. The next milestone is procedural — the NCA's formal response to the claim, and whether the agency chooses to contest it or settle. Either outcome will be read as a signal of how much legal exposure regulators carry when politically sensitive investigations spring a leak.
