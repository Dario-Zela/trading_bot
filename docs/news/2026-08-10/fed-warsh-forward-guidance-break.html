Fed's Warsh keeps steering away from forward guidance

Kevin Warsh, sworn in as Federal Reserve chair on May 22, 2026, has broken with two decades of Fed practice by declining to issue forward guidance on rates. — Official portrait, Federal Reserve / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Fed Chair Kevin Warsh has dropped forward guidance from FOMC communications, leaving markets to trade data instead of Fed hints — and risking sharper shocks when they guess wrong.

Nine weeks into the job, Kevin Warsh has made his central bank easier to describe in one sentence and harder to predict in the next. Since taking the Federal Reserve's gavel on May 22, the new chair has stripped the FOMC's post-meeting statements of the explicit rate-path signals that have anchored trader expectations for two decades, and at his last two press conferences he has declined outright to say where policy goes from here.

"Others have, I'd say, different views, and think as a general proposition forward guidance isn't the business we should be in," Warsh told reporters after the July 28-29 meeting, where the FOMC voted 9-3 to hold the federal funds rate at 3.50%-3.75%. Asked by a CNBC reporter how markets should read the Fed's silence on future moves, he offered a nine-word alternative: markets should be "letting buyers and sellers meet at prices for Treasuries."

A tool built after 2008, set aside

Forward guidance — the practice of telling markets explicitly what the Fed expects to do with rates, and under what conditions it might change course — became the central bank's primary steering mechanism after the 2008 crisis, when the policy rate hit zero and words had to do the work interest-rate cuts no longer could. Ben Bernanke, Janet Yellen and Jerome Powell each leaned on it, refining phrases like "data dependent" and "higher for longer" into signals traders could trade against. Warsh, a Fed governor from 2006 to 2011 who watched that machinery built from the inside, has concluded it calcified into something closer to a promise than a forecast — one that boxed in his predecessors when conditions shifted.

His argument, laid out in July, has two parts: guidance trains markets to front-run the Fed rather than react to incoming data, and it leaves the committee reluctant to change course once it has told the world what it plans to do. "Financial markets perform best when they react to incoming data," he has told colleagues, according to people familiar with the discussions — a wait-and-see posture that puts the onus on bond yields, not committee text, to do the signaling.

The dissent behind the silence

The July hold was not unanimous, and the split matters for how the no-guidance approach lands. Cleveland's Beth Hammack, Minneapolis's Neel Kashkari and Dallas's Lorie Logan all dissented in favor of a quarter-point hike — the first time since September 2016 that three FOMC members have broken ranks in the same direction. With inflation still running above the Fed's 2% target more than five years after it first crossed that line, the hawkish trio's dissent is itself a form of signal, just one Warsh's terser statements no longer package into an official view.

The cost of that ambiguity showed up fast. After one recent press conference, bond markets read Warsh's silence as a signal the Fed would hold rather than move — a bet that unwound into a sharp Treasury sell-off that spilled into equities, dragging the Dow down 840 points in a single session. It was a preview of the volatility a Fed without a stated reaction function can produce when investors are left to guess and guess wrong.

What comes next

Nine weeks is a thin sample, and Warsh has been explicit that this is a deliberate institutional shift rather than an improvised one — he has assigned one of five internal task forces to rethink how the Fed communicates. The September FOMC meeting, where the same three-way hawkish dissent could resurface against a still-sticky inflation backdrop, will be the next real test of whether markets can adjust to trading data instead of Fed hints — or whether every meeting now carries the shock-risk of July's press conference.
