Diageo shares jump on $1.2bn restructuring despite weak results

Diageo's global headquarters at 16 Great Marlborough Street in central London. — Mkorukmez / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Diageo shares surged on a $1.2bn cost-cutting plan even as organic sales fell 2% and the dividend was slashed — investors are backing discipline over demand recovery.

Diageo shares had their best day in years on Thursday — not because the drinks giant reported growth, but because it convincingly promised to stop chasing it the expensive way. The company behind Johnnie Walker, Guinness and Smirnoff unveiled a $1.2bn restructuring programme aimed at wringing out roughly $1bn in annual savings, alongside full-year results that showed organic sales falling 2% and a dividend cut to less than half its previous level. The stock jumped as much as 11% intraday before settling to close up around 8.7%, its strongest session since November 2020.

In one sentence: Diageo shares surged on a $1.2bn cost-cutting plan even as sales fell 2% and the dividend was slashed — investors are betting on management discipline, not a demand rebound.

The reaction is the story here. A dividend cut and a 2% sales decline would, in most years, be treated as bad news. That the market chose instead to reward the company suggests investors had already priced in weak trading and were waiting for evidence that Sir Dave Lewis — brought in as chief executive on January 1 and nicknamed "Drastic Dave" for his cost-cutting record at Tesco — intended to act on it.

A weaker year than the share price suggests

Strip away Thursday's rally and the underlying numbers are unambiguously soft. Net sales fell 3% to $19.6bn in the year to June, with organic sales — which exclude currency and acquisition effects — down 2%, split between a 0.4-point volume decline and 1.6 points of negative price and mix. North America, historically Diageo's most profitable region, was the worst performer: sales there dropped 8.4%, with tequila down 21% as US consumers traded down and pandemic-era premium-spirits demand kept fading. Europe grew 3% and Africa 13.3%, but neither offset the American slide.

Reported operating profit fell 27% to $3.2bn and net profit dropped almost 23% to roughly $2bn, dragged down by $2.4bn in exceptional charges — $1.5bn of impairments, including a write-down on the Don Papa rum brand and hits from Turkish hyperinflation accounting, plus $900m of restructuring costs. Organic operating profit, which strips out those one-offs, actually rose 2% to $5.6bn, helped by $540m of savings already delivered under Diageo's existing Accelerate efficiency programme. That distinction — a business whose underlying profitability is intact even as reported earnings look grim — is likely what gave the market the confidence to look past the headline loss.

The $1.2bn bet

Lewis used the same day's Capital Markets Day, his first full strategy presentation since taking over, to set out how the money will be spent. Diageo will invest $1.2bn to redesign its operating framework and supply chain, targeting about $850m of savings from a leaner corporate structure and roughly $150m more from supply-chain efficiencies, for close to $1bn in total annual savings by the third year. Around 40% of the savings are expected to land in fiscal 2027, with the rest following in fiscal 2028. The company said 90% of its markets would be operating under the new model by September.

The cuts are aimed squarely at back-office functions rather than front-line sales and marketing teams, though some corporate departments face reductions of 20-30% of headcount, and several distilleries are being shut or scaled back to address overcapacity built up during the pandemic-era demand surge. Severance costs alone came to $514m in the year. Lewis told reporters the programme would have "very significant impacts" on staff, but framed it as necessary discipline rather than crisis management.

"The revised operating framework will allow us to invest in the turnaround without needing to reduce operating profit," Lewis said, adding that the new structure gives the company "confidence that we can return Diageo to a business consistently creating value for shareholders."

Resetting the dividend, resetting expectations

The more contentious decision was the dividend. Diageo cut its final payout to 30 cents a share from 62.98 cents, taking the full-year dividend to 50 cents from 103.48 cents — a reduction of more than half, subject to shareholder approval at the annual meeting in November. It comes alongside a new policy that ties payouts to a 30-50% share of earnings, with a $0.50-a-share annual floor, replacing a progressive-dividend approach that had committed Diageo to raising the payout every year regardless of trading conditions.

For a stock long held partly for its dividend reliability, that is a real repositioning — an acknowledgment that the old commitment had become a constraint on the balance sheet rather than a signal of strength. Investors appear to have accepted the trade: a smaller, more flexible dividend in exchange for cash freed up to fund the turnaround, rather than a payout maintained by stretching the balance sheet through a prolonged sales slump.

What the market is actually pricing

Taken together, the results and the rally send a consistent signal: shareholders are not yet betting on a near-term recovery in spirits demand. Diageo's own guidance for fiscal 2027 calls for only broadly flat organic sales growth, with North America still expected to decline by mid-single digits, alongside low-to-mid single-digit operating profit growth. Longer term, management is targeting low-single-digit sales growth and mid-single-digit profit growth through fiscal 2029 — modest by the standards of Diageo's pre-pandemic decade.

What moved the stock instead was confidence that Lewis will execute on costs regardless of what demand does. That is a lower bar than a growth story, and arguably a safer one for a company that has disappointed on sales for several years running. For a wider read on consumer and luxury-goods exposure, Diageo's performance functions as something of a bellwether: if the market is content to reward cost discipline over top-line recovery here, it may be signalling the same recalibrated expectations across premium consumer names more broadly.

The next test comes at the November shareholder meeting, where investors vote on the new dividend policy, and then at half-year results early next year, the first checkpoint on whether the North American decline is stabilising or still deepening.
