European wildfires force 330,000 to flee as drought grips France and Spain

Fire burns near a cemetery in Piedralaves, Ávila province — part of Spain's largest wildfire on record. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
European wildfires have forced 330,000 people to flee France and Spain as historic drought and heat scorch the harvest that feeds global grain markets.

The fires started, as they so often do now, in the dry margins where farmland meets forest — a spark near Ávila in central Spain in late July, another west of Bordeaux in France's Gironde. Neither has fully gone out. Together they have driven roughly 330,000 people from their homes across the two countries, one of the largest coordinated evacuations Europe has seen outside wartime, and left a trail of scorched land that stretches from the Atlantic coast to the plains south of Madrid.

In one sentence: European wildfires have forced 330,000 people to flee France and Spain as historic drought and heat scorch the harvest that feeds global grain markets.

The scale is not in dispute. In France, some 220,000 people were moved out of the Gironde and Landes departments as fire crews fought a blaze that has burned over 420 square kilometres of pine forest — an area four times the size of Paris — and generated its own pyrocumulonimbus storm clouds, a phenomenon French officials say they had never recorded before. In Spain, roughly 79,000 people fled over a single weekend as a fire near Ávila, west of Madrid, grew into the largest wildfire in the country's recorded history, consuming more than 500 square kilometres of hillside. At least 13 people have died in Spain's fires this year. "This doesn't have a precedent," one firefighter with two decades' experience told the Associated Press.

A heatwave that outran the models

What set this summer apart was not any single fire but the sequence of heat that preceded it. Western Europe recorded its hottest June on record, part of a run of heatwaves that began in May — a full month earlier than the season typically turns dangerous. France's public health agency has tallied 5,764 excess deaths during the June 17–July 2 heatwave alone, describing the mortality among people over 15 as "unprecedented"; more than half of those deaths clustered in the three hottest days, June 25–27. Spain has been bracing for its fourth heatwave of the summer, with forecasters warning of temperatures above 40°C in parts of the south.

The underlying mechanism is unremarkable to climate scientists even as its effects have been anything but: extreme heat draws moisture out of soil and vegetation, turning forest and scrubland into fuel, while Europe's above-average warming rate — roughly twice the global mean — means these conditions are arriving earlier and more often than the historical record would suggest. Spain's national statistics show 1,530 square kilometres burned nationwide so far this year.

The harvest under strain

The agricultural toll is now showing up in the numbers that matter to traders as much as to farmers. France, ordinarily the European Union's largest wheat producer, is forecast to bring in around 32 million tonnes this year, with average yields down from 7.42 to 6.93 tonnes per hectare — a decline concentrated in the central and western regions hit hardest by drought and repeated heat. Spain's crop is expected to fall even further, to 5.9 million tonnes, nearly a quarter below last year's record harvest. Across the EU and UK combined, the 2026 grain crop is now forecast at 286.6 million tonnes, down roughly 9 million tonnes from June's estimate.

Markets have already registered the shift. French wheat touched a record €219.25 a tonne on July 14 as traders priced in the threat to late-season crops, and the European cereals trade body EUCOLAIT has cut its EU wheat production estimate to 140.8 million tonnes, well below last year's 149.8 million. The USDA now expects global wheat demand to outstrip production by roughly 0.8% in 2026/27 — a reversal from last year's surplus — leaving the market with less cushion to absorb a bad summer in France and Spain than it had a year ago.

Britain's turn in the heat

The drought line runs north too. The UK's Met Office has forecast temperatures approaching 36°C by Thursday, and 2026 is on pace to equal or break the record for days above 30°C nationwide. Hosepipe bans now cover more than 25 million customers, including all of Thames Water and South East Water's territory and, as of August 8, most of the South West. England and Wales recorded their driest July since rainfall records began in 1836.

The consequences extend beyond gardens and lawns. Across the Alpine region, hydropower — which supplies roughly 28% of the EU's electricity in an average first quarter — is running well below capacity; Verbund, Austria's largest utility, says it is missing roughly a third of the water volume its plants would normally process. That leaves grids leaning harder on gas and other backup generation precisely when heat is pushing cooling demand higher, a pattern utilities and grid operators are watching closely as the season wears on. Insurers, meanwhile, are recalibrating: drought-linked losses reached an estimated $13 billion globally in 2025, according to Aon, and this year's fire and crop damage will feed directly into how reinsurers price climate risk for 2027.

What follows

None of the individual pieces — the fires, the heat deaths, the wheat forecast, the hosepipe bans — is unprecedented on its own. What is harder to dismiss is the pattern: a heatwave season that started in May, drought conditions gripping two of the EU's three largest grain producers simultaneously, and a UK water and energy system absorbing its own version of the same stress. The next test is the scale of the actual harvest once combines move through what's left of the crop in the coming weeks; a confirmed shortfall anywhere near the current forecasts would tighten European wheat supply further just as global stocks are already thinning. Firefighters in Gironde and Ávila say their fires are largely contained but not extinguished — and forecasters are not ruling out a fifth heatwave before summer's end.
