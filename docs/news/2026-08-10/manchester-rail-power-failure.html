Manchester rail signalling failure disruption drags into third day

Manchester Piccadilly, one of the stations hit by cascading delays after the nearby Ashburys signalling centre lost power on 6 August. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A generator that failed to start turned a 90-second power cut at Manchester's rail signalling hub into days of North West train chaos.

It took ninety seconds for the lights to go out at Manchester's Rail Operating Centre, and the better part of a week for the North West's railway to recover from it.

The outage struck at 1.41pm on Thursday 6 August, when a fault on a high-voltage cable in Harpurhey — traced by SP Energy Networks to an incident affecting more than 400 postcodes in the area — cut power to the Ashburys signalling hub that controls train movements across a wide swathe of north-west England and the trans-Pennine corridor. Signals defaulted to red. Every train in the affected area stopped where it stood.

What should have been a brief interruption became a multi-day mess for one reason: the backup generator that was supposed to carry the centre through the gap did not start in time. Network Rail has since confirmed the emergency supply failed to activate when mains power dropped, leaving the signalling system dark for longer than its design was meant to allow.

A short circuit, a long tail

No trains could move between Manchester Piccadilly, Manchester Victoria and Bolton on the Thursday afternoon, and operators including Avanti West Coast, Northern, TransPennine Express, CrossCountry, East Midlands Railway and Transport for Wales issued do-not-travel notices as cancellations rippled outward across the Midlands. Chris Wright, Network Rail's North West route director, said at the time that "major disruption is expected for the rest of the day" — an understatement, as it turned out.

Engineers restored the signalling system that evening, but power coming back on was only half the job. Safety rules meant every section of track had to be checked and brought back into service in stages rather than switched on at once, and the trains and crews displaced by the shutdown were, by then, scattered in the wrong depots and the wrong cities. Northern warned of problems into Friday morning; TransPennine Express said disruption could run until midday. By the weekend, most timetables were close to normal, though residual cancellations and short-formed trains continued to surface into this week as the network absorbed the knock-on effects.

The resilience question

Ashburys is one of twelve regional Rail Operating Centres that Network Rail has built since 2012 to replace more than 800 local signal boxes, concentrating control of vast stretches of track into a handful of buildings on the promise of fewer delays and lower cost. The trade-off is that each centre becomes a single point of failure for the region it serves — and Thursday's generator fault is exactly the scenario that model is supposed to be hardened against. Network Rail has opened an internal investigation and says it is working to "build additional resilience into our signalling systems to ensure this doesn't happen again," language that will be tested the next time a substation trips.

For now the practical toll falls on commuters, business travellers and the freight and just-in-time supply chains threaded through Manchester's rail nodes, all of whom just had a preview of what happens when one cable fault outgrows its backup plan. The findings of Network Rail's review, and whether SP Energy Networks identifies a cause for the original cable incident, are the next things worth watching.
