Executives warn Britain risks falling behind in the AI race without faster telecoms upgrades

An Openreach engineer works on the UK's fixed-line network — the fibre backbone executives say needs faster investment. — Kkempton / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
UK executives warn patchy fibre and grid capacity could cost Britain the AI race, but the only dated example of a stalled project is a power delay, not a fibre one.

Britain's technology executives have a new complaint about the country's readiness for the artificial intelligence boom: the pipes aren't big enough. Industry figures cited in a Guardian report this week warn that sluggish fibre-optic and data-centre buildout could leave the UK trailing the US and Asian rivals in the race to deploy AI at scale — a claim that turns out to be easier to assert than to prove.

"The UK is simply unfit for data centre development, with some of the world's highest electricity prices, an ill-suited planning system and a systemic failure of governance."

That's Joshua Leahy, chief technology officer at trading firm XTX Markets, whose frustration is echoed by telecoms bosses. Lee Myall, chief executive of network operator Neos Networks, has been blunter still, arguing Britain risks "losing out on one of the greatest economic opportunities" of the decade without more investment in the fibre backbones that carry data between cities and into data-centre campuses.

The numbers behind the warnings come mostly from a Neos Networks-commissioned survey of 300 UK data-centre operators, enterprise IT leaders and local-government officials, conducted last autumn. It found 82% of data-centre operators had delayed site builds or expansions because of insufficient fibre availability, and 89% of local authorities said fibre gaps had held up regional projects. Separately, campaigners at the Social Market Foundation have pointed out that powering a UK data centre costs roughly four times what it costs in the US — a gap that has more to do with electricity markets than broadband cabling.

Thin on specifics

What's missing from the warnings is a clear line from "fibre is patchy in some places" to "AI investment is actually going elsewhere." Ofcom's own tracking shows full-fibre coverage climbing steadily — reaching 82% of UK homes by January 2026, with gigabit-capable coverage at 89% and rising. The one concrete, dated example of a marquee AI project running into infrastructure trouble this year is Nscale's £2bn data centre in Loughton, Essex, which was told in July its 90-megawatt grid connection won't be ready in time for a planned 2027 opening — a power problem, not a fibre one. The company is reportedly in talks with Bloom Energy about fuel cells to bridge the gap.

That distinction matters. Executives quoted in this debate tend to fold grid capacity, planning delays and fibre availability into a single undifferentiated complaint about "infrastructure," which makes the warnings sound more urgent — and more vague — than the underlying data supports. No company has yet said it moved an AI investment out of the UK because of a fibre shortfall; the closest thing to hard evidence is a single power-connection delay on one project.

The broader competitiveness gap is real by some measures — the UK controls a small fraction of the world's AI computing capacity compared with the US — but that's a story about chip supply and capital spending as much as cabling. For now, the fibre warnings function more as a lobbying pitch for faster planning reform and continued network investment than as evidence of AI workloads already stalling on British shores.

The next test comes when Ofcom publishes its autumn Connected Nations update and when Nscale confirms whether its Essex site's power workaround holds — proof points that will say more about the UK's AI infrastructure than another survey of anonymous executives.
