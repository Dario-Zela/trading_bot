Iceland votes in knife-edge referendum on restarting EU membership talks

Kristrún Frostadóttir, Iceland's prime minister, has framed the referendum around currency stability rather than EU membership itself. — Dati Bendo / European Union, 2025
In one sentence
270,000 Icelanders voted in a referendum too close to call on restarting EU accession talks, pitting the volatile króna against fears for fishing sovereignty.

Iceland went to the polls on Saturday to decide something that sounds procedural but carries outsized weight: whether the government should reopen membership negotiations with the European Union, talks that have sat frozen since 2013. Roughly 270,000 people were eligible to vote, and by evening the count looked too close to call, with pre-referendum polling from Gallup and Maskína putting both camps within a point or two of 50-50.

The ballot question itself is narrow — "Should Iceland resume accession negotiations with the European Union?" — and a yes vote would not make Iceland an EU member. It would restart a process that stalled with 11 of 35 policy chapters provisionally closed, and with the single most contentious one, fisheries, never opened at all. But narrow as the question is, the campaign around it has been anything but.

A currency argument dressed as a sovereignty fight

Prime Minister Kristrún Frostadóttir, whose Social Democratic Alliance leads the governing coalition, has framed the vote less as a referendum on Brussels and more as a chance to find out what terms Iceland could actually get. Her core pitch is economic: the króna's volatility feeds the inflation and high interest rates that have squeezed households, and running an independent currency, she has argued, is "costing us a ton" in reserves Iceland must hold to defend it. Eurozone entry, on her telling, would bring investment stability along with softer sweeteners — Arctic agriculture grants, infrastructure loans, deeper academic ties.

The no camp doesn't contest that the króna is volatile. It contests what Iceland would give up to fix it. Opponents, drawing on a well-worn vein of Icelandic politics, warn that EU membership would eventually put the country's fishing waters — the resource that built its post-war prosperity — within reach of foreign vessels and a common fisheries policy. That argument has held up remarkably well since Iceland first walked away from talks in 2013, and it's the reason polling has stayed locked near an even split for months rather than drifting either way.

Geopolitics at the margins

The backdrop has shifted since Iceland last had this argument. Renewed American interest in Greenland and the wider Arctic, and the broader contest over shipping routes and critical minerals opening up as sea ice retreats, has put Iceland's strategic position back on the map in Brussels and Washington alike. Officials in the EU have made little secret of wanting Reykjavík back at the table, seeing it as a low-friction addition compared with the bloc's stalled Balkan and eastern candidates. But pollsters and Icelandic political scientists have been consistent on one point: domestic economics and fisheries, not Arctic strategy, are what's actually moving voters.

What a knife-edge result means either way

A result this close settles little regardless of which side clears 50%. A narrow yes hands Frostadóttir's government a mandate to negotiate but not necessarily to conclude — a second referendum on the terms themselves would still lie years off, and a slim margin gives the no camp every reason to keep contesting the process at each stage. A narrow no freezes the question again, probably for years, while leaving the underlying complaint — a small, volatile currency exposed to outsized swings in fish exports and energy prices — unresolved.

For markets watching the króna, the near-term signal is less about EU accession itself, which remains a decade-scale prospect even in the most favourable outcome, and more about political durability at home. A coalition that wins this vote narrowly, or loses it narrowly, heads into the next election cycle with an electorate still split down the middle on the country's fundamental economic orientation. Final results were expected within hours of polls closing Saturday night; the next marker to watch is whether Frostadóttir's government treats any yes vote as licence to move quickly, or whether — as after 2009 — accession talk quietly stalls again under the weight of the fisheries question.
