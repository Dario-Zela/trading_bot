Households could save up to £173 a year by switching to a fixed energy tariff

Gas prices are driving most of October's price cap increase, with unit rates up 8.7% on the back of wholesale market volatility. — Federico Cardoner / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
UK households on variable tariffs can save up to £173 a year by fixing now, before October's price cap rise and a forecast winter gas spike erode the gap.

British households paying variable-rate energy tariffs are leaving money on the table just as the market's cost of insurance against a cold winter starts to rise. Consumer analysis this week put the potential saving from switching to a fixed-rate deal at up to £173 a year — a gap that is likely to close, not widen, once October's price cap increase lands.

Ofgem confirmed the cap will rise 3.6% from 1 October, adding £60 to the typical dual-fuel bill and taking it to £1,723 a year. The increase would have been closer to 6% without the government's temporary cut to VAT on electricity, from 5% to zero, which runs for six months. Gas is doing most of the damage: the unit rate is up 8.7%, against a modest 0.8% rise in electricity, reflecting a wholesale gas market that Martin Lewis of MoneySavingExpert this week described as the highest he's seen "since the Ukraine crisis."

The arithmetic of switching now

Around 22 million UK households sit on Ofgem's default price cap; the rest, roughly a third, are already on fixed deals and insulated from the October move. For those still exposed, the calculus is straightforward: the best fixed tariffs on the market are currently priced below even July's cap, before this month's rise is added on top. Fuse Energy's 18-month fixed deal is running about 7.6% cheaper than the July cap including cashback; E.on Next's 12-month exclusive fix is roughly 5.8% cheaper. Both undercut the £1,723 October figure by a wider margin still.

That's the mechanical source of the £173 headline saving — a household locking in a competitive fix today pays a rate set before the latest gas-driven increase, and keeps that rate through winter regardless of what wholesale markets do next. The saving is usage-dependent and postcode-dependent, but the direction is consistent across comparison sites: fixed beats variable by somewhere in the £170–£185 range for a typical medium-use household right now.

Why the window may not stay open

The complication is that fixed-tariff pricing isn't static. Suppliers reprice their offers against forward wholesale curves, and those curves have been climbing. Ofgem's own commentary attributes the October increase to volatility tied to the Middle East conflict, and forecasters are pencilling in a further cap rise of around 10% for January — driven by the same tight European gas storage position now drawing "winter panic" talk in wholesale circles. If suppliers pull forward that expectation into their fixed pricing, today's below-cap deals get replaced by tomorrow's more expensive ones, and the saving on offer shrinks or disappears.

That creates the asymmetry at the heart of the story: households delaying a decision aren't preserving optionality, they're gambling that fixed prices fall before the cap catches up. Recent history argues against it — comparison sites note that fixes were cheaper still six weeks ago, meaning the market has already been moving against latecomers.

What it signals beyond the household bill

The switching data is also a rough proxy for sentiment. A wave of households rushing to fix ahead of winter is effectively retail demand agreeing with wholesale markets that gas is going higher — the same signal embedded in Europe's depleted storage levels and in gas futures pricing through the winter strip. Utilities and suppliers watch this closely: a fixed-tariff book locked in below marginal cost is a margin risk if wholesale prices spike further, which is part of why some suppliers already limit volumes on their cheapest fixes.

The next hard data point is Ofgem's January price cap announcement, expected in late November, which will confirm or soften the forecast further rise. Until then, the trade households are being asked to make is simple: pay a known, fixed rate now, or keep betting on a wholesale gas market that has spent 2026 moving in one direction.
