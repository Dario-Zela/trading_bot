Greens demand UK datacentre building ban; Labour rejects the call

Green Party leader Zack Polanski, who is calling for a temporary halt on new UK data centre approvals. — Rob Browne / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
The Green Party wants a pause on new UK data centres over water and energy use during a record drought; Labour says that would cost jobs and national security.

Zack Polanski wants the government to stop approving new data centres in England, at least for now. Labour's answer, in essence, is no — and the exchange has become the clearest test yet of whether Britain's drive to build AI infrastructure will bend to environmental limits or simply override them.

The Green Party leader set out the demand this week: a temporary halt on new data centre approvals, carve-outs for small local facilities, and a requirement that operators publish annual water and energy figures. "We need to slam the brakes on the rampant building of new data centres which could put our communities at risk of water shortages," Polanski said. "Data should serve people, not the other way around — but right now, people come a poor second."

The timing is pointed. England has just recorded its driest July on record, with roughly three-quarters of the country now in drought. Polanski called it "outrageous" that data centres are treated as critical infrastructure while households face restrictions. "There must be no scenario in which a data centre receives priority in a drought over households, public services or essential food production," he said, accusing Labour of a response to AI's environmental impact that has been "negligible and negligent."

Why critical status matters

The flashpoint is a 2024 decision, taken under the previous government and kept by Labour, to designate data centres as Critical National Infrastructure — the same bracket as water treatment works and power stations. That status doesn't hand data centres priority access to water or power, but it does streamline planning permission and guarantees them extra support during incidents like cyber-attacks or extreme weather. The Greens argue that in practice it stacks the deck in a drought, even if no formal priority exists on paper.

Hard numbers on UK data centre water use are thin — there's no official national tally — but industry estimates put a single large facility's daily draw in the range of a small town's supply, which is the scale that has campaigners worried as reservoirs run low.

Labour's rejection

The government's response was blunt. A spokesperson said a construction pause "would simply send investment and good jobs abroad, while leaving Britain dependent on overseas computing for the processing of sensitive health and defence data," adding: "Put simply: it would be a disaster for jobs and national security." That framing — jobs and sovereignty versus water and power — is the same one ministers have used to wave off telecoms-infrastructure warnings and AI Act comparisons, and it signals where Labour's priorities sit as it courts hyperscaler investment.

What it actually changes

As an opposition proposal, Polanski's call has no binding force; the Greens hold four Westminster seats and no path to blocking a planning decision. What it does is put a marker down before the next drought-linked controversy, and it sharpens the choice Labour has made explicit: growth first, mitigation later. The next test comes whenever the Environment Agency or a local council has to decide, in the middle of a water shortage, whether a data centre's cooling supply gets treated any differently from a household's tap.
