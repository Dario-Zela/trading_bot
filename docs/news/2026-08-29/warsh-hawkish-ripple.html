Warsh's hawkish Jackson Hole turn ripples through rates, gold, gilts and stocks

Fed Chair Kevin Warsh delivered his first Jackson Hole address since taking over from Jerome Powell in May 2026. — Official portrait, Federal Reserve / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Warsh's Jackson Hole warning that inflation hasn't cooled enough sent September hike odds toward 60%, sinking gold, bitcoin and stocks while gilt yields stuck above 5%.

In one sentence: Fed Chair Kevin Warsh told Jackson Hole that summer's cooler inflation prints don't convince him the fight is won, and futures markets responded by pricing in a September hike, dumping gold and crypto, and locking UK borrowing costs above 5%.

Kevin Warsh used his first Jackson Hole speech as Federal Reserve chair to say, in effect, that he isn't buying the good news. Inflation reports this summer came in softer than economists expected — the kind of data that, under his predecessor, might have opened the door to a rate cut. Warsh closed it instead. Core PCE inflation is running at 3.7% over the past year and at a 4.1% annualized pace over the last six months, he told the Kansas City Fed's annual symposium in Wyoming on Friday, and the recent softness "does not tell me that underlying trends have meaningfully improved."

Traders took him at his word. CME FedWatch pricing for a September rate hike jumped from roughly 35% before the speech to near 60% after it — a 25-point swing compressed into a single Friday morning, and one immediately visible across four separate corners of the market: US rates, gold, gilts and equities.

The speech that moved the curve

Warsh, confirmed in May by the narrowest Senate vote for a Fed chair in history — 54-45 — is five months into the job and had not yet given a marquee address on where he stands. Jackson Hole answered that. He argued the labor market is effectively at full employment, that financial conditions are "not particularly restrictive" despite where the policy rate sits, and that the Fed "should not indulge a regime in which market participants are looking primarily to the Fed for their next trade" — a pointed rejection of the forward-guidance habit his predecessors leaned on.

That refusal to pre-commit is itself notable: Warsh gave no explicit signal on September, and investors moved anyway. The policy-sensitive two-year Treasury yield rose as much as 9 basis points to 4.32%, the ten-year added roughly 4 basis points to touch 4.72% intraday, and the dollar gained 0.4%. Markets didn't need forward guidance; they read the inflation framing and repriced the reaction function themselves.

Gold, bitcoin and a $488 million unwind

The clearest read on how much leverage had been built around a softer-Fed narrative came from commodities and crypto. Spot gold fell as much as 2.4% on the day, and bitcoin dropped below $78,000 — at one point touching roughly $76,900 — as traders unwound positions that had assumed the Fed's tightening cycle was largely done.

The move cascaded through leveraged crypto positioning: roughly $488 million in liquidations hit the market within hours, more than $360 million of it long positions caught wrong-footed by the sudden repricing. It is the kind of number that illustrates, more than any single yield move, how much of this summer's rally in gold and digital assets had been built on an assumption Warsh just declined to validate.

On Wall Street itself the reaction was comparatively restrained but still directional: the S&P 500 fell about 0.3% to 7,711.05 and the Nasdaq Composite lost roughly 0.5% to 26,400.56, giving back part of Thursday's Nvidia-driven tech rally. Muted by the standards of the bond and crypto moves, but consistent with them — a market trimming risk into a September FOMC meeting that now looks more contested than it did a week ago.

The gilt market takes notice

Warsh's remarks didn't stay contained to dollar assets. Ten-year UK gilt yields held near 5.01%, and traders pushed their expectations for the Bank of England's next move further out, with markets now pricing the next hike as more likely to land in 2027 than late 2026 — roughly 24 basis points of tightening priced in by December and 36 basis points by February. The mechanism is straightforward: a more hawkish Fed lifts the dollar and global yields together, and a Bank of England already wary of moving out of step with Washington has less room to ease even where domestic UK data might otherwise argue for it.

That matters beyond currency traders. Gilt yields feed directly into UK mortgage pricing and the government's own borrowing costs — relevant context as Chancellor Rachel Reeves faces her own fiscal arithmetic ahead of the autumn Budget. A Fed that stays higher for longer makes every other central bank's job of easing that much harder to justify.

What ties it together

The throughline across rates, gold, crypto and gilts is the same: markets had priced in a Fed on the verge of confirming the soft landing, and Warsh spent his first big speech as chair refusing to confirm it. The gap between what the data seemed to say and what the Fed chair was willing to say about the data is now the thing being traded. September 16-17, when the FOMC next meets, is the obvious catalyst — Warsh's studied refusal to offer forward guidance means the August jobs report (due September 5) and the next CPI print will do more to settle the odds than anything said in Wyoming. Until then, the market has already made its own call: 60% and rising.
