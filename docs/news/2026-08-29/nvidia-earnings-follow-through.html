Nvidia's AI-chip boom rolls on as data-centre revenue nearly doubles

Jensen Huang presents Nvidia's Blackwell chip architecture, the platform now ramping through data centres worldwide. — Pronoia / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
Nvidia's data-centre revenue nearly doubled to $89bn, but the faster growth now sits with smaller AI buyers, not the big four hyperscalers.

Nvidia's data-centre business — the unit that sells the chips powering ChatGPT, Gemini and every other large language model in commercial use — generated $89bn in the quarter ended 26 July, up 117% on the same period last year. Total company revenue came to $96.2bn, roughly double what it was twelve months ago. Wall Street had already priced in a strong number; Nvidia beat it anyway, and then raised the outlook further.

The company guided third-quarter revenue to $108bn, plus or minus 2%, and chief executive Jensen Huang told analysts on the earnings call that demand for calendar 2027 is tracking toward roughly 70% growth — close to double what analysts had modelled. Shares rose more than 5% in the following session's premarket trading, adding to a run that has made Nvidia the most consequential single stock in the S&P 500's earnings calendar.

Where the growth is actually coming from

The headline number obscures a more interesting split. This was the first quarter Nvidia broke revenue into two buckets: sales to "hyperscalers" — Microsoft, Amazon, Google and Meta — and sales to what it calls AI Clouds, Industrial and Enterprise customers, a group that includes smaller cloud providers, sovereign AI projects, and corporate buyers outside the big four. Hyperscaler revenue rose to $48.7bn, up 13% on the prior quarter. The other bucket, ACIE, brought in $40.3bn — up 138% year-on-year and 31% quarter-on-quarter, roughly double the hyperscalers' sequential pace.

That is the detail that matters for the bubble argument. If Nvidia's growth were simply four companies buying more chips to build bigger data centres for the same handful of AI labs, the trade would rest entirely on those four balance sheets staying generous. Instead, the faster-growing segment is the one spread across a wider set of buyers — evidence, at least for this quarter, that demand is broadening rather than concentrating. Huang put it plainly on the call: AI demand has "spread far beyond one anchor lab."

The capex arithmetic behind it

None of this happens without the hyperscalers' own spending. Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon and Meta together spent $166bn on capital expenditure in the June quarter, up 87% year-on-year, and combined hyperscaler capex has risen 272% over the ten quarters since the start of 2024. Nvidia's own figures put the "top five" hyperscalers' combined 2027 capex at $1.3 trillion, up from $800bn this year — a number that, if it holds, keeps the current chip cycle running well past the next earnings date.

Huang also pushed back on treating that $1.3 trillion, or Nvidia's oft-cited $500bn multi-year demand figure, as a ceiling. He said the number reflects only what is already visible on the order book through 2025 and 2026, and that "many new developments should increase our expectation" — a hedge that lets Nvidia bank a conservative floor while leaving room to beat it, as it has done for several quarters running.

The quarter's growth also arrived without any contribution from China, where U.S. export controls kept H20 chip sales at zero for the entire period. That Nvidia still doubled its data-centre business without its largest historically restricted market is, on its own, a data point for the bulls; it is also a reminder of how much upside sits outside the current guidance if the restrictions ever ease.

What breaks the story

The read-through for markets is straightforward and has been for several quarters: as long as hyperscaler and enterprise capex keeps climbing, Nvidia's supply chain — foundries, memory makers, networking vendors — keeps printing. The genuine open question is not this quarter's number but next year's mix. If ACIE keeps outgrowing hyperscaler revenue, the AI buildout looks like an economy-wide capital cycle. If hyperscaler spending decelerates and the broader cohort fails to pick up the slack, the same numbers that today read as diversification could just as easily read as a slower unwind of a concentrated one.

The next checkpoints are Nvidia's third-quarter print, due in late November, and the hyperscalers' own capex guidance in their October results — the two data sets that will show whether the $1.3 trillion 2027 figure is holding up or already being revised.
