Wainua heart-drug trial data draws neutral-to-negative read-through for rivals

AstraZeneca's Cambridge headquarters, where the CARDIO-TTRansform trial result has raised questions about the company's late-stage pipeline credibility. — D Wells / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Wainua's full ATTR-CM trial data showed no benefit added to standard stabiliser therapy, denting AstraZeneca and Ionis and complicating the read for Alnylam's rival pipeline.

The fuller picture is in, and it isn't kind. At the European Society of Cardiology Congress in Munich this week, AstraZeneca and Ionis Pharmaceuticals presented the complete dataset from CARDIO-TTRansform, the 1,432-patient trial testing their gene-silencing drug Wainua (eplontersen) against placebo in transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy — a rare, stiffening heart-muscle disease. The topline miss was already known from a July disclosure. What the full data added was worse: among patients already taking a standard stabiliser drug such as tafamidis, Wainua added no measurable benefit at all.

That detail matters more than the original headline failure, because it goes to the commercial logic of an entire drug class. Roughly 57% of trial participants were on a TTR stabiliser at enrolment and another 24% started one during the study — reflecting how ATTR-CM is actually treated today. In that real-world population, adding Wainua on top of standard care produced a rate ratio of 0.89 (95% CI 0.73–1.09, p=0.277) on the composite of cardiovascular death and recurrent cardiac events — not statistically significant. A prespecified subgroup of patients on eplontersen alone, without a stabiliser, did show a nominally significant benefit (hazard ratio 0.71), but that is a shrinking slice of the addressable market as stabiliser use becomes routine.

A credibility problem, not just a revenue one

When the topline result first landed in July, AstraZeneca shares fell nearly 10% in their steepest single-day drop since late 2024; Ionis, which discovered eplontersen and shares economics with AstraZeneca, fell close to 21%. Jefferies analysts called it a case where the trial "was meant to be a slam dunk, making the outright failure surprising," while estimating the direct hit to AstraZeneca's 2030 sales ambitions at only around 2% on a net-present-value basis. The bigger cost, in their view, is reputational: a late-stage cardiology trial expected to be routine instead became a public miss.

The fuller ESC dataset released this week did comparatively little fresh damage to the share prices — AstraZeneca's US-listed shares slipped about 1%, Ionis closed down 2% — suggesting the market had already priced in the broad shape of the failure. Stifel analysts nonetheless described the combination-therapy hazard ratio of roughly 1.14 as "clearly worse than expected," adding that it "makes it hard" to see a regulatory path for Wainua in this indication as currently studied.

What it means for Alnylam

The more interesting question for investors is what CARDIO-TTRansform says about Alnylam Pharmaceuticals, whose rival RNA-interference drug Amvuttra (vutrisiran) is the only approved gene-silencing option left standing in ATTR-CM now that eplontersen has stumbled. Jefferies framed the read-through as "neutral to negative," arguing the results reinforce that oral stabilisers from Pfizer and BridgeBio remain the preferred first-line choice and that gene silencers as a class face a higher bar to prove incremental value.

Other desks read it differently. Oppenheimer noted that Wainua's effect on a standard six-minute-walk functional test at 30 months was "far less robust" than Amvuttra has shown in monotherapy, and that Wainua's rate of treatment discontinuation from adverse events — 6.4% — was roughly double Amvuttra's 3%. Stifel went further, suggesting the weak combination-therapy data could push Alnylam to lean harder into monotherapy positioning for its own late-stage candidate, nucresiran, calling that potentially "good for the stock." Alnylam used its own ESC presence to press the point, showing expanded HELIOS-B data in which vutrisiran cut the risk of a recurrent cardiovascular event or death by 28% overall, and by 33% in patients who hadn't started tafamidis at baseline — filed as evidence for positioning vutrisiran as a foundational therapy rather than an add-on. None of this is independently adjudicated; it is competing pharma companies and sell-side analysts framing the same numbers for different audiences, and it should be read that way.

What's next

AstraZeneca and Ionis have not said whether they will pursue a narrower monotherapy-focused approval pathway for Wainua in ATTR-CM, given the nominal benefit seen without background stabilisers — a decision likely to hinge on discussions with regulators in the coming months. Alnylam's own outcomes data for nucresiran, its next-generation candidate, is the more consequential catalyst: any readout there will be measured directly against the bar CARDIO-TTRansform just reset for the entire ATTR-CM field.
