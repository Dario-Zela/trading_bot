Bessent confirms Treasury's stabilization fund swapped reserves into yen

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, whose letter to Sen. Elizabeth Warren confirmed the ESF's euro-for-yen swap. — U.S. Department of the Treasury / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Bessent told Warren the Treasury's currency fund swapped euros for yen, not a loan to Japan, but still won't say how much it bought.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has confirmed what currency traders had largely inferred already: the government's emergency currency fund sold euros to buy yen, and it did so using existing reserves rather than any new taxpayer money. The admission came in a letter to Senator Elizabeth Warren, capping two weeks of sparring over an intervention that briefly steadied the yen in late July before much of the effect wore off.

The Exchange Stabilization Fund — a roughly $150 billion pool that is Treasury's standing tool for currency operations — sold euro holdings to acquire yen after the Japanese currency slid to a 40-year low near 163 to the dollar in late July. It was the first joint US-Japan intervention since 1998: Japan's Ministry of Finance sold an estimated $85 billion of dollars for yen on July 30 and 31, while the New York Fed executed the euro-for-yen leg on Treasury's behalf through Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley.

"Treasury exchanged existing Exchange Stabilization Fund foreign-currency assets for yen," Bessent wrote in his response, dated Thursday. "No new congressional appropriation was involved, and no credit was extended to Japan. Japan owes Treasury nothing."

A swap, not a loan

That last line was aimed squarely at Warren's original letter, sent August 13, which warned that "American taxpayers would ultimately bear the cost if Japan were unable to repay" Treasury — a framing that treated the intervention as a loan rather than a straightforward asset swap. Bessent seized on the error, telling Warren she should "take any entry level course in international finance for her and her staff, or I can personally give her a tutorial on Foreign Exchange for Dummies."

Warren's underlying questions were less easily dismissed. Treasury did not notify the European Central Bank in advance that it intended to sell euros — a break from decades of coordination norms among Western central banks — and has still not disclosed the size of the yen purchase, the execution rate, or the position's current value. Warren also pointed to last year's $20 billion ESF-funded bailout of Argentina as evidence of an increasingly discretionary use of the fund, and Bessent's letter did not address the comparison.

Why it matters for positioning

For traders, the unresolved arithmetic matters more than the political theater. The yen strengthened to around 157 per dollar immediately after the July intervention but had drifted back to roughly 159 by mid-August, erasing about half its gains — a sign that reserve purchases alone are not holding the line against structural yen weakness tied to the rate gap between the Federal Reserve and the Bank of Japan. Bessent's own justification leaned on financial-stability logic rather than a currency target, warning that "disorderly yen markets can trigger forced unwinds, which could destabilize global markets and ultimately raise borrowing costs for American families and businesses." That reads as a hedge against yen carry-trade unwinds rather than a commitment to a specific exchange rate, which leaves open whether Treasury intervenes again if the yen retests its July low.

The next marker will be Treasury's quarterly ESF report, which typically discloses currency positions with a lag and could finally put a number on the yen holding Bessent has so far declined to size.
