BEYOND THE TAPE · HATE CRIME · By L. Bordeaux · 2026-08-29 · 4 min read

Four plead guilty over arson attack on Jewish community ambulances

The Old Bailey, London's Central Criminal Court, where the four defendants entered their guilty pleas. — Acabashi / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Four men admitted torching Jewish charity ambulances in a targeted London hate crime, exposing how thinly essential community services are protected.

Four young men stood in the dock at London's Old Bailey on Friday and admitted setting fire to ambulances — not military vehicles, not symbols of the state, but the plain white vans that carry the sick and injured to hospital. That the vans belonged to Hatzola, a volunteer Jewish ambulance service, was the point.

Hamza Iqbal, 20, Rehan Khan, 19, Judex Atshatshi, 18, and Saif Ali, 18, pleaded guilty to destroying or damaging property while reckless as to whether life would be endangered. Prosecutor Emma Harraway told the court it was "a premeditated and targeted attack against the Jewish community." A fifth man, Subhan Ahmed, 18, denies assisting an offender and goes to trial in January. Sentencing for the four is set for spring 2027.

What happened on Highfield Road

The attack itself took seconds. Just before 1:40am on 23 March, three of the men doused four Hatzola Northwest Trust ambulances parked outside Machzike Hadath synagogue in Golders Green with an accelerant and set them alight, while the fourth waited in a getaway car. Oxygen cylinders inside the vehicles exploded, shattering windows in nearby flats. Six fire engines and 40 firefighters took over an hour to bring the blaze under control. Three ambulances were destroyed outright, a fourth badly damaged, for a total bill of roughly £1 million. No one was hurt, though Golders Green — home to one of Britain's largest Orthodox Jewish communities — spent the following hours waking up to scorched metal outside a house of worship.

A Telegram channel calling itself Harakat Ashab al-Yamin al-Islamiya claimed the attack within hours. British authorities have since moved to proscribe the group, assessing it as an Iranian front designed to give Tehran deniability for attacks on Western soil — part of a pattern of alleged Iran-linked plots against Jewish and Israeli targets in Europe over the past two years. Defence lawyers for the four men pushed back on any formal terrorism link in Friday's hearing, and the charges brought were criminal damage rather than terrorism offences, but the hate-crime classification stood throughout the investigation.

A charity built to protect life, targeted anyway

Hatzola runs on volunteers who respond to medical emergencies in their own neighbourhood, often arriving before NHS ambulances thanks to sheer local density. Chief Rabbi Ephraim Mirvis called the attack "a particularly sickening assault" on an organisation "whose sole mission is to protect life, Jewish and non-Jewish alike." The government has since pledged funding to replace the destroyed vehicles, and the Community Security Trust — the charity that coordinates security for British Jewish institutions — said protection around synagogues and community services would stay at heightened levels well beyond the immediate aftermath.

That is the detail worth sitting with. Hatzola is not a bank, an embassy, or a government building with an obvious threat profile and a budget to match. It is a charity staffed by volunteers, and its vehicles sat parked overnight with the same casual vulnerability as any other fleet on a residential street. Community-safety groups have pointed to the case as evidence that low-profile, high-value civic infrastructure — ambulances, community centres, faith buildings — remains comparatively undefended even after years of rising documented antisemitic incidents in the UK.

What it signals beyond the courtroom

For those tracking social cohesion as a risk factor rather than a headline, the case is a small but legible data point. Convictions close a criminal matter; they do not repair the underlying exposure. Essential community services that operate on trust and low security budgets — ambulances, shelters, food banks, religious sites — are cheap to attack and expensive to protect, and the gap between those two numbers tends to widen precisely when regional tensions are already elevated. Insurers and local authorities assessing community and reputational risk in areas with visible minority populations have another concrete incident to weigh, on top of the broader pattern of anti-Jewish and anti-Israel-linked incidents recorded across UK cities since late 2023.

The next marker is January, when Subhan Ahmed's trial opens at the Old Bailey and more detail about the getaway and any wider network may surface. Sentencing for the four who pleaded guilty follows in spring 2027, and with it a clearer signal of how seriously the courts will treat attacks on services that exist, in the charity's own words, to protect life regardless of who needs saving.
