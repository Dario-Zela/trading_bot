WHO says DRC Ebola outbreak remains an international health emergency

Congo's Health Minister Roger Kamba administers an Ebola vaccine to a health care worker in Kisangani, 27 August 2026. — Samy Kamanga / AP
In one sentence
WHO's emergency committee says the Bundibugyo-strain Ebola outbreak in DRC still qualifies as a global emergency, with nearly 2,800 dead and Kisangani now vaccinating with a vaccine not licensed for this strain.

The World Health Organization's emergency committee has confirmed what the case curve already suggested: the Ebola outbreak tearing through the Democratic Republic of the Congo has not been brought under control, and the world's highest alert status for it stays in place.

Meeting on 18 August, the International Health Regulations committee found that the epidemic — caused by the Bundibugyo strain of Ebola, a rarer and less-studied relative of the virus behind the 2014–16 West Africa outbreak — continues to meet the criteria for a Public Health Emergency of International Concern. WHO Director-General Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus accepted that finding on 24 August and issued updated recommendations to member states. One committee member dissented from a separate, narrower question — whether the outbreak also meets the bar for a "pandemic emergency" — but the panel majority said it did not, for now.

As of 26–27 August, the Congolese health ministry had logged 5,794 confirmed cases and 2,786 deaths, a case-fatality rate of roughly 48%, meaning very nearly one in two people confirmed infected has died. The committee's own modelling suggests the true case count could be three to four times higher, given how much of eastern DRC is under-surveilled.

No sign of a turn

What troubled the committee most was not the raw numbers but the trajectory. When the outbreak was declared in mid-May, transmission was confined to three health zones in Ituri province. By August it had reached 55 zones across six provinces, with active transmission in 49 of them. Mortality data imply a doubling time of roughly three weeks. In its report, the committee described "the continuing rapid growth and geographic expansion of the epidemic" as being "of grave concern, requiring urgent, intensified and sustained action," warning that without a change in trajectory the outbreak "risks reaching a scale with profound national and regional consequences."

The response is under-resourced against that growth: the committee put treatment-bed capacity at around 1,000 against an estimated need of 3,000. Of 160 health workers infected so far, more than 40 have died. Compounding the difficulty, much of the worst-affected territory in Ituri sits amid active fighting among armed groups including the ADF and CODECO, which has repeatedly disrupted contact tracing and safe burials in past Congolese outbreaks.

A vaccine borrowed from a different virus

On 27 August, Health Minister Roger Kamba launched a vaccination drive in Kisangani, a city of more than a million people that has recorded cases despite lying outside the outbreak's Ituri core. "We have decided to use the Ervebo vaccine to protect first those who are on the front line," Kamba said.

The caveat worth stating plainly: Ervebo is licensed against the Zaire strain of Ebola, not Bundibugyo. The two viruses are related but distinct enough that protection cannot be assumed. WHO has authorised 70,000 doses for compassionate use — about 50,000 for health and frontline workers, with the remaining 20,000 earmarked for a clinical trial specifically testing whether Ervebo cross-protects against Bundibugyo. Separately, Oxford's Vaccine Group and Moderna have begun early human trials of a vaccine designed for the Bundibugyo strain itself, though any licensed product remains a long way off.

The committee is due to reconvene within three months, or sooner if the epidemiology worsens materially. Until a vaccine with confirmed efficacy against this strain exists, the outbreak's course will depend on how fast case-finding, isolation and safe burial can scale against a doubling time still measured in weeks, not months.
