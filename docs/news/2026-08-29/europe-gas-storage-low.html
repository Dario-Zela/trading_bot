Europe's gas storage at 13-year seasonal low as 'winter panic' talk builds

The Gate LNG import terminal at Rotterdam's Maasvlakte, a key entry point for the US and Middle Eastern cargoes Europe is counting on to refill storage. — Rijksoverheid / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
Gas storage is at a 13-year seasonal low and prices are up 120% this year, but the shortfall traces to a mild-winter LNG shock and hot summer, not a structural supply collapse.

European gas benchmark Dutch TTF futures have climbed roughly 120-130% since the start of the year, touching three-year highs above €68 per megawatt-hour in late August before easing back toward €65. Underground storage across the EU sits at about 63-64% of capacity — the lowest for this point in the season since at least 2013, and by some counts since 2009. Traders have started calling it a "winter panic." Whether that panic is proportionate to the underlying risk is a separate question.

Why the tank is running low

The proximate causes are specific and largely dateable. A colder-than-usual winter drew storage down further than usual heading into spring — EU inventories stood at just 28% capacity on April 1, below each of the prior three years. Then, six months into the Strait of Hormuz disruption tied to the Iran conflict (a standoff still running, as tracked elsewhere in this edition), Qatari LNG exports were largely knocked out of the market, removing a cargo stream Europe had counted on to help refill storage over summer. Record heat across Europe in June and July compounded it: drought curbed hydro output, high temperatures strained nuclear cooling, and gas-fired plants were run harder to cover the gap — burning exactly the molecules that were meant to be going into the ground.

SEB's Bjarne Schieldrop put it plainly: the market has "run into a bit of a winter panic over the past week." Oxford Economics' Daniel Kral was more measured, noting that "several adverse supply-side risks have materialised, and gas storage levels are historically low ahead of the heating season" — a statement about probability, not certainty.

The gap between the number and the risk

The headline comparison — to 2018, or 13 years, or two decades, depending on which tracker is cited — is doing more work than it should. The EU's storage mandate was itself relaxed this year, with the binding target cut from 90% to 80% and the deadline pushed out to December 1. At the current refill pace, storage is tracking toward roughly 78% by November 1 — short of even the loosened target, but not the yawning gap the seasonal comparisons imply. US LNG now supplies close to two-thirds of Europe's imports, and while American terminals are running near capacity and cargoes are increasingly diverted to higher-paying Asian buyers, that is a competition-for-supply problem, not an absence-of-supply one. Centrica's Chris O'Shea has warned the UK has "almost no gas in storage," but analysts including Kral and Goldman Sachs are explicit that no physical shortage is expected this winter — the argument is over price, not volume.

Goldman's own numbers illustrate the range of outcomes: it reckons prices would need to clear €100/MWh only if Middle Eastern export flows stay disrupted through winter, with a base case closer to €60/MWh. That is a wide band, and it hinges on Hormuz and Qatari output resuming — not on storage math alone.

What to watch

The EU's revised storage deadline of December 1 is the next hard checkpoint, followed by the first sustained cold snap, which will reveal whether withdrawal rates outrun the flexible LNG supply analysts are counting on. Either way, the number worth tracking is not the storage percentage itself but whether Qatari and Hormuz-linked flows start moving again — that, more than any seasonal comparison, will decide whether this becomes 2022 again or a pricier, but manageable, winter.
