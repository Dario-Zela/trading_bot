Strait of Hormuz blockade grinds into month six as Iran reports trade hit

The Strait of Hormuz, the 33km chokepoint between Iran and Oman that has been effectively closed since February. — Wikimedia Commons / CIA map, public domain
In one sentence
Six months into the Hormuz blockade, Trump pivots to sanctions as Iran and Oman negotiate a temporary shipping corridor with no ceasefire in sight.

Six months ago, President Trump described the opening strikes on Iran as a "little excursion." On 28 August the war marked its half-year anniversary with no ceasefire, no reopened strait, and an American strategy that has quietly swapped bombs for balance sheets.

The Strait of Hormuz — the 33-kilometre chokepoint between Iran and Oman that before February carried roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil and liquefied natural gas — has been effectively closed since the US and Israel struck Iranian targets on 28 February. Iran's Revolutionary Guard responded within hours, warning ships off the water by VHF radio and mining sections of the shipping lane. The US Navy says it has since cleared more than 100 suspected mines from the strait's traffic-separation scheme, but tanker owners have not returned in force: strikes on commercial vessels, including two UAE-flagged supertankers hit this summer, have kept crossings well below pre-war levels even during lulls.

A corridor, not a ceasefire

The closest thing to progress this month is a proposed "joint temporary navigational corridor," discussed by Iranian and Omani officials and reported by Al Jazeera and The Globe and Mail. Under the plan, inbound vessels would route entirely through Iranian territorial waters from the Gulf of Oman, with both countries then negotiating for 30 to 60 days toward a permanent route. Oman's foreign minister has said he is hopeful an announcement could come soon. But Iran has attached a political precondition: Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi said Washington must first "fully implement all of the commitments it has violated" before any reopening proceeds — a bar the US has shown no sign of clearing. In practice, the corridor talk is a workaround for a blockade, not an end to one.

The cost of that standoff shows up most plainly in insurance markets. War-risk premiums on Gulf transits, which ran around 0.25% of hull value before the war, have climbed as high as 7.5–10% in recent weeks, according to shipping-insurance data reported by The National and Al Jazeera — turning a routine $375,000 line item on a $150 million tanker into a bill of $1.5 million to $4.5 million per voyage. That cost gets passed through to freight rates, and from there into the energy and shipping benchmarks that price everything from jet fuel to container rates worldwide.

Washington's pivot to pressure

This week the Trump administration formalised what had been building for months: a shift from military escalation toward what Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has called "economic D-Day." Under the banner of "Operation Economic Outcast," the administration expanded secondary sanctions targeting any country or entity still doing business with Tehran, spanning digital assets, gold, aviation, technology and shipping. Trump has been working the phones directly with foreign leaders to press the point. Axios and CBS News both noted that the rollout landed with less immediate force than its framing promised — more a slow-burn sanctions campaign than a single decisive blow — but the intent is clear: squeeze Iran's remaining trade lifelines rather than open a new military front.

Iran's own numbers suggest the squeeze is working, if not conclusively. President Masoud Pezeshkian told state media this week that Iranian exports and imports have fallen by close to 35% under the combined weight of US sanctions and the naval blockade, and that critical imports — including gasoline — are struggling to reach the country at all. He noted Iran managed to sell around 90 million barrels of oil during a brief window in June when a short-lived US-Iran understanding permitted sales, a detail that undercuts the scale of the current shutdown by comparison. The 35% figure comes with no baseline year or independent verification, and Tehran has every incentive to dramatise the pain sanctions are causing. Independent data points in the same direction regardless: the IMF projects Iranian inflation at 68.9% for 2026, and Iran's own statistical centre reported July consumer prices up 87.9% year-on-year, with food inflation above 128%.

What it adds up to

None of the individual pieces — a partial shipping corridor, a sanctions campaign, a disputed trade statistic — amounts to a turning point on its own. Together they describe a war that has stopped looking for a knockout blow and settled into attrition, with Washington betting that financial isolation will eventually do what six months of strikes have not. That bet has a timeline of months, not weeks, which means the Strait of Hormuz is likely to stay a source of elevated oil-futures and shipping-insurance pricing well into the autumn. The Iran-Oman corridor talks, if they produce an announcement, are the nearest concrete marker to watch — a limited reopening would be the first tangible sign that any diplomatic track is functioning at all.
