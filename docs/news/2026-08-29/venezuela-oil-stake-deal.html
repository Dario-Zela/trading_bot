Trump claims 55% stake in Venezuelan oil venture as Pentagon vets local partner

The Puerto La Cruz refinery in Anzoátegui state, part of the Venezuelan oil infrastructure now central to Washington's claimed 55% stake. — Guaiquerí / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Trump claims a 55% US stake in Venezuela's 65bn-barrel oil reserves, but sanctions, legitimacy and a controversial middleman leave enforceability unresolved.

President Trump announced Friday evening that the United States has secured what a White House official described to CNN as "55% effective output of a new private joint venture" controlling more than 65 billion barrels of Venezuela's proven oil reserves — roughly 21% of the country's total and, on paper, the second-largest private oil company in the world by reserves. Trump called it "THE BIGGEST OIL DEAL IN WORLD HISTORY," struck, he said, "at no cost to the American taxpayer."

The number is real enough to move markets. Whether the arrangement behind it is real enough to survive contact with Venezuelan politics, US sanctions law and a Swiss prosecutor's office is a separate question — and one the announcement does not answer.

What was actually agreed

According to details relayed by administration officials, the 55% figure is not a straightforward equity stake. It splits into an ownership share plus a right to buy Venezuelan crude at cost, layered onto a new joint venture built with an as-yet-undisclosed private Venezuelan operator. Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth led the US side; on the Venezuelan side sat Delcy Rodríguez, the country's acting president since Nicolás Maduro's removal from power in January.

Rodríguez has granted the venture 100-year concessions over fields holding an estimated 63 billion barrels, spanning 17 sites in the Orinoco heavy-oil belt and around Lake Maracaibo. Rubio framed it as mutual benefit, promising "nearly $100 billion in private investment" and "thousands of high-paying jobs" for Venezuela's reconstruction; Caracas has floated a $209 billion state-tax projection over the concession's life. Chevron and Halliburton are reported to be involved on the operating side. Rodríguez, for her part, described the goal as consolidating Venezuela's position as an energy power and putting its reserves "at the service of national development."

The middleman problem

The deal's mechanics run through a second, less flattering story. Bloomberg reported this week that the Pentagon's Office of Strategic Capital — a body created in 2022 to channel private money into critical technology projects, not oil concessions — has been discussing a parallel investment structure with Alejandro Betancourt, a 46-year-old Venezuelan businessman who controls North American Blue Energy Partners and has positioned himself as Washington's principal fixer in Caracas.

Betancourt's history complicates that role. He co-founded Derwick Associates, which won roughly $5 billion in no-bid Venezuelan power contracts despite no prior sector experience; Transparency International has estimated the firm overbilled the Venezuelan state by $2.9 billion. He has faced money-laundering investigations in Switzerland, Spain and the United States tied to allegations that more than $1 billion was embezzled from state oil company PDVSA through a loan scheme — though he has not been charged in any jurisdiction. The Washington Post reported this week that senior US officials intervened with Swiss authorities to help close the criminal case against him and lift his travel restrictions, clearing the way for him to broker the oil arrangement. Separately, the OSC itself may lack the legal authority to take direct equity stakes of the kind now under discussion, a gap officials have not explained how they intend to close.

Whose signature actually counts

The deeper problem is that Rodríguez's government is not universally recognized as Venezuela's legitimate authority, and the sanctions architecture built around Maduro's PDVSA has not been formally unwound. Harvard economist Ricardo Hausmann has argued that "an illegitimate interim government... has no legitimacy to strike this unconstitutional deal," and legal analysts note that OFAC sanctions on PDVSA and the Venezuelan oil sector remain on the books; absent new Treasury authorizations, US and allied companies engaging in the sector risk penalties regardless of what the White House announces. A 100-year concession is only as durable as the government that signed it, and Venezuela's recent history is not a strong argument for continuity.

Markets shrugged, for now

Crude did not rally on the news. Brent slipped 0.4% to $89.38 a barrel and WTI fell 0.6% to $83.01 in the session after the announcement, with both benchmarks still down 4-5% on the week even after a Thursday rebound. That muted reaction reflects the same skepticism running through the legal commentary: traders have learned, over six months of the Strait of Hormuz blockade squeezing roughly a fifth of world oil flows, that geopolitical announcements about supply do not always translate into barrels reaching a port. US pump prices have held above $4 a gallon for the entire month of August regardless.

The test now is not the size of the number Trump announced but whether it survives the machinery required to make it real: an OFAC license, a Venezuelan succession that holds, and a Pentagon office that may need new legal authority just to hold the stake it has been promised. Watch for Treasury guidance on sanctions relief and any legal challenge to the concessions' validity — either would tell traders more about supply timing than the headline figure does.
