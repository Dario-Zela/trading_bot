NatWest looks to America for growth as UK banking competition bites

Paul Thwaite, NatWest Group's chief executive, whose bank has won Fed approval for a US representative office. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
NatWest won Fed approval for a small Connecticut office, a cautious first step back into the US as UK retail margins stay squeezed by deposit and mortgage competition.

NatWest has quietly reopened a door it shut more than a decade ago. On August 20, the US Federal Reserve approved the bank's application to open a representative office in Stamford, Connecticut — a small liaison outpost that cannot take deposits or write loans, but can market NatWest's products to American companies and support relationships with existing clients. It is a modest step. It is also the clearest signal yet of where the bank thinks its next decade of growth has to come from, and it isn't the UK high street.

The office builds on a broker-dealer NatWest already runs out of Stamford serving institutional clients, and will add a handful of relationship and credit staff. What makes it possible is a change to Britain's post-crisis ring-fencing regime that took effect in January 2025, which lifted restrictions barring ring-fenced UK banks from setting up branches or subsidiaries outside the European Economic Area. Those restrictions were written into law after 2008 specifically to stop banks like NatWest's predecessor, RBS, from taking the kind of overseas risk that nearly brought the group down. RBS had owned Citizens Financial Group in the US since 1988; it sold out entirely by 2015 as part of the state-backed clean-up following its £45.5 billion bailout.

Home-market arithmetic

The reason to go looking abroad again is straightforward: UK retail banking is a crowded, margin-thin business. NatWest's net interest margin has been recovering from the sub-2% lows of 2022 — it came in at 2.49% in the second quarter of 2026, up from 2.28% — but the bank has flagged that older, higher-margin lending is rolling off its books faster than new business is replacing the yield, while competition for savings deposits keeps eating into what it can charge. Mortgage pricing has told the same story all year: two- and five-year fixed rates from NatWest, Santander, Nationwide and Halifax have moved in near lockstep as lenders undercut each other for market share, most recently with lenders adding cashback incentives on top. It is a market where volume is available but pricing power is not.

Against that backdrop, NatWest arrives at its US ambitions from a position of relative strength rather than desperation. The bank returned to full private ownership last year, when the UK government sold its last remaining shares from the bailout stake, freeing it from the strategic constraints of state ownership. It has also been buying its way into higher-margin businesses at home, completing a £2.7 billion acquisition of wealth manager Evelyn Partners this year, and second-quarter operating profit rose 29% to £2.3 billion, comfortably ahead of forecasts. Management has since brought forward its next share buyback decision by six months. This is a bank redeploying surplus capital, not one retreating from a business in crisis.

Cautious company

NatWest is not alone in eyeing North America. Lloyds Banking Group — which has no equivalent overseas retreat to live down — has said it wants to grow North American operations to 30% of revenue in its corporate and institutional banking division by 2030. European rivals are testing the same water: Revolut applied for a US banking licence in March, while Wise and Bunq have both been turned away and Monzo abandoned its own application in 2021. The Trump administration's lighter-touch stance toward bank licensing has widened the opening, but the graveyard of failed applications is a reminder that regulatory approval is only the first hurdle.

NatWest's own history is the loudest cautionary tale in the room, which is presumably why the Stamford office is being built so deliberately small. A representative office generates negligible earnings on its own; it exists to build relationships and credit lines ahead of any decision to seek a fuller US banking licence. Whether that decision ever comes — and on what scale — is not something the bank has said publicly.

What to watch next is NatWest's full-year results, due early next year, where investors will be listening for any timeline or capital figure attached to the US push, and for management's read on whether UK margin pressure is stabilising or still building. Until a number is attached to the ambition, Stamford is a statement of intent rather than a strategy.
