Over 1,500 missing after Nepal-Tibet border floods as China restricts coverage

The Sino-Nepal Friendship Bridge at the Rasuwagadhi border crossing, photographed before it was destroyed in the 2015 earthquake — the crossing has now been devastated again. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
More than 1,500 people — including 33 Britons — are missing after Nepal-Tibet border floods, as China restricts reporting from the Tibetan side.

The number came first as a scale almost too large to picture: more than 1,500 people missing along a single stretch of Himalayan border. By Friday, as rescue teams picked through what was left of villages on the Nepal side and Chinese authorities added their own tally from Tibet, the combined figure had climbed past 2,900. Among the missing are 33 British nationals — trekkers, pilgrims and one family visiting relatives — swept up in a flash flood that has become both a humanitarian emergency and, in its retelling on either side of the frontier, a study in contrasts.

The flood struck the Bhotekoshi valley on Wednesday morning, after a magnitude-4.4 to 5.2 earthquake near Gosainkunda triggered an ice avalanche high in the mountains above the Nepal-China border. Debris blocked the Lhende river on the Tibetan side, forming a temporary lake that engineers now believe held back roughly 20 million cubic metres of water before it gave way. Nepal's disaster management officials have taken to calling what followed "a tsunami from the sky."

A wall of water on the roof of the world

The destruction runs across 40 to 50 kilometres of valley. Timure, a border town that served as the main crossing point for Nepal-China trade, has largely vanished from satellite imagery taken since the flood — customs buildings, hotels, restaurants and homes gone in the space of minutes. The Miteri Bridge, rebuilt only last year after an earlier flood destroyed its predecessor, is down again. On the Tibetan side, roughly three kilometres of the highway leading to Kyirong port have been washed out, and five telecommunications stations were knocked offline, cutting the area off from contact for a time.

Nepal's death toll has reached at least 626, with Tibet reporting a handful of confirmed fatalities — figures that Nepali officials expect to rise as rescue teams reach villages that have so far been unreachable by road. More than 3,000 people have been pulled out alive. Hanging over the recovery effort is a second danger: a new debris-blocked lake has formed upstream, and hydrologists are watching it for signs it too could burst.

Britons among the missing

Of the 33 UK nationals unaccounted for, many were travelling in organised groups on pilgrimage to Mount Kailash, the peak sacred to Hindus, Buddhists, Jains and followers of Bon that sits roughly 200 kilometres inside Tibet. The tour operator Alpine Eco Trek said ten of its clients — three men aged 52, 64 and 65, and seven women aged between 44 and 65 — were missing from one group, including Hema Amin, 63, from Croydon. A separate tour run by the Trekkers' Society lost contact with nine Britons, among them a 14-year-old boy travelling with his family.

Others were caught up for more personal reasons. Pramod Poudel, from Sussex, was searching for four family members — his wife, mother, brother and uncle — after his father was found alive; his relatives remain unaccounted for. Sarada Simkhada, a senior nurse at Darent Valley Hospital in Kent, was on the Kailash pilgrimage when the flood hit. Nabhi Das of Streatham and Leena Bector of Slough are also among the missing.

The UK has pledged £5 million in emergency assistance and is sending a rapid deployment team to Kathmandu to support consular efforts, though officials have cautioned that reaching some of the affected valleys — narrow, high-altitude gorges with roads now gone — will take time. The scale of the British contingent reflects how popular the Kailash route and the Langtang trekking region either side of the border have become; both draw thousands of foreign visitors every trekking season, mostly in the same narrow window of late-summer weather this flood arrived in.

Behind China's blackout

What is known about the flood's toll comes almost entirely from the Nepali side. Security-camera footage showing the wave tearing through the border crossing circulated briefly online before it was scrubbed from Chinese platforms; state television has broadcast a single still frame. Beijing has for years required foreign journalists to obtain permits before travelling to the Tibet Autonomous Region — a rule in place since 2007 — and reporters seeking access this week, including from the ABC, were turned down, with officials citing the difficulty of reaching the area and the risk of further disasters.

Chinese authorities have put Tibet's toll at a fraction of Nepal's: three confirmed dead and 558 missing, including 260 foreign nationals, with no breakdown of their nationalities offered. Ryan Fioresi of the International Campaign for Tibet said Beijing had relocated residents and barred journalists and independent observers from the affected area, leaving outside understanding of the disaster dependent on "official accounts" rather than survivors' testimony. Tencho Gyatso, also of ICT, drew the comparison directly: "While Nepal is providing timely information... we hope that, in Tibet, disaster relief efforts will be accompanied by timely and transparent information."

Nepal, by contrast, has published rolling updates naming tour operators, nationalities and even the ages of missing trekkers — the kind of detail that let British newspapers piece together who, specifically, their readers should be worried about. The asymmetry is not new; it mirrors how the two governments have handled cross-border floods in past years. But it is unusually stark this time, because the same event, the same river system, produced two entirely different information environments on either side of a mountain pass.

What happens next

The search will be shaped by geography as much as by politics. Rescue teams are working narrow, landslide-prone valleys where roads have been cut and helicopters are often the only way in; Nepal's army has appealed for more aircraft. A second flood, should the newly formed debris lake burst, would hit many of the same communities again before they have finished counting the first one's dead. For British families, the immediate marker to watch is whether the Foreign Office's Kathmandu team can reach the Kailash route in the coming days — and whether Beijing, under growing pressure from rights groups and foreign governments alike, allows any independent account of what happened on its side of the border at all.
