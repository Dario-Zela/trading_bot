Actors campaign for a UK law against AI voice cloning

Hugh Bonneville, one of more than 80 performers backing the campaign for a legal right to own one's voice. — Martin Kraft / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Over 80 British actors have asked PM Burnham to legislate a personal right to own one's voice, warning AI clones need just three seconds of audio to steal it.

Three seconds of audio is all a modern voice-cloning system needs. Feed it a clip from a podcast, an old advert, a voicemail, and it can generate a synthetic version of that voice saying anything at all — in any language, with no way for a listener to tell the difference. That is the premise behind a campaign that landed on Prime Minister Andy Burnham's desk this week: an open letter, signed by more than 80 British performers, asking for something no law anywhere has fully delivered — a personal, statutory right to own your own voice.

The letter comes from Save Our Voices Now, a campaign founded by the audiobook narrator Alice Sockett and the actor and comedian Peter Caulfield. Its signatories read like a cross-section of British film, television and comedy: Hugh Bonneville, Nicola Coughlan and Siobhán McSweeney of Derry Girls, Matt Lucas, Luke Evans, Niamh Cusack, Jen Brister and Pearl Mackie among them. Alongside the letter sits a petition on the government's own petitions site, asking ministers to legislate rather than merely study the problem.

In one sentence: More than 80 British actors are asking PM Burnham to give every person in the UK a legal right to own their voice, arguing three seconds of audio is now enough for AI to steal it.

A gap in the law that predates the technology

The practical problem is straightforward: UK law has no statute that specifically protects a person's voice from being cloned and reused without consent. Copyright covers a recording someone else made; it does not cover the underlying voice itself. Passing off and defamation exist but were built for different harms and rarely fit. Performers have contracts that govern how a recorded performance may be used, but a synthetic clone built from that recording, or from an interview, a radio clip, or a home video, sits outside anything they signed.

"In just three seconds, with the right AI system, your voice can be cloned, stolen and reinterpreted without your knowledge," Caulfield has said of the technology driving the campaign. Bonneville put the underlying claim more simply: "My voice is unique. It's mine. It's part of my identity."

That framing — identity, not intellectual property — is what makes the ask unusual. Performers' unions have spent the AI era negotiating over consent clauses, digital-replica terms and compensation in individual contracts. This campaign asks Parliament to skip past contract law altogether and grant a right that exists simply because a voice belongs to the person who has it, enforceable whether or not any recording, contract or copyright is involved.

Money, not just principle

The campaign's leverage comes as much from economics as from principle. Voiceover work — adverts, audiobooks, corporate narration, video-game dialogue — is a meaningful income stream for actors between screen and stage jobs, and it is also the segment of the industry most exposed to synthetic replacement, because a client only ever needed the voice, not the person. Equity Ireland's president, Gerry O'Brien, has described a member being congratulated for a voiceover they never recorded — a synthetic version of their voice had been used and paid for elsewhere. Equity in the UK says it is now handling more than 20 member claims over unauthorised use of their voices, and last year an indicative ballot found 99.6% of respondents unwilling to accept digital body-scanning without contractual guarantees attached.

The campaign also points to a wider public exposure. Save Our Voices Now cites a figure — echoed in separate banking-industry research — that 28% of UK adults report having been targeted by a voice-cloning scam, whether a fake call from a "relative" in distress or a cloned executive's voice used to authorise a fraudulent payment. That statistic is doing deliberate work in the letter: it reframes voice cloning from an actors'-union grievance into a public-safety question that touches every constituency, not just Equity members.

Denmark's head start

The campaign has a working model to point to. In mid-2025 Denmark proposed an amendment to its Copyright Act granting every citizen — not just public figures — rights over their own face, body and voice. The Danish proposal, which had cross-party backing and went through public consultation over the summer, would let anyone demand takedown of unauthorised AI content bearing their likeness, claim compensation regardless of reputational harm, and hold platforms liable if they fail to act; protection would extend 50 years after a performer's death. France, Italy and the Netherlands have long recognised versions of "personality rights" — French law has protected image rights since 1858 — while Britain has never given voice or likeness that standing.

The UK government's response so far has been to promise a consultation on how to address AI-related harms while "protecting legitimate innovation," the standard formulation ministers have used since generative AI became a legislative headache. For campaigners, a consultation is not a law, and the gap between the two is exactly the space in which cloning tools are being built, sold and used commercially right now.

What a UK right would actually change

If Parliament did legislate along the lines the letter proposes, the practical effect would be to shift the burden of proof. Instead of a performer having to show that a specific contract was breached or a specific reputation was damaged, they would only need to show the voice was theirs and the use was unauthorised — closer to how image rights work in France than to anything in existing UK copyright or personality law. That would matter well beyond acting: it would apply to anyone whose voice ends up in a scam call, a synthetic advert, or a dubbed clip they never recorded.

It would also put the UK ahead of the EU's AI Act, which requires labelling of AI-generated content but does not create an ownership right over a person's voice, and ahead of the US, where protection is a patchwork of state right-of-publicity laws with no federal standard. A British statute modelled on Denmark's approach would give voice-synthesis companies operating in the UK a clear compliance line to meet, rather than the current mix of contract disputes and reputational risk.

The test now is whether Burnham's government treats the letter as more than a talking point. The promised consultation has no published timetable; campaigners want a bill, not a discussion paper, and they are framing this as a closing window — the industry standard for voice AI is still being set, and once it hardens, retrofitting consent into it becomes far harder than building it in now.
