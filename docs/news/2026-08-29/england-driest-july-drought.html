CLIMATE · UK DROUGHT · By S. Whitfield · 2026-08-29 · 4 min read

England provisionally records driest July on record as drought spreads

Haweswater Reservoir in Cumbria, pictured at markedly reduced levels during the 2026 dry spell. — Ian Taylor / Geograph, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Seven English regions were declared in drought after the driest July on record, with reservoirs near 72% and falling and hosepipe bans spreading.

England's rain gauges told an unusually stark story this July: 6.5mm fell across the month, roughly a tenth of the long-term average and less than half the previous record low of 13.4mm set in 1911. The Met Office has provisionally confirmed it as the driest July since national records began in 1836 — and only the third driest month of any kind, behind February 1891 and June 1925. In southern England the figure was starker still: 1.9mm, about 3% of average, the driest calendar month the region has ever recorded. Nineteen counties saw a millimetre of rain or less across the entire month.

The consequence arrived on schedule. On 29 July the Environment Agency declared seven of its operational areas — covering just over half of England by land area — officially in drought: East Anglia, Hertfordshire and London, the Thames Valley, Hampshire and the Isle of Wight, Devon and Cornwall, the West Midlands, and Wessex. National reservoir storage had slipped to around 72% of capacity, roughly 7–8 percentage points below what is normal for late summer. Five reservoirs, including Wimbleball in Somerset and Hanningfield in Essex, were classified as exceptionally low for the time of year.

A second summer, a faster decline

What distinguishes this drought from recent memory is less the rainfall deficit itself than its speed. Reservoir storage fell 16% during July alone — the steepest single-month drop the Environment Agency has recorded in at least three decades — as a run of exceptional heat and the sunniest month England has ever measured pushed evaporation and demand up in tandem. Helen Wakeham, who chairs the Environment Agency's National Drought Group, described conditions as "an exceptionally serious situation" and noted that England is now in its second consecutive summer drought: "The hot and dry weather means we are currently using water faster than nature can replenish it." Water minister Emma Hardy struck a similar note, calling the pattern part of a "new normal" that infrastructure built for a different climate is not yet designed to absorb.

The drought has continued to widen since the July declaration. By 10 August, ten Environment Agency areas were in drought, covering 71.3% of England and putting more than 27 million people under some form of water-use restriction; reservoir storage had fallen further, to around 69%, and by mid-August to roughly 63%. Wessex Water introduced its first hosepipe ban in fifty years on 19 August — the company's first since the drought of 1976 — after demand across its region ran 30% above seasonal norms, equivalent to nearly half a million hosepipes running continuously. It joined seven other companies, including Thames Water, Anglian Water, Southern Water and South West Water, that had already imposed temporary use bans covering more than 23 million customers.

Farms, rivers and a regulatory squeeze

The strain is not confined to taps and gardens. In East Anglia, where the drought bites hardest, the Environment Agency has imposed Section 57 restrictions on 302 abstractors, forcing 50% cuts to river extraction, and more than 1,500 irrigation licences across the region are now curtailed. The National Farmers' Union says the earliest harvest in twenty years has come with reduced yields of wheat, spring barley, oats and grassland, while heat stress has already cut an estimated 18.5 million litres from national milk production. "Water is a vital ingredient in food production," said NFU deputy president Paul Tompkins, "and without a secure supply, we cannot build resilient farm businesses or strengthen the country's food security."

For water utilities, the drought lands in the middle of a regulatory reckoning. Ofwat's PR24 settlement, finalised in December 2024, commits the sector to £104bn of investment over five years, including nine new reservoirs and £2bn on interconnectors meant to move water between regions during exactly this kind of stress. None of that capacity exists yet — the earliest reservoirs are years from completion — which leaves companies managing this drought with the same pipe network, at roughly 9% leakage reduction achieved so far against ambitious targets, that struggled through 2022. A temporary use ban costs a utility little directly, but a hosepipe ban that fails to stop supplies to households, or restrictions that spill into a second season, invite the kind of public and regulatory scrutiny that has already cost the sector heavily on sewage and financial resilience.

What comes next

The Environment Agency's National Drought Group meets again in September to reassess whether more areas cross into drought status and whether abstraction restrictions need to widen further. Reservoir recovery now depends on autumn and winter rainfall returning to something like average — a below-average autumn would carry the shortfall into 2027 planting and supply planning. For now, the reference points to watch are straightforward: the weekly Environment Agency dry-weather bulletins, national reservoir storage (last near 63% and still falling in mid-August), and whether any of the ten water companies now under temporary use bans has to escalate to the more severe restrictions — standpipes or drought orders — that England has not seen since the 1976 drought Wessex Water's ban now explicitly recalls.
