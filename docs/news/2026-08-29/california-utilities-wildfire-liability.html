California lawmakers block Newsom bid to shield utilities from wildfire lawsuits

Governor Gavin Newsom, whose wildfire liability package was rejected by state lawmakers in the final days of the legislative session. — Office of Governor Gavin Newsom / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
California lawmakers rejected Newsom's plan to limit insurer lawsuits against utilities over wildfire damage, leaving PG&E and Edison's liability exposure unresolved.

California's investor-owned utilities went into the weekend with their wildfire liability exposure exactly where it was before Governor Gavin Newsom tried to change it — wide open. Democratic lawmakers in Sacramento walked away from closed-door talks late Thursday night without agreeing to the centrepiece of Newsom's plan: a measure that would have stopped insurance companies from suing utilities such as PG&E and Southern California Edison to recover what they pay out to policyholders after a power-line fire.

That right of recovery — known as subrogation — is the mechanism insurers use to claw back billions in wildfire payouts from the utility whose equipment started the blaze. Newsom's proposal would have curtailed it, alongside a cap limiting most pain-and-suffering damages to $150,000 for people in a burn perimeter who didn't lose a family member or suffer injury, and a rule capping what local governments could recover for destroyed infrastructure at depreciated value rather than the cost of rebuilding. Lawmakers rejected all three.

"Not going to negotiate with companies"

The governor's office framed the package as a trade: faster, guaranteed payouts for fire victims through a state-run fund, in exchange for narrowing the lawsuits that keep utility liability estimates — and insurance costs — unsettled for years after a fire. Newsom's staff also warned that without the changes, investor confidence in the utilities could erode further, feeding into higher customer bills.

Lawmakers weren't persuaded. State Senator Sasha Renee Perez said the Legislature was "not going to negotiate with companies that want to act like terrorists." Senator Ben Allen, warning about the subrogation carve-out specifically, said it would "create a massive new strain on the insurance system that could break basic questions of affordability for Californians." Consumer advocates, wildfire survivors and local governments lined up against the bill too, arguing it amounted to a liability bailout for PG&E, Edison and San Diego Gas & Electric. What lawmakers did keep from Newsom's package — tighter safety-violation fines, curbs on executive bonuses after a destructive fire, and funding for home-hardening — will move forward regardless.

The market registered the setback quickly. PG&E shares fell 7.4% to $16.61 on Thursday on volume nearly four times its three-month average, while Edison International dropped 4.8% to $70.15. UBS held its Buy rating and $22 price target on PG&E, arguing the door isn't fully shut — Newsom has suggested liability reform could still evolve in the months ahead even if this year's session ends empty-handed.

The stakes are not abstract. The Eaton Fire in January 2025 killed 19 people and destroyed roughly 9,400 structures; the Justice Department has since sued Southern California Edison over that fire and the 2022 Fairview Fire, seeking more than $77 million in damages, on top of the private claims already working through the courts. Every unresolved suit like it adds to the uncertainty investors are pricing into utility equity and into the premiums insurers charge across the state.

California's legislative session ends August 31. Newsom's office has signalled the fight isn't over even if nothing passes this week, which means the next marker to watch is whether a scaled-back version resurfaces in a special session — or whether the state's AB 1054 wildfire fund, already strained, becomes the venue where this liability question gets resolved by default.
