Judge rules Trump administration illegally retaliated against Anthropic over Pentagon ban

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth ordered the Pentagon boycott that a federal judge has now ruled unconstitutional. — Chad J. McNeeley / U.S. Department of Defense, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A federal judge permanently blocked the Pentagon's 'supply chain risk' boycott of Anthropic, ruling it was unconstitutional retaliation for the company's AI-safety guardrails.

A federal judge in San Francisco has told the Trump administration, in essence, that national security is not a magic word. On Thursday, U.S. District Judge Rita Lin issued a permanent injunction barring the Pentagon from enforcing a boycott it had ordered against Anthropic, the AI company behind the Claude chatbot, after finding the government punished the firm for refusing to drop safety restrictions on how the military could use its technology.

The 59-page ruling is unambiguous about what it thinks happened. The Department of Defense's decision to brand Anthropic a "supply chain risk" — a label historically reserved for firms tied to foreign adversaries — was not, Judge Lin found, a genuine security assessment. It was retaliation, dressed up in security language, for a company that said no.

"The empty invocation of national security is not a blank check to punish and retaliate against government critics."

How a $200 million contract turned into a blacklist

The dispute traces back to a deal that looked, on paper, like a Silicon Valley success story. In mid-2025, Anthropic became the first company to get a frontier AI model cleared for use on classified military networks, and it shared a two-year, $200 million contract with a rival to build tools for the Pentagon. Built into that agreement was Anthropic's acceptable-use policy: the military could use Claude broadly, but not for mass domestic surveillance of Americans or to power fully autonomous weapons that select and fire on targets without a human in the loop.

That arrangement held until the Pentagon — renamed the Department of War under Secretary Pete Hegseth — decided it wanted Claude available for "all lawful purposes," full stop, and pushed Anthropic to drop the two carve-outs. CEO Dario Amodei refused. "Frontier AI systems are simply not reliable enough to power fully autonomous weapons," he wrote in a public statement defending the position. "We will not knowingly provide a product that puts America's warfighters and civilians at risk."

The Department of War did not treat that as a negotiating position. It treated it as insubordination. On February 27, President Trump posted on Truth Social ordering federal agencies to "IMMEDIATELY CEASE all use of Anthropic's technology," with a six-month phase-out window for the Pentagon. Hegseth, who had reportedly summoned Amodei to a tense meeting days earlier and later called the company "sanctimonious," followed on March 5 with formal notice designating Anthropic a supply chain risk — a determination that bars defense contractors from using Claude in any military-related work, on pain of losing their own contracts.

The case for retaliation

Anthropic sued on March 9, and the case moved fast by federal court standards: Judge Lin granted a preliminary injunction that same month, the government lost a bid to lift it on appeal in April, and Thursday's ruling made the block permanent. Throughout, Lin's central finding stayed consistent — that the "supply chain risk" label wasn't really about supply chains.

Her opinion states the designation was driven by a desire "to make a public example out of Anthropic for its 'arrogance' in criticizing the government." That's a striking word for a court to seize on, because it concedes the government's own motive: not a security lapse, but wounded institutional pride at being told no by a vendor. Lin was similarly dismissive of the idea that Anthropic posed any actual sabotage risk, calling those concerns "entirely unfounded," and pushed back hard on the government's underlying theory of how a contractor is supposed to behave. "An IT vendor does not become a potential adversary," she wrote, "whenever it asks probing questions or stubbornly insists on particular contracting terms."

That line does a lot of work. It draws a boundary around what the government can and cannot do when a company it depends on refuses a request: it can walk away from the deal, but it cannot use a national-security designation — with its cascading effects on every other contractor who works with that company — as a cudgel. Lin grounded the ruling in two constitutional claims: that the boycott order violated the First Amendment by punishing Amodei's public advocacy on AI safety, which she described as consistent with his "longstanding practice of public speech" on the subject, and that it violated the Fifth Amendment's due process clause by imposing sweeping penalties without the procedural footing the law requires.

What the injunction actually blocks

The order is broad. It voids the supply chain risk designation itself, blocks enforcement of Trump's directive to federal agencies to drop Anthropic, and bars Hegseth's boycott order — meaning defense contractors are, for now, free to keep using Claude without risking their own Pentagon business. Anthropic called the designation unlawful and said it remains committed to working with the government on national security applications; the two safety carve-outs that started the fight were not challenged in the ruling and stay in place.

The administration isn't done fighting. Justice Department lawyers filed notice of appeal to the Ninth Circuit in April, and that case is still pending — meaning Thursday's permanent injunction, however forcefully worded, is not necessarily the last word. A reversal on appeal would revive the boycott and the underlying legal theory that a national-security label can be deployed against a domestic company for a policy disagreement rather than a demonstrated threat.

For now, though, the ruling sets a marker that will likely get cited well beyond this case: procurement power is not a lever the executive branch can pull to punish a company for the positions it takes in public. Whether that marker holds depends on what a Ninth Circuit panel — and, if it goes further, the Supreme Court — makes of Judge Lin's reasoning when the appeal is finally argued.
