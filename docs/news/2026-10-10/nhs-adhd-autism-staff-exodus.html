NHS faces exodus of ADHD and autism service staff to private clinics

In one sentence
Reports of NHS ADHD and autism specialists moving to private clinics could deepen already long waits, though the departure figures remain unverified.

A report that private clinics are drawing specialists away from NHS ADHD and autism services points to a risk the sector has worried about for some time. Diagnostic waits are already long enough to push patients towards paid care, and a loss of public-sector staff could lengthen them further. The report's headline figures could not be checked for this article. No count of departures, no period and no named trusts have been confirmed, so the claim stays unquantified here.

What can be said with confidence

The pressure on the service is well documented. The Guardian reported in January 2026 that the NHS is overspending by about £164m a year on ADHD services, with a growing share going to poorly regulated private assessments. The same coverage put the number of people awaiting an ADHD assessment above 500,000.

Autism waits are also long. The Nuffield Trust, using NHS data that runs to late 2023, found 172,000 adults and children on an autism assessment list, the highest figure recorded then. The median time on that list had risen above nine months. The think tank also reported that 24% of patients referred for ADHD in England were waiting one to two years. Those figures are now almost three years old, and a current national total could not be located.

Local accounts are bleaker, though less reliable. Coventry and Warwickshire's integrated care board has described typical adult ADHD waits of three to six years and children's waits of five to ten. That statement is undated and local, so it should not be read as a national picture.

Why a staffing drain would matter

Many patients have used the Right to Choose route, which lets an NHS referral be taken up by an independent provider at public expense. Some ICBs now say that capacity is capped. Hampshire and Isle of Wight ICB says many providers have reached the limit of what they can deliver, so some patients will not be seen for a new assessment this financial year. Leicester, Leicestershire and Rutland's January 2026 update says contact will in many cases come from April 2026 onwards.

If experienced clinicians are moving from NHS teams to private ones, the same people may end up working at both ends of the system. The public service would have fewer assessors, and the independent sector would take on more of the work. That would suit patients who can pay and would worsen the queue for those who cannot. This is an inference from the structure of the market. It is not a finding, and it depends on departure numbers that remain unconfirmed.

There is a counter-current. A trust has reported that ADHD waiting lists are being clogged by patients returning to the NHS after problems with private assessments. Some of those assessments do not follow National Institute for Health and Care Excellence guidance, or the provider lacks staff qualified to continue prescribing. Some patients have faced prescription costs above £200 a month after GPs withdrew from shared-care arrangements with private clinics. The flow between sectors therefore runs in both directions.

What to watch

The government's position is that the NHS medium-term planning framework, published on 24 October 2025, sets expectations for ICBs to use existing resources to cut ADHD waits over three years. Whether that is achievable depends on retaining the clinicians who do the assessments. The data that would settle the matter are NHS England's monthly mental health statistics and workforce figures for the specialist teams. Those would show whether departures are measurable and whether waits are lengthening as a result.
