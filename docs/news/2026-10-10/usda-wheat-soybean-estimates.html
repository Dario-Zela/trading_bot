USDA puts 2026/27 wheat crop at 1.534bn bushels, soybean end stocks at 315m

In one sentence
USDA's reported 2026/27 estimates — 1.534bn bushels of wheat, 315m of soybean ending stocks — are small revisions that, on current evidence, do not show a food-inflation effect.

The US Department of Agriculture's latest crop estimates put the 2026/27 American wheat crop at 1.534 billion bushels and soybean ending stocks at 315 million bushels, according to a Benzinga report citing the department. The figures are routine balance-sheet updates. They are the numbers grain traders, millers and commodity desks reprice from, but they are not by themselves a story about food prices.

What the numbers say

The wheat figure sits close to where the department has been. Its July outlook had all-wheat production for 2026/27 at 1.536 billion bushels, so 1.534 billion implies a trim of roughly 2 million bushels, or about 0.1%. That is small against a crop of this size. The September report left new-crop wheat estimates and US ending stocks (717 million bushels) unchanged from August.

The soybean figure is a larger move. September's report put new-crop ending stocks at 310 million bushels, down 10 million from August, alongside a production forecast of 4.535 billion bushels. A 315 million estimate would therefore be a 5 million-bushel increase, if it is measured against the same series. Ending stocks are the cushion left when the marketing year closes, and a rise slightly loosens the balance sheet.

These comparisons rest on secondary reporting. The October release itself was not available to check at the time of writing, and the Benzinga numbers have not been matched against the department's World Agricultural Supply and Demand Estimates (WASDE) tables. They should be read as reported, not confirmed.

The inflation question

The discovery pitch for this item tied the estimates to food-price inflation, with grain markets already strained by energy costs. Nothing supplied with the figures supports that link. No price reaction, futures move, or wholesale or retail food-price data accompanied them, and revisions of this size rarely feed through to consumer prices on their own. Energy does matter to grain: diesel, fertiliser and freight are real input and transport costs. That pressure is the subject of other coverage in this edition, including the Gulf of Mexico shut-ins and the US licensing of Russian diesel. But a trim to a wheat estimate and a small lift to soybean stocks do not show it.

What to watch

The test is the market's response: whether Chicago wheat and soybean futures moved on the release, and whether the full WASDE tables confirm the Benzinga numbers. The next monthly WASDE, due in November, will show whether the revisions held. Evidence on food inflation would have to come from the consumer price index's food components, not from the crop estimates.
