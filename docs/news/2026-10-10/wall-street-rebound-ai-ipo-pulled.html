Wall Street rebounds from AI scare after Nvidia-backed data-centre firm scraps IPO

In one sentence
Nvidia-backed Firmus withdrew a roughly US$5bn IPO on weak demand and OpenAI revenue doubts hit AI stocks, before the Nasdaq 100 rebounded on Friday.

A data-centre operator backed by Nvidia has withdrawn a planned stock-market listing, and the episode gave Wall Street a reason to reconsider how much it will pay for the AI build-out. Firmus, an Australian AI data-centre company, shelved an initial public offering that Reuters-based reports put at about US$5bn. AI-linked shares sold off. The Nasdaq 100 then recovered during Friday's session, 9 October.

The rebound was broad, but the withdrawal is the more informative event. A market that is discounting AI infrastructure will show it first in new issues, where the price has to be negotiated with investors. Existing stocks reprice every second.

A listing that did not clear

Firmus had been preparing an Australian listing. Reports differ on its size, and on the valuation sought. One account cites an intended equity valuation of US$30.6bn, against about US$10.5bn after an August private round. Another outlet reports a figure of roughly A$44bn and a late price cut to A$8.25. The price-cut detail appears in only one source and is unconfirmed here, and the valuation figures should be treated with caution.

The common thread is weak demand. Reuters-based coverage said the company had only two centres operating and no track record of building AI data centres at scale. One account says investors pulled orders after escrow terms would have let existing holders sell more than half their stock at listing.

Firmus said the offer terms did not reflect the strength of its business or its long-term growth outlook. It will pursue private fundraising instead. Its backers include Nvidia, Coatue Management, Blackstone and Jane Street.

The point matters beyond one company. Private rounds can be struck at valuations that public buyers will not accept. When a deal built on those marks fails to clear, the gap between the two prices becomes visible.

The OpenAI question

The withdrawal landed against a backdrop of doubt about OpenAI. The Wall Street Journal reported that OpenAI has missed internal targets for revenue and users. According to that report, finance chief Sarah Friar has told colleagues that slower revenue growth could leave the company unable to honour future computing contracts. Oracle, a large supplier of capacity to OpenAI, fell more than 6% on the report. OpenAI's executives responded that they were "totally aligned on buying as much compute as we can".

The link to Firmus is inferential. OpenAI is a very large buyer of data-centre capacity, and a smaller operator's revenue case depends on customers like it continuing to spend. If OpenAI's growth is slower than its commitments assume, the demand behind a new operator's valuation looks less certain. No source ties Firmus's decision directly to the OpenAI reports, and the brief for this piece gave no index levels or percentage moves for the sell-off.

The rebound

Friday's session recovered the ground. Nine of the 11 S&P 500 sectors finished higher, with real estate leading. The Nasdaq 100 recovered during the session, though the size of the move was not available at the time of writing.

One stock moved against the tide. T-Mobile shares fell 12%. No cause has been established, and nothing in the available reporting connects the fall to the AI sell-off. It is best read as a company-specific event until a reason emerges.

A one-day rebound is weak evidence of anything. The sectors that led were not AI-exposed, which suggests the recovery was broad rather than a verdict on AI valuations. It does not undo the signal from the primary market.

What to watch

The test is whether other AI infrastructure issuers can still price deals. Firmus's pivot to private funding shows that capital remains available to it, but at terms set by a smaller group of investors. If further listings are delayed or cut in size, the funding path for data centres narrows and the public valuations of listed peers face more scrutiny. Further reporting on OpenAI's revenue against its compute commitments, and any confirmed explanation of T-Mobile's fall, are the next data points. Confirmed index levels for Friday's rebound would help judge how much of the AI scare was actually priced in.
