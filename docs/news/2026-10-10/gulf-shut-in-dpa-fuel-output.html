US FUEL SUPPLY · By M. Halloway · 2026-10-10 · 4 min read

Gulf of Mexico shuts in about 72% of oil output as White House turns to Defense Production Act

In one sentence
The offshore regulator reports about 72% of Gulf oil output shut in, probably storm-related, while the White House prepares a Defense Production Act push to lift fuel supply as diesel prices rise.

The US Gulf of Mexico has lost most of its oil output, and the White House is reaching for wartime powers to raise fuel supply elsewhere. Neither development is yet fully documented. The federal offshore regulator reports that about 1.5m barrels a day of Gulf oil production is shut in, roughly 72% of the region's total, along with 58.8% of its gas output. Separately, the administration is reported to be preparing a directive that would use the Defense Production Act (DPA) to override state and local rules and lift domestic fuel production, with diesel prices as the trigger.

The two items arrive together, and they pull against each other. One removes barrels from the market. The other tries to add them.

What the Gulf figures say

The 72% figure comes from the Marine Minerals Administration, as relayed by Benzinga. The triage material did not give a cause or a date, and no primary release was available to check. The surrounding record points strongly to weather. Reuters reported on 7 October, citing the same agency name, that producers had shut in about 25% of Gulf oil output and 16.4% of gas output as Tropical Storm Isaias approached. Personnel had been evacuated from eight production platforms, 2.16% of the Gulf's 371 manned platforms. Shell said it was pulling staff and halting production at Mars, Olympus, Ursa, Vito and Appomattox. Chevron began shut-in procedures at four facilities.

If the later 72% reading is the same storm at a later stage, the shut-in nearly tripled in a few days. That is plausible, since these counts tend to climb as operators evacuate ahead of landfall. It is an inference, though, not a confirmed link. No restart or damage assessment for Isaias has been found.

The figure is large but not unprecedented. The regulator's predecessor reported 72.53% of Gulf oil shut in during Hurricane Ivan in 2004. The Bureau of Safety and Environmental Enforcement reported 72.82%, about 1.38m barrels a day, during Hurricane Barry in 2019. Both were temporary. Production returned over days to weeks, depending on platform damage and pipeline and port access.

That history is the main guide to market impact. A storm shut-in of this size is a timing shock. Crude and gas that were not produced this week are mostly produced later, unless facilities are damaged. A structural loss would be different, and nothing in the available reporting suggests one.

Washington's response

The second thread is policy. Per Benzinga and BBC reporting, the White House plans to direct department heads to override state and local regulations and to use the DPA to increase oil and fuel production. The reported trigger is rising diesel pump prices. The plan is described as a directive in preparation, not a signed order, and its text has not been confirmed.

The legal groundwork exists. In April the president signed DPA memoranda covering petroleum production and refining, coal-fired power and natural gas pipelines. A Justice Department Office of Legal Counsel opinion asserts that DPA orders can pre-empt state and local environmental law. In August a federal court in California declined to block an emergency order restarting Sable Offshore's Santa Barbara pipeline over the state's objections, saying the law gives the administration broad discretion. One report says refineries are already running at about 98% of capacity, with diesel above $6 a gallon.

That last point matters for what the DPA can do. Overriding permitting rules can speed new projects, but it cannot add refining capacity this quarter. It also does little for a shortfall caused by evacuated platforms.

The rig count

The drilling data show a modest response. Baker Hughes reports the US oil rig count rose by six to 462, with the total at 603. A six-rig weekly gain is routine noise. The source ties it to higher prices and political pressure, but offers no quantified link, and rigs added now affect output only after a lag of months.

What to watch

Three things will settle the shape of the story. First is the regulator's next daily shut-in update, which will show whether the 72% figure is peaking and how quickly platforms are re-staffed. Second is whether the DPA directive is signed and what it says about pre-empting state rules, which would determine whether it is a production measure or mainly a political signal. Third is next week's Baker Hughes count, to see whether the six-rig gain was the start of a trend. If the Gulf comes back within the usual window, the lasting effect will be on diesel and policy, not on Gulf supply.
