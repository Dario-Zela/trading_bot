Nikon rules prize-winning image that sparked backlash was AI-generated

In one sentence
Nikon disqualified the winner of its Small World In Motion contest, a microscope video of a child's airway cells, for breaching its generative AI rules after scientists challenged it.

A video that won first place in Nikon's Small World In Motion competition has been disqualified for breaking the contest's rules on generative AI. The award, for a microscope clip of a child's airway cells, drew complaints from microscopy specialists within days of the announcement. On 9 October Nikon said the entry "did not comply with the competition rules regarding generative AI". The headline describes a prize-winning image, but the entry was moving footage. That detail matters, because the contest exists to reward what a microscope actually recorded.

What was awarded, and what unravelled

Small World In Motion honours photographs and video taken through a microscope, and has been running for 15 years. The winning entry came from Ning Xu, an optical engineering researcher at Tsinghua University at the time of filming. It showed hair-like cilia, magnified 100 times, in the airways of a child with primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD), a rare genetic disease.

Scientists, some of them former judges of the contest, started questioning the footage on social media. They also raised it with Nikon on its LinkedIn page. Edward Phelps, an associate professor at a Florida university, wrote in a comment on a Nikon post, since deleted, that the nuclei "do not behave as nuclei" and that the cilia "appear from nowhere and do not match the known size".

Robert Hirst of the University of Leicester, a PCD specialist, said the cells and cilia "look nothing like those from PCD patients". He added that the controversy "has impacted a lot of patients, scientists, doctors and PCD support networks around the world".

The harm was to a disease community

The fuss over AI in photography contests usually turns on fairness to other entrants. This case differs in that the supposed subject was a real condition. A microscopy community open letter warned that "a potentially misleading portrayal of PCD is shaping public understanding of an already poorly understood rare disease", and asked for the winners' work to be examined externally.

That is a different problem from the one raised in 2022, when a still AI image won at the Colorado State Fair and its maker said he had broken no rules. A fake landscape deceives nobody about the world. A synthetic video of diseased cells can mislead patients and clinicians who rely on what such footage shows.

Nikon's response

Nikon framed the decision as one about eligibility. It said it was not "a judgement of the entrant's professional reputation or scientific contributions". It also said it would review the contest's rules and procedures. The company has not said, in the coverage reviewed, how the entry passed judging in the first place. Nor has it said what detection or verification steps it will add.

The top spot has gone to Nguyen Nam Nhat, a Vietnamese researcher whose video of a microscopic roundworm meeting a single-celled organism called a Dileptus had placed second. First prize is US$3,000.

What to watch

The obvious gap is how the contest checks entries. In this case, outside experts did the checking, after the prize was announced. The review Nikon has promised is the next marker. Contests could require raw files or acquisition metadata, or ask specialist judges to scrutinise entries before awards are announced. Whether Nikon publishes the outcome of its review, and whether it commits to any of those measures, will show whether this was a one-off embarrassment or the start of tighter rules across photography prizes.
