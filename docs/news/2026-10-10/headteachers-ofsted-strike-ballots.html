Headteachers threaten strike ballots over Ofsted's graded inspections

In one sentence
The NAHT head teachers' union wants graded Ofsted inspections halted, citing wellbeing harm, but a formal strike ballot has not been confirmed.

Headteachers' leaders are pressing the government to stop graded school inspections in England. The dispute has a history of escalation, but the evidence for an imminent strike ballot is thinner than the threat suggests.

The framing in some coverage needs a correction. Ofsted no longer issues the single-word judgments ("outstanding", "inadequate") that long defined the inspectorate. They were replaced a year ago by report cards that grade several areas of a school separately. The complaint now is that the replacement is no gentler.

What the union is asking for

The National Association of Head Teachers (NAHT) surveyed more than 5,000 people. Nearly three-quarters of school leaders said the direction of travel on inspections had worsened over the past year. The union wants the government to halt graded inspections while the system is reviewed, and to replace the colour-coded grades with a short narrative report on strengths and areas for development.

Paul Whiteman, the NAHT's general secretary, called the new framework "even more stressful" than the old one. He said it "poses a real and present danger" to the wellbeing and mental health of leaders and teachers, "possibly even a risk to life". The union has sent examples of members' "damaging experiences" to the education secretary, Lucy Powell.

The union's evidence is partly anecdotal. Amanda Hulme, head of Claypool Primary School in Bolton, described losing sleep, raised blood pressure and a pulse rate she linked to an inspection under the new framework. She also considered early retirement. Other leaders criticised a "secure fit" approach as box-ticking that limits inspectors' judgement, and said the new wellbeing questions "seem purely perfunctory".

From court to industrial action

The NAHT has already tried other routes. It brought a legal challenge against the report-card plans, backed by two other unions, and the High Court dismissed it last November. Whiteman then said the union would consider an appeal and consult members on industrial action. At an online meeting of nearly 2,000 members, 89% agreed the union should consider industrial action options if the proposals went ahead.

That is a mandate to explore action, not a ballot. The search for this article found no confirmation that a formal ballot on inspection has been called, and no timetable. Any such ballot would also face the legal thresholds on turnout and support that apply to strikes in England. The union's wider dispute covers pay, funding, workload and recruitment as well as inspection, which makes it harder to say what a ballot would be about.

The government's position

Ofsted has said it will keep engaging with unions but will continue to grade schools. A spokesperson said it exists to raise standards and give parents clear reporting, and that it consulted parents on the new reports. Whiteman welcomed Powell's stated concern for leader wellbeing and her aim of reforming accountability, which suggests the union sees room to negotiate.

The next signal is whether Powell's department commissions a review of how graded inspection works. If it does, the NAHT has a reason to hold off. If it doesn't, the union will have to decide whether to put industrial action to a formal vote.
