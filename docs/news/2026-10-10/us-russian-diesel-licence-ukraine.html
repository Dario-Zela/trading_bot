RUSSIAN DIESEL · By C. Tanaka · 2026-10-10 · 5 min read

US licenses Russian diesel imports after Trump–Putin call; Zelensky calls it a gift to Putin

In one sentence
Trump says Russia will ship 300,000+ tons of diesel after a Putin call, and Treasury's licence through April 2027 makes it policy; Zelensky calls it a gift to Putin.

The United States has loosened its sanctions on Russian diesel, and it has done so in writing. Donald Trump announced on Truth Social on 9 October that, after a phone call with Vladimir Putin, Russia would put Russian diesel on US and global markets. Treasury then issued a licence to match. Volodymyr Zelensky, whose country is still negotiating an end to the war, called the move a gift to Putin. Washington, in effect, has traded a sanctions lever on Russian energy revenue for a hoped-for easing in fuel prices.

What Trump announced

Trump's post said Russia would supply more than 300,000 tons of diesel "immediately", then 500,000 tons during November and 1 million tons "immediately thereafter". Reports of the post add that a further 3 million tons would follow, depending on the condition of Russia's refineries. Those figures are the president's own, and Moscow has not published a schedule of its own. The Kremlin said only that Russia had confirmed its readiness to supply oil and petroleum products to the US and global markets.

The volumes are unverified, and several outlets put the call on Thursday and others on Friday. The licence is a different matter. Reporting on the Treasury action describes OFAC General License 135, which authorises transactions related to the sale, delivery, offloading or importation of diesel of Russian origin until 7 April 2027. I could not find the OFAC notice itself in a search, so the licence number and expiry rest on press accounts. Democratic senators described the authorisation as lasting six months and covering global purchases, which is consistent with that date.

The licence is what turns the post into policy. A president's social-media claim can be walked back. A general licence, which any buyer can rely on without applying for permission, changes what shippers, traders and refiners can lawfully do, and it stays in force until Treasury revokes it or it expires.

Why Washington is doing it

The fuel market explains the timing. Diesel prices have spiked this year, and the administration has been looking for supply. Reports tie the pressure to the US war with Iran, while elsewhere in this edition the Gulf of Mexico has shut in about 72% of its oil output and the White House has turned to the Defense Production Act. Newsweek framed the deal as an effort to bring prices down ahead of the midterm elections.

There is also a precedent. Treasury's GL 134 series, introduced in the spring over disruption linked to the Strait of Hormuz, allowed transactions in Russian crude and products already loaded on vessels, while leaving the wider sanctions on the Russian energy sector in place. GL 135 goes further. It is not limited to cargoes already at sea, and it is specific to a fuel Moscow is actively trying to sell.

Whether it can deliver is less clear. Russia banned most diesel exports by fuel producers in July after Ukrainian drones hit several large refineries, and it has extended the ban through 31 October, citing problems in its domestic market. Deputy Prime Minister Alexander Novak has said exports could partly reopen if production exceeds demand. Putin has said Russia has enough diesel but that sanctions stop it reaching global markets. A European diplomat quoted in the reporting doubted that the deal would move prices much, saying "the symbolism will have a real impact". The EU and UK import bans and the $100-a-barrel product price cap, according to reports, remain in force, which limits where the fuel can legally go.

Kyiv's objection

Zelensky wrote that "gifts to Putin will not bring peace or any benefit to the civilised world", and that Russia "will repay the diesel with further terror and perfidy". He called it "a weak decision by our strong partners" and said allowing Russia to sell petroleum products was an investment in a war that must be ended, not prolonged. He also warned that Ukraine's negotiating team, which was in Florida for two days of talks with US officials, risked being used as a "smokescreen".

The objection has a specific edge. Ukraine has spent months striking Russian refineries, and those strikes are the reason Russian diesel is scarce. Zelensky has said Kyiv would stop hitting refineries if Russia stopped attacking Ukraine's energy infrastructure, and that Ukraine proposed that exchange to Washington. Putin called a proposed swap of Black Sea ship strikes for refinery strikes "absurd". Moscow is reported to be seeking sanctions relief as the price of any energy truce. From Kyiv's side, the licence gives Russia a concession before any truce has been agreed.

In Washington, Senators Jeanne Shaheen, Chuck Schumer and Elizabeth Warren called the authorisation "a total betrayal of Ukraine, our European allies and American national security". They said it funds Russia's war and undercuts recent legislation on Russian energy revenues. CNBC reported that the deal has been accused of contradicting Russia sanctions law, though the legal arguments are not set out in the accounts available.

The talks it sits inside

The diesel decision landed in the middle of a diplomatic round. US envoy Steve Witkoff described the Ukraine talks as "productive", and participants pledged to continue diplomacy and identified areas for further exploration. No agreement was named. The one concrete item on the table was the diesel concession, and that is the item Kyiv is wary of.

That sequence matters for how the licence is read. Washington presents it as a supply measure and a price measure. Kyiv reads it as sanctions relief handed over while the negotiation is still open, which weakens a lever Ukraine's backers had held in reserve for a settlement. The administration has not said what it expects Moscow to give in return beyond the fuel itself.

What to watch

Three things will show what the licence is worth. The first is whether Russia lifts its diesel export ban when it comes up for renewal after 31 October, since without that the 300,000 tons and the November cargo would have to come out of limited stocks. The second is whether any of the pledged volumes show up in tanker tracking and US diesel prices in November. The third is whether Congress tries to challenge the licence under the sanctions statute, as the senators' statement suggests, and whether the EU and UK hold their own bans. The licence runs to 7 April 2027, so Washington has left itself room to renew it, or to revoke it if the diplomacy stalls.
