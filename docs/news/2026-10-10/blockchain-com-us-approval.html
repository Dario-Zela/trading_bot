Blockchain.com seeks US approval for prediction markets and crypto derivatives

In one sentence
Blockchain.com has applied to the CFTC for exchange and broker licences to offer US prediction markets and crypto derivatives, per CNBC; no decision has been reported.

Blockchain.com, one of the older names in crypto, has applied to the US Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) for licences that would let it offer prediction markets and crypto derivatives to American customers. The application was first reported by CNBC. The CFTC has not commented publicly, and no decision has been reported.

What the company is asking for

According to coverage of the CNBC report, Blockchain.com is seeking two authorisations. The first is designated contract market (DCM) status, which would let it run its own exchange listing event contracts — the yes/no instruments on which prediction markets are built. The second is registration as a futures commission merchant (FCM), which would let it act as a broker for derivatives contracts. The products would be aimed at US retail and institutional customers.

Neither the filing date nor any volume figures have been reported, and the secondary coverage rests on CNBC's account rather than a regulator's statement.

Moving onshore

Blockchain.com is not starting from scratch. It already offers prediction markets through a partnership with Polymarket, and perpetual futures powered by Hyperliquid, to selected international customers. Polymarket's international exchange remains closed to US users; its American business operates separately as a CFTC-regulated venue. Chief executive Peter Smith is reported to want to fold these services into a single platform. A US licence would give that platform a domestic home rather than an offshore one.

The company is also reported to be weighing a stock-market listing at a valuation of up to $6bn, raising about $500m. That report is unconfirmed here, but a regulated US business would sit more easily in an IPO prospectus than a geoblocked one.

The filing follows a similar move by Binance.US, which said it would seek a DCM licence from the CFTC. The pattern suggests large crypto platforms see a regulated route as worth the cost of compliance, though two applications are a thin basis for a trend.

A contested regulatory backdrop

The route is not settled. CFTC chair Michael Selig has described well-functioning prediction markets as "truth machines", and he is reported to be the agency's sole commissioner, so the decision rests with a single official. States are less welcoming: more than 11 have issued cease-and-desist orders against prediction-market operators, and litigation over whether federal or state authority governs these contracts is continuing. A CFTC licence would not by itself resolve that dispute.

What to watch

The immediate test is whether the CFTC acts on the DCM and FCM applications and how quickly. Beyond that, the questions are whether state challenges reach the courts in a way that narrows federal approval, and whether Blockchain.com's reported IPO plans proceed. This is one application, not a rule change; its weight depends on the regulator's answer.
