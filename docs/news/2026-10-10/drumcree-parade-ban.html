Chris Bryant bans Drumcree parade, risking loyalist backlash

In one sentence
Northern Ireland Secretary Chris Bryant banned the Orange Order's Drumcree march on 9 October at the police chief's request, overriding the Parades Commission and leaving unionists demanding answers.

The Northern Ireland Secretary, Sir Chris Bryant, banned an Orange Order march along Portadown's Garvaghy Road on Friday 9 October. That ended a stand-off that had lasted almost two weeks. It reverses a determination by the Parades Commission, the statutory body that normally rules on contested parades. The Orange Order is weighing a court challenge, and unionist leaders are demanding that the government explain itself.

The ministerial role is unusual, so it needs saying plainly. Under the Public Processions (Northern Ireland) Act 1998, contested parades are decided by the Parades Commission, not by ministers. The reports reviewed for this article do not name the statutory power Bryant used. They say only that the PSNI chief constable asked him to review the Commission's determination, and that he then banned the procession on public-interest grounds.

A dispute older than the settlement

Drumcree is a church on the edge of Portadown. For the Orange Order, its annual service has always ended with a march home along the Garvaghy Road, which is now a largely Catholic nationalist area. Residents have opposed that for decades. The 1996 stand-off there was one of the most dangerous episodes of the Troubles' last years. The Commission has banned the Garvaghy section since 1998.

That made September's decision startling. After a High Court ruling in late August that quashed an earlier ban for a procedural error, the Commission approved a limited march. It allowed 35 Orangemen to walk at 8am on Sunday 27 September, with no band and no supporters. That would have been the first Orange parade down the road in almost 30 years. The DUP called it a "significant breakthrough". Sinn Féin's Michelle O'Neill condemned it.

A residents' coalition, backed by Sinn Féin, the SDLP and People Before Profit, challenged the decision. It lost in the High Court, which upheld the determination in an unprecedented late-night sitting. The Court of Appeal later reserved judgment, saying it needed more time. Meanwhile protesters blocked the road, and the parade did not proceed. Orangemen remained at Drumcree.

The Secretary steps in

Pressure on Bryant came from one side. Sinn Féin's Mary Lou McDonald and O'Neill urged him to intervene, and O'Neill told him to "do his job". A group of 25 Labour MPs and peers wrote asking him to use his powers to overturn the Commission. Bryant hosted talks at Hillsborough Castle, where options included mediation and a "one-and-done" final march. They ended without agreement.

The trigger came on the day itself. According to reports, PSNI Chief Constable Jon Boutcher asked Bryant to review the determination. Boutcher said "all available, lawful and proportionate options to facilitate completion of the procession had been exhausted". He warned of "an unacceptable risk of serious injury, disorder and a wider contagion effect across Northern Ireland".

Bryant said: "I've decided that it's not in the public interest for it to proceed, and I've therefore banned the procession." He added: "We've been talking it through, trying to find a solution, and unfortunately, we've not been able to do that." He cited the interests of wider public interest and safety.

Reaction and the risk of backlash

Sinn Féin described an "enormous sense of relief", and O'Neill said "there can be no going back" while also saying Bryant should have acted at the start. SDLP leader Claire Hanna said the parade "should never have been given permission in the first place". Taoiseach Micheál Martin called the ban a "significant relief".

On the unionist side the language was sharper, though the reporting so far contains no explicit threats. DUP leader Gavin Robinson said Bryant must "explain what his government intends to do to restore confidence in the rule of law". Deputy First Minister Emma Little-Pengelly accused Sinn Féin of "utter recklessness" and criticised the "jubilance" of some on the night. The Orange Order has not been quoted directly, but the stated option of a court challenge is its only formal response so far.

The argument loyalists are likely to make is straightforward. A statutory body, upheld by the courts, authorised the march. A blockade then prevented it, and a minister removed the permission. The PSNI said on the same day that it would send interview letters to people "reasonably suspected" of offences linked to the unlawful protests, including O'Neill. That fuels a perception of selective enforcement, a point unionists may press. The chief constable's warning of wider disorder is the counter-argument, and it is the one Bryant's decision rests on.

What to watch

Three things matter in the coming days. One is whether the Orange Order goes to court, and whether a judge treats a ministerial override of the Commission as lawful. Another is the Court of Appeal's reserved judgment on the residents' challenge, which may now be overtaken by events. The third is the policing of loyalist protests, which would show whether the Chief Constable's "wider contagion" warning was well founded. The ban settles Drumcree for now. It also shows that, when the Commission's process fails to produce a workable outcome, the Secretary of State will step in.
