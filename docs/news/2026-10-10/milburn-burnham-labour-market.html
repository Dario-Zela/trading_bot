Milburn proposes pre-apprenticeships; Burnham pledges to curb non-competes

In one sentence
A government adviser has urged a pre-apprenticeship scheme for jobless young people, while Andy Burnham has pledged to curb non-competes; neither comes with costings or law.

Two proposals on the British labour market surfaced on the same day, from different corners of the Labour orbit. Neither is law, and neither comes with a price tag. A government adviser, Milburn, has urged a new pre-apprenticeship scheme to narrow the gap in youth unemployment. Separately, Andy Burnham, the Mayor of Greater Manchester, has promised to curb the non-compete clauses that bind many workers after they leave a job.

A new rung on an old ladder

The youth proposal is the more consequential of the two, because it comes from inside the government's circle of advisers. A pre-apprenticeship is generally understood as preparation for those not yet ready to start a full apprenticeship. It usually combines basic skills, work experience and guidance, and it is meant to move a young person from being out of work towards a proper training place.

The idea is not obviously new. Britain already has apprenticeships, traineeships, skills bootcamps and a patchwork of local employment programmes, and successive governments have rebranded and reshuffled them. The test for Milburn's scheme is therefore less whether it is sensible than what it adds. Would it reach young people the existing routes miss? Would it be funded separately, or carved out of current budgets? And would employers offer the places?

Those questions are open. The reporting so far, from the Guardian, describes the proposal in outline. Figures, costings and a timeline have not been confirmed, and this article does not rely on any. An adviser's recommendation also binds ministers to nothing. Governments commission reports and then adopt the parts that suit their spending plans.

The backdrop is a live concern. Youth joblessness has been rising in the UK, and it is politically awkward: it touches education, welfare and business policy at once. A formal, adviser-backed pre-apprenticeship route would show whether ministers are willing to put weight behind that rung of the ladder. For now it signals direction, not commitment.

Burnham and the non-compete

Burnham's pledge belongs to a different register. A non-compete clause bars an employee from joining a rival, or setting up a competing business, for a set period after leaving. Critics say such clauses suppress wages and job mobility. Defenders say they protect investment in staff and trade secrets.

Burnham is a prominent Labour figure, and his promise is a campaign position, not a policy change. The government itself has been weighing limits on non-competes, with options said to include an outright ban or a cap on duration. The current status of that work should be checked against official publications, and it has not been confirmed here. The real question is whether a high-profile voice pushing for curbs makes ministers more likely to act, or simply adds to the pressure on a debate that is already running.

What ties them together

Both proposals sit under one heading: how easily people move into work, and then onward to better work. One tries to get young people through the door. The other would loosen the clauses that can keep them from walking out of it. Each is a statement of intent without a published design behind it.

The next real evidence will be whether the government responds to the Milburn proposal with a costed plan, and whether its work on non-competes produces a formal consultation response. Until either appears, both stay in the realm of argument.
