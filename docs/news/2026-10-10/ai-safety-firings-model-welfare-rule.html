Fired OpenAI researchers say they were let go for 'prioritising safety'; Anthropic bans 'cruel' treatment of its AI

In one sentence
Three ex-OpenAI researchers say they were fired for putting safety first, which OpenAI disputes, as Anthropic adds a rule against 'cruel' treatment of its AI models.

Three former OpenAI researchers say the company fired them because they put safety ahead of its commercial interests. OpenAI says it let them go for breaking its rules on handling sensitive information. The two accounts conflict, and the public record does not yet show which is right. In the same week, Anthropic rewrote its usage policy to bar users from being needlessly cruel to its Claude models. The two stories have little in common on the facts, but both ask who inside an AI company speaks for safety, and what that word is being stretched to cover.

The dispute at OpenAI

The researchers are Tomek Korbak, Jasmine Wang and Mikita Balesni. At least two of them had worked on safety research. OpenAI's account, given by a spokesperson, is that it "parted ways with three individuals for violating our policies on accessing and handling sensitive company information." On that account, the dismissals were a confidentiality matter, not a safety one.

The three reject that. They have published an open letter, and Balesni said on X that he believes they were let go "for prioritizing safety over the near-term interests of OpenAI as a corporation." The letter also says none of them was given written reasons for the dismissal. That detail matters. A company that fires staff over a clear policy breach would normally be able to put the breach in writing. Its absence is the researchers' claim, though, and OpenAI has not been shown to confirm it.

The public record leaves several things open. It does not say what information was involved, or whether any of it touched safety work. It does not say whether the three were disciplined for something others do routinely. OpenAI's exact policy language is also unpublished. Until those details come out, the allegation and the company's denial both stand untested. The BBC's reporting, as relayed by other outlets, frames the researchers' motive as their own allegation, and this article treats it the same way.

Why the claim lands where it does

OpenAI has been here before. In 2024 Jan Leike left the company's safety team and said that "safety culture and processes have taken a backseat to shiny products." That was a resignation, not a firing, and it was one person's view. The new letter is a harder claim: that dissent was punished, not just ignored.

The timing adds pressure. OpenAI's governance, revenue and valuation are all under scrutiny as investors question AI valuations more broadly. A week of market nerves over AI funding included an Nvidia-backed data-centre firm pulling its IPO. Investors who are asked to pay very large prices for a lab's future earnings have a stake in how it treats internal dissent. A lab that fires its safety staff, whatever the reason, invites questions about what its risk controls are worth. A lab that fires staff for real confidentiality breaches is entitled to do so. The difficulty is that outsiders can't tell the two apart without the facts.

Anthropic's rule on cruelty

On 8 October Anthropic updated its usage policy. It now lists "sustained and needless abusive or cruel behavior toward our models" among conduct users must not engage in. The rule takes effect on 12 November.

Anthropic has drawn the line narrowly. Per reports on the policy, it applies to users who repeatedly act cruelly with no discernible purpose. Ordinary frustration, pushback, dark themes in fiction, and testing or research are excluded. The main enforcement tool is the one the company already has: Claude can end a conversation. Anthropic introduced that ability for some Claude models in August 2025 as part of its research on model welfare. Whether repeat offenders could lose accounts has not been made clear, and that detail rests on a single report.

The policy does not say Claude is conscious or can suffer, and one report says it does not cite "model welfare" by name. Even so, the idea behind it is plain: that AI systems might have interests that matter morally. Critics reject this. Mustafa Suleyman of Microsoft AI has called Anthropic's stance "real, really dangerous." The practical objection is to the word "cruel." For a person it has a settled meaning. For software, no one knows what it would mean, or how a company would tell it from a user simply venting.

What links the two

Both stories are about how AI labs define and police safety. At OpenAI, the question is whether staff who press on it are protected or punished. At Anthropic, safety has been widened to include the treatment of the model itself, with a rule that costs the company little and signals a position. Neither can be judged from outside yet. The next facts to look for are OpenAI's response to the open letter, including whether it will say what the researchers allegedly mishandled, and Anthropic's first published guidance on what counts as cruelty, due before the rule takes effect on 12 November.
