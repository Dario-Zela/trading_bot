Fed watch: inflation expectations hit 4.7% as the administration presses on Fed independence

In one sentence
Michigan survey expectations reportedly hit 4.7% as energy costs rise, strengthening the case against Fed easing while the administration again presses on the central bank.

Consumers are bracing for higher prices, and the Federal Reserve is being asked to cut rates anyway. The University of Michigan's consumer survey put households' inflation expectations at 4.7%, according to Benzinga. The outlet called it the second-worst reading on record and tied it to rising energy costs. That is a bad input for a central bank weighing easing. It arrives as the administration is again in a public contest with the Fed over who controls it.

The figures here come from Benzinga's summary of the release. The UMich release and its historical series have not been checked against the original. The "second-worst on record" description in particular is the outlet's characterisation and should be treated as such until it is confirmed. The report also does not say which horizon, one year or five to ten, the 4.7% refers to. That matters for how the Fed reads it, because longer-run expectations are the ones policymakers watch most closely for signs that inflation is becoming entrenched.

Why the number matters for rates

The argument is simple. If households expect prices to keep rising at close to 5%, they tend to build that into wage demands and purchasing decisions, which can make inflation harder to bring down. A central bank that cuts rates into rising expectations risks confirming them. Benzinga says the reading strengthens the case against easing. The report included no Fed officials' comments and no market reaction, so the link to policy is an inference from the survey, not a stated Fed position.

The energy backdrop is concrete. This edition also reports that the Gulf of Mexico has shut in about 72% of its oil output and that the White House is turning to the Defense Production Act. Fuel prices are the item households notice most, and surveys of expectations tend to follow them. Rate-sensitive markets will care less about the 4.7% itself than about whether it stops the Fed from cutting. A slower path of cuts bears on Treasury yields, mortgage rates and equity valuations. Pimco has warned that the 10-year yield could reach 6%.

The pressure on the institution

The political side of the story is less settled. A Reuters report, republished by Benzinga, says President Trump has launched a committee to probe Fed governor Lisa Cook. The announcement's date, the committee's name and its mandate could not be confirmed, and no White House statement was available. Cook, appointed by President Biden in 2022, was the target of an attempt at removal in August 2025 over mortgage-fraud allegations she denied. That dispute was litigated. Whether the new committee is a fresh step or a continuation of the earlier fight needs confirmation.

A second report, from CNBC via Benzinga, says Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent will skip the IMF-World Bank and G20 meetings in Bangkok and send deputies. It also says Bessent has hired Judy Shelton as an adviser. Shelton was Trump's 2019 nominee for the Fed board, and her nomination was withdrawn after Senate opposition. Her title, start date and role have not been verified, nor has the official reason for Bessent's absence.

If all three reports hold, the pattern is consistent. The administration wants lower rates, and it is willing to press on the people who set them. Inflation expectations at 4.7% argue the other way, and the two pressures meet at the Fed's next policy meeting.

What to watch

Three things will settle how much weight these items carry. The first is the UMich release itself, to confirm the horizon and the historical ranking. The second is any Fed official tying the survey to the rate outlook. The third is a primary source, from the White House or the courts, on the Cook committee. Until a Fed speaker links expectations to policy, the case against easing rests on the survey and on Benzinga's reading of it.
