Flydubai attacker's 'drift towards extremism' began in Australia, UAE says

In one sentence
UAE officials say the Flydubai cockpit attacker's radicalisation began in Australia, but the claim is an official attribution from one outlet and other reporting is anonymously sourced.

The United Arab Emirates has begun to put a history to the man accused of attacking the captain of a Flydubai airliner with an axe. According to a BBC World report citing UAE officials, his "drift towards extremism" began in Australia, where he studied a decade ago. The attribution is official and comes from a single outlet. Other reporting on the Australian chapter rests on anonymous sources, and the UAE has not said publicly what evidence it relies on.

The attack

On 30 September, Flydubai flight FZ1073 was flying from Dubai to Tel Aviv with 182 people aboard. Its co-pilot, identified in reports as 29-year-old Omani national Hamam al-Hammami, allegedly attacked the captain, Smit Machchhar, with a crash axe and tried to take control of the aircraft. The plane entered a steep dive. The captain unlocked the cockpit door and passengers and crew restrained the co-pilot. A reserve pilot then flew the aircraft to an emergency landing at Tabuk in Saudi Arabia. The reports reviewed do not mention deaths or injuries.

The UAE attorney general has called the incident a terrorist attack and said investigators are still examining motive, planning and possible links to others. Presidential adviser Anwar Gargash described it as "a new warning" that the region's fight against extremism is not over. Israel's prime minister, Benjamin Netanyahu, said the suspect had undergone "Islamist radicalization" and was investigating whether he had been sent by anyone. ABC News, citing officials, reported that he indicated he meant to crash the aircraft in Israel.

The Australian thread

Australian police told the BBC they are conducting an in-depth investigation into his background, and a Victorian joint counter-terrorism team is looking at his links to the country. Reuters, citing sources briefed on the inquiry, reported that he studied at a Melbourne university; a further source named RMIT, about a decade ago. Reports say he held a student visa from 2014 to 2017, cancelled in 2019. The Financial Times, citing two sources, said he allegedly visited "radical mosques and extremist websites" while there. Reuters said it could not immediately verify that. New Zealand police have said he also spent time in their country and that authorities will look into it.

The timeline matters. The best-documented warning sign is not Australian. ABC News reported that in 2024 he was found with extremist materials while a co-pilot trainee at Oman Air, and was moved to an administrative role and barred from flying. He was later hired by Flydubai. None of the sources found here dates the onset of his radicalisation, and the Associated Press said investigators had found no link to a militant group so far, which points toward a lone actor. "Began in Australia" is therefore a UAE assessment, not an established finding.

The screening question

The larger question is how someone flagged in one Gulf state ended up in the cockpit of a carrier in another, on a route to Israel. A Gulf diplomat told the AP that standard checks focus on criminal convictions and may not capture signs of radicalisation. Mr Netanyahu said a "loophole" appeared to have let the pilot join the flight and that Israel would strengthen its checks. Oman Air, Omani officials and Flydubai have not commented beyond earlier statements.

If the Australian origin holds up, it would show a pathway running from a Western university town to a Gulf cockpit, across at least four jurisdictions. That would strengthen the case for airlines and regulators to share intelligence on pilots across borders, and to screen for ideology and not just criminal records. If the Oman episode proves the more significant, the failure looks more like a gap between employers in neighbouring states.

What to watch

The first test is whether Australian investigators publicly corroborate the UAE account, or say it overstates what they have found. The second is whether the UAE attorney general's inquiry establishes motive and any accomplices. Neither Flydubai nor Oman Air has said what was shared when he changed employers, and that answer will shape any change to hiring rules.
