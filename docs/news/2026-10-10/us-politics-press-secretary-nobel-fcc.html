Trump names Katie Zacharia press secretary and attacks Norway over Nobel as FCC chair defers to Pentagon

In one sentence
Trump reportedly named ex-DHS spokesperson Katie Zacharia press secretary via Truth Social, called Norway's Nobel a 'stain', and the FCC chair said he'd defer to the Pentagon; all unverified.

Donald Trump has named Katie Zacharia as the next White House press secretary, announcing the appointment on Truth Social rather than from the briefing room. The choice of platform says as much as the choice of person. Zacharia, described in the reporting as a Truth Social communications adviser and a former Department of Homeland Security spokesperson, takes the job as part of what is reported to be a shake-up of the communications team.

The account rests on a single post as relayed by the financial news site Benzinga. This article could not confirm the appointment independently, Zacharia's earlier roles, or the scope of the staff changes. Neither a White House statement nor a major wire report was available to check against.

A message run from the platform

Presidents have long announced personnel through the press office or a formal statement. Trump uses Truth Social, a platform he owns, for announcements of this kind. Naming a press secretary there means the person who will brief the press first appears in a channel the president controls.

The reported résumé points the same way. A Truth Social adviser moving into the briefing-room job suggests the two operations are becoming one. Whether that changes how often the briefing room is used, or what is said in it, will only show once Zacharia is in post.

Norway and the Nobel

On the foreign-policy side, BBC World reports that Trump called Norway's Nobel Peace Prize a "stain on the country". Only the headline and a short summary were available, so the date, the full remarks and any response from Oslo are unconfirmed.

Two points of context apply. Norway is a NATO ally. The Peace Prize is awarded by a committee appointed by the Norwegian parliament, but it is formally independent of the government. A president criticising it is a diplomatic irritant, not a change of policy. It would become more than that only if rhetoric turned into official action toward Norway, and nothing in the reporting suggests it has.

The FCC and the Pentagon

The third thread is a regulatory one. Brendan Carr, the FCC chair, said on CNBC that the agency would defer to the Pentagon on broadcasting military executions, according to Benzinga's account of the interview. The full wording and context were not available.

The FCC's authority comes from the Communications Act of 1934 and covers the broadcasters it licenses. The Pentagon has no role in licensing. If Carr meant that the military would decide what licensees may air, a regulator would be handing a content judgment to a department with an obvious interest in the answer. That would raise First Amendment questions and would invite scrutiny from broadcasters. A narrower reading is also possible, such as deference on operational security or on what footage exists. Only the full remarks would settle which he meant.

What ties it together

The three items are separate, but they share a theme: public communication handled informally, through a social-media post, a presidential aside or a television interview, with the formal channels coming afterwards. That is a pattern, not a conclusion, and each item needs its own confirmation.

The next checks are practical. A White House statement or wire report would confirm Zacharia's appointment and show how soon she holds a briefing. Any reaction from the Norwegian government or the Nobel committee would show whether the remarks are being treated as noise. A clarification from the FCC would show what Carr's deference actually covers.
