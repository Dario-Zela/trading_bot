Germany to release up to 15m barrels as tanker rates hit $1.3m a day

In one sentence
Germany will release up to 15m barrels from emergency stocks, diesel and heating oil first, as part of a G7 draw that treats refined fuels as the tightest part of the market.

Germany plans to release up to 15 million barrels of oil and oil products from its emergency stocks. The release is Berlin's contribution to a coordinated G7 drawdown, and diesel and heating oil are first in the sequence. Economy Minister Katherina Reiche said Berlin will fully implement the G7 agreement. The ordering matters as much as the volume. It shows which fuels policymakers think are scarcest.

The 15m-barrel figure and the diesel-and-heating-oil sequencing come from the triage reporting. The official German and G7 accounts reviewed for this piece do not state Germany's share. The 'up to' wording leaves room for a smaller draw.

What the G7 agreed

The G7 (Germany, France, Italy, Britain, the United States, Canada and Japan) agreed on 2–3 October to release up to 100m barrels of crude and diesel. The IEA is coordinating the release, which runs over four months from the decision. Further volumes were not ruled out. At 159 litres a barrel, 100m barrels is about 15.9bn litres. Germany's reported 15m barrels would be roughly 15% of that, though the G7 statement does not set national quotas.

The backdrop is a market under strain. Brent has risen more than 60% since the start of the year and has traded above $100 a barrel, driven by the Iran conflict and attacks on tankers near the Strait of Hormuz. Euro-area inflation reached 3.8% in September, the highest in three years, with energy a main cause. Crude exports from the Middle East are nearing pre-war levels. Damaged refineries still limit how much of that crude becomes fuel, and Russia has its own diesel export ban.

That is why the reported order of release makes sense. The binding constraint is refined product, not crude. Reiche framed the aim as turning "a tense situation into a reliable joint approach". She added that the G7 would keep energy flowing among members and coordinate refinery capacity. Trump dropped a threatened US diesel export ban, and members agreed not to impose export restrictions on one another.

Signal first, price second

Berlin presents the release as reassurance rather than price management. Germany's economics ministry says its reserves could cover a complete import outage for three months and that the draw poses no supply risk. A release requires a statutory order from the ministry, and the Petroleum Stockholding Association (EBV) manages the stocks. The reports reviewed say German volumes are sold at market prices.

Pump prices moved in the right direction after the announcement. Diesel fell about six cents a litre nationwide by Sunday, and Brent briefly slipped below $100. Economist Samina Sultan of the IW says such releases give motorists only short-term relief, if any. Earlier this year Berlin resisted US pressure to draw down diesel stocks, arguing that reserves are for physical shortages. This release reverses that position.

Two things remain open. The 'up to' wording sets a ceiling and not a commitment. The release also has no stated trigger for how much Germany actually draws or when. The volume will probably track how tight diesel and heating oil are as winter heating demand arrives.

Freight as a second gauge

Freight costs show the same stress. Benzinga reports tanker rates of $1.3m a day and says nine shipping stocks have doubled this year. The vessel class and route behind that figure are not specified. Other outlets put the record for very large crude carriers on Gulf-to-Asia routes at about $1.0–1.1m a day, citing Baltic Exchange data, so the $1.3m figure could not be independently confirmed. Trafigura has launched Volare Shipping to operate VLCCs, which shows how attractive the rates are to owners.

If freight is this expensive, moving barrels costs more even where barrels exist. That suggests stock releases address only part of the problem. A drawdown adds supply at the consuming end. It does nothing for the cost of shipping or the shortage of refining capacity. Macron said the G7 would also work on lower refinery margins and transport costs.

What to watch

The first indicator is whether the ministry issues the release order and puts a volume on the 'up to' figure. The second is the monthly pace of the IEA-coordinated draw over its four months. The third is whether diesel cracks and tanker rates ease, or whether the six-cent fall in diesel prices proves a one-off.
