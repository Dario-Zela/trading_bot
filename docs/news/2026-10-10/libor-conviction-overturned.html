Ex-Deutsche Bank trader's rate-rigging conviction overturned

In one sentence
London's Court of Appeal has quashed ex-Deutsche Bank trader Christian Bittar's Euribor rate-rigging conviction, the eighth such reversal after the Hayes and Palombo ruling.

A London court has quashed the conviction of Christian Bittar, the former Deutsche Bank trader jailed for rigging a benchmark interest rate. The Court of Appeal ruled on Friday 9 October. It is the first time a conviction resting on a guilty plea has been overturned in the benchmark-rate cases.

The case concerned Euribor, the euro-denominated benchmark, rather than Libor. The two scandals are usually treated as one, and the legal reasoning that has unpicked the Libor convictions now reaches the Euribor ones.

Lord Justice Edis gave the ruling, with the reasons held back: "For reasons we will set out at a later date, this conviction has been quashed." Until the written judgment appears, the precise grounds are known only through the arguments Bittar's side put forward.

A guilty plea, undone

Bittar pleaded guilty in 2018 to conspiring to manipulate Euribor and was sentenced to just over five years in prison. Reports differ on the exact term: five years and four months in some accounts, five years in others.

His lawyer, Adrian Darbishire, argued that the plea rested on fundamental legal errors. Prosecutors, he said, had not been required to prove that Bittar agreed to submit "false or misleading" Euribor rates. The Serious Fraud Office opposed the appeal.

That argument comes from the Supreme Court's ruling last year in the cases of Tom Hayes, formerly of UBS, and Carlo Palombo, formerly of Barclays. The court found that the directions given to the juries were flawed. The Court of Appeal has since said the same errors affected later trials. Bittar's appeal applied that reasoning to a case that never reached a jury.

Eight and counting

The ruling follows a run of reversals. On Wednesday the Court of Appeal quashed the convictions of five former Barclays traders: Jay Merchant, Jonathan Mathew, Philippe Moryoussef, Alex Pabon and Colin Bermingham. They had served time for conspiring to rig Libor and had pleaded not guilty. Counting Hayes, Palombo and Bittar, eight rate-rigging convictions have now been set aside.

Bittar said in a statement that he had "waited a very, very long time for this day."

Jason Williams, head of division at the SFO, said: "We argued for a different outcome but respect the court's decision in relation to Christian Bittar." He added that the agency "remains committed to tackling the most complex fraud, bribery and corruption."

What remains

The prosecutions that followed the rate-rigging scandal are now a diminished set. Peter Johnson, formerly of Barclays, pleaded guilty and was sentenced to four years. He is in the early stages of his own appeal. His case will test whether the Bittar reasoning extends to every guilty plea entered under the old legal framing.

The written judgment is the next marker. It will show how far the court rested on the Supreme Court's reading of the offence, and how much room it leaves for the remaining convictions to stand.
