LVMH returns to growth as Dior revival lifts fashion and leather goods

The Christian Dior flagship boutique at 30 Avenue Montaigne, Paris, the house's historic home since 1946. — Frédéric BISSON / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
LVMH's fashion arm snapped a two-year sales slide in Q2, but the entire rebound was carried by US shoppers while Europe, China and the Gulf stayed flat.

The number that mattered most in LVMH's second-quarter results was not a euro figure at all. It was two: as in two years, the length of the losing streak that the group's fashion and leather goods division — Louis Vuitton, Dior, Loro Piana, Fendi — finally broke in the three months to June.

Organic sales in the division rose 1 percent to €9.01 billion, a modest number by the standards of luxury's boom years but a meaningful one after seven straight quarters of decline. The relief was visible in the share price only faintly — LVMH stock rose 1.1 percent on the print, still more than a quarter below its 52-week high — a reminder that investors have been burned enough by false dawns in this sector to withhold real enthusiasm.

What gives the number its edge is where it came from. Almost all of the acceleration traced back to one market: the United States.

An American rebound, everywhere else flat

Group-wide, LVMH's revenue rose 3 percent organically in the quarter to €19.5 billion, lifting first-half sales to €38.6 billion, up 2 percent. Chief financial officer Jean-Jacques Guiony told analysts on the earnings call that "the sharp acceleration in U.S. offshore demand in Q2 came on top of a tangible improvement in local demand" — company language for American shoppers buying more, both online and in stores. U.S. sales growth doubled, roughly, from 3 percent in the first quarter to 6 percent in the second, which LVMH attributed to demand "fuelled by newly created wealth," a reference to gains concentrated in AI and technology stocks rather than broad-based income growth.

Everywhere else, the picture was flatter. Europe stabilized after a first-quarter decline but did not grow, with Guiony noting tourism "improved... driven by Asian clienteles" without translating into a real acceleration in local spending. China was, in his description, "flattish," with local and touristic demand alike stuck, and Chinese purchases "increasingly clustered around shopping events" rather than steady year-round buying — a sign that even the world's second-largest luxury market is spending in bursts, not building momentum. The Gulf, meanwhile, was still working through the effects of the Iran conflict earlier in the year; Guiony described "regular and gradual improvement" but called the region's trajectory "still very much unknown." LVMH estimated the Middle East conflict alone shaved roughly a percentage point off the fashion and leather goods division's growth.

Dior leads, but the base is narrow

Within the division, Dior did the heaviest lifting. The house posted double-digit sales growth among American and Japanese clients, and management said Dior ran slightly ahead of the group average, with Louis Vuitton also back in positive territory. Guiony credited the improvement to "continued excitement around the unveiling of Jonathan Anderson's new creative vision, with good response to bags and to ready-to-wear." Anderson, formerly of Loewe, took over as Dior's sole creative director this year and has drawn attention well beyond the fashion press — his design for Taylor Swift's wedding dress became one of the most-discussed luxury moments of the year, the kind of cultural spillover that houses like Dior depend on to justify runway spending.

The rest of the group told a similar story about who is actually still spending. Watches and jewellery, not fashion, was LVMH's fastest-growing division, up 11 percent organically as Tiffany and Bulgari posted mid-teens growth — evidence, as one line in the results put it, that "luxury brands are increasingly dependent on a small cohort of ultra-wealthy clients" while aspirational, credit-sensitive shoppers stay on the sidelines. Sephora added a steadier 5 percent. Group net profit held flat at €5.70 billion for the half, ahead of the roughly €5.22 billion consensus, on an operating margin of 22.5 percent — margin discipline doing the work that top-line growth, in a normal cycle, would otherwise do.

A recovery with a narrow base

The pattern is not subtle. LVMH's traditional demand engines — European tourism, Gulf wealth, mainland Chinese consumption — are flat or convalescing, while a US market flush with tech and AI-driven paper wealth is carrying the fashion division back into growth almost on its own. That is a different recovery from the pre-pandemic one, when Chinese demand alone could move LVMH's headline numbers by several points a quarter. It also raises the obvious question of durability: American luxury spending tied to equity and AI-adjacent wealth gains is, by nature, more volatile than the tourist flows and old-money spending that used to anchor the sector.

The next test comes with LVMH's third-quarter sales update in October, which will show whether the US acceleration held through the northern summer or whether it was a one-quarter phenomenon tied to Dior's Anderson-driven publicity cycle. Investors will also be watching whether China's shopping-event pattern — spikes around specific dates rather than sustained spending — turns into something steadier, and whether the Gulf's "gradual improvement" survives if the ceasefire covered elsewhere in this edition proves fragile. Until then, LVMH's growth streak is real, but it is standing on one leg.
