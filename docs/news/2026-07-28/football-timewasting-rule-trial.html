English football trials rule to end goalkeeper time-wasting tactical timeouts

Gianluigi Donnarumma, whose goalkeeper-injury stoppage for Manchester City drew accusations of gamesmanship from Leeds boss Daniel Farke. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
English football will dock teams a player for a minute whenever a goalkeeper needs medical treatment, in a bid to stop stoppages being used as free tactical timeouts.

The Football Association has agreed, with the game's law-making body IFAB, to trial a new rule from the start of the 2026-27 season aimed at ending one of football's most reliably irritating rituals: the goalkeeper who goes to ground clutching a limb, receives two minutes of soothing treatment from the physio, and returns to the game fully recovered — and with his manager having used the stoppage to reorganise the entire team.

Under the trial, any stoppage for a goalkeeper injury now carries a cost. The head coach has ten seconds to nominate an outfield player who must then leave the pitch and stay off for at least a minute after play restarts — a straight one-man penalty designed to make the tactical value of an injury break disappear. It will run across the Premier League, the EFL, the Women's Super League, the National League and FA Cup competitions, covering the men's and women's professional game in England down to National League Step 2.

There are carve-outs, inevitably. A goalkeeper fouled and needing immediate treatment is exempt. So is one who collides with an outfield player who also needs attention, and so is any goalkeeper who is bleeding. The FA says the aim is to "preserve genuine medical attention for injuries while eliminating potential competitive advantages from stoppages" — a sentence that concedes, in its own way, how hard it is to tell the two apart from the touchline.

A grievance with a long charge sheet

The tactic long predates this trial, but last season supplied a tidy rota of examples for anyone building the case. In November, Leeds manager Daniel Farke accused Manchester City's Gianluigi Donnarumma of feigning injury to "bend the rules" after the goalkeeper went down late in a 3-2 win, allowing Pep Guardiola nearly two minutes to reorganise his side from the touchline. Farke's complaint was pointed precisely because he also admitted it was legal: "it is within the rules," he said, "and it is smart." That is the trial's whole premise in a sentence — a tactic nobody disputes is against the letter of the game, deployed by players and coaches who know exactly what they're doing with it.

Donnarumma was not alone in the accusations column. Gary Neville suggested on air that Newcastle's Aaron Ramsdale went down on a passed-on instruction from Eddie Howe's bench, and Brighton's Fabian Hurzeler complained in the spring that Arsenal's David Raya had taken what he called a tactical timeout of his own. None of these produced a sanction, because none of them broke a rule that, until now, didn't exist.

Part of a pattern, not a standalone fix

The injury trial is the third piece of stopwatch legislation English football has introduced inside about a year. The eight-second limit on goalkeepers holding the ball — replacing an old six-second rule that referees rarely enforced — arrived for 2025-26 and was tested first, its backers point out, before being written into law. Martin Dubravka became its first Premier League casualty, penalised in Burnley's opening game of that season. A five-second countdown for delayed throw-ins and goal kicks, and a ten-second limit on substituted players leaving the pitch, both start alongside the new injury rule this season, beginning with the Carabao Cup's opening round on 1 August.

What's missing, so far, is any published evidence that the eight-second rule actually changed goalkeeper behaviour across a full season, rather than producing one high-profile early booking and then fading into the same patchy enforcement as its predecessor. The FA's own language for the injury trial commits only to monitoring "throughout the season in collaboration with IFAB and stakeholders" — a promise to watch, not a target to hit or a date by which the trial will be judged to have worked or failed.

What to watch

The test comes fast: the first live use of the rule under FA jurisdiction lands with the Carabao Cup's opening fixtures on 1 August, with the Premier League and WSL seasons following in mid-August. Whether coaches simply adapt — instructing a already-tiring or tactically expendable player to be the one who walks off — will be the first tell of whether this closes the loophole or just relocates it.
