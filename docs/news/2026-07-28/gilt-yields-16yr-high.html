UK gilt yields hold above 5% for first time since 2008 as Burnham unsettles markets

Andy Burnham became UK prime minister on 20 July 2026; his comments on fiscal rule "flexibility" preceded the gilt sell-off. — House of Commons / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
UK 10-year gilt yields held above 5% for a week — the longest since 2008 — after PM Burnham's fiscal comments, with markets now pricing a 70% chance of a September BoE hike.

UK government borrowing costs have settled into territory nobody under the age of about 35 has traded through. The 10-year gilt yield closed last week at 5.04%, having spent five straight sessions above the 5% mark — the longest such stretch since July 2008, when Northern Rock was a fading memory and Lehman Brothers still had two months to live. The trigger was not a banking collapse but a single phrase from a new prime minister: Andy Burnham, days into the job, told reporters his government would "stick to the fiscal rules... and use obviously any flexibility within them."

Bond markets do not like the word flexibility applied to borrowing limits. Within hours of Burnham's remarks on 20 July, the 10-year yield jumped eight basis points to 5.049% and 30-year borrowing costs rose to their highest since late May, near 5.75%. The UK now carries the highest sovereign borrowing costs in the G10 — a distinction that costs real money. Debt servicing ran to £11.8 billion in June alone.

A chancellor, appointed to calm a market

The scale of the reaction forced Burnham's hand quickly. He named John Healey, previously defence secretary, as chancellor in place of Rachel Reeves, and yields eased slightly across the curve the following morning as investors read the appointment as a signal of continuity rather than a lurch toward looser spending. It was a partial repair job, not a fix. Healey has said little publicly beyond committing to the existing fiscal framework, and markets now understand that the substance of "flexibility" — what it actually means for the size of the deficit — will not be spelled out until the Autumn Budget, expected in October. Until then, every Burnham speech is a potential re-pricing event.

The political framing has not helped. Keir Starmer, whom Burnham replaced, has compared his rival's instincts to Liz Truss's — a comparison that lands hard in a market still scarred by the September 2022 mini-budget, when 30-year gilt yields spiked by more than a percentage point in days and forced the Bank of England into emergency bond purchases. Nobody seriously expects a repeat of that scale of dysfunction. But the comparison itself illustrates how thin the market's patience has worn for anything read as fiscal experimentation, four years on.

Oil is doing at least half the work

Burnham's comments were not the whole story, and fund managers are careful to say so. Brent crude has risen by roughly a third since the start of July, pushing toward $100 a barrel as the conflict between the US and Iran flares back up — a separate but simultaneous shock that is doing real damage to the UK's inflation outlook. "It's very difficult to separate what the gilt market is thinking about [Burnham] and what's happening with the oil price," said Colin Finlayson of Aegon Asset Management, a fair summary of the disentangling problem facing anyone trying to price UK duration right now.

The two shocks compound rather than offset. Energy-driven inflation was already pushing the Bank of England toward a more hawkish stance before Burnham took office; Bank Rate has held at 3.75% since the spring, well above the eurozone's 2.25%, precisely because UK inflation has been slower to come down. Governor Andrew Bailey has called the current level "a reasonable place given... the unpredictability of events in the Middle East," but the framing has shifted from an active cutting cycle to what Goldman Sachs analysts describe as a swing from roughly 50 basis points of expected cuts to 60 basis points of expected hikes within a matter of weeks. Money markets are now pricing a 70% probability that the Monetary Policy Committee raises rates at its 17 September meeting — a reversal that would have seemed implausible as recently as May, when gilt yields were easing and rate-cut bets were still in play.

The stagflation trade-off

The uncomfortable arithmetic is that a September hike, if it materialises, would be tightening policy into an economy that is not obviously overheating. Growth has been sluggish, and a rate rise driven by energy-cost pass-through and fiscal jitters rather than domestic demand is the textbook precursor to stagflation — rising prices alongside weak output. Some analysts, including Goldman's own, argue the gilt sell-off has already overshot what fundamentals justify, and that hiking without any offsetting fiscal restraint would be actively harmful to growth. That view depends heavily on oil prices cooling and on Healey producing a budget that reassures rather than unsettles.

The next hard catalysts are dated. The Bank of England's MPC meets on 17 September, the point at which the 70% hike probability either gets vindicated or unwound. Before that, expect Burnham and Healey to face pressure to say more about what "flexibility" actually costs, ahead of an October Budget that markets are already treating as the real test of whether this government can borrow more without paying a permanent premium for it.
