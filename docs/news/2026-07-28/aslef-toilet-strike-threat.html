Train drivers' union Aslef weighs strike action over lack of on-board toilets

An LNER Class 800 train at Arbroath — one of the fleets whose drivers are covered by the industry's stalled welfare charter. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Aslef boycotted the latest talks on driver toilet access, saying operators have ignored a regulator-backed welfare charter for 18 months.

Aslef, the train drivers' union, has walked away from talks with rail operators over one of the least glamorous items on any industrial-relations agenda: toilets. On June 24 the union boycotted an industry meeting on driver welfare, arguing that eighteen months of promises to fix on-board facilities and driving-time limits have produced almost nothing.

The complaint traces back to November 2024, when Aslef published Dignity for Drivers, a report built on a survey of more than 3,000 drivers by Unity Consulting. It made for uncomfortable reading: drivers described urinating in bottles, defecating in cabs, changing sanitary products at the controls, or leaving stationary trains to relieve themselves beside the track, all for want of a working toilet or the time to reach one.

Regulators took note. In July 2025, Richard Hines, the Office of Rail and Road's chief inspector of railways, wrote to operators calling reliable welfare facilities "a basic requirement in any civilised society" and the status quo "unacceptable." The Rail Safety and Standards Board drew up a Welfare Facilities Charter — safe, clean and dignified toilets network-wide, a four-hour cap on time in the driving seat without a break, no disciplinary action for using the toilet, free sanitary products — and operators were urged to sign by World Toilet Day, November 19, 2025.

Eighteen months, no fix

That deadline came and went. A first roundtable in June 2025 brought operators, the Department for Transport, Network Rail and unions together to work through the charter; a follow-up meeting a year later, on June 24, was meant to check progress. Aslef decided there was no point attending.

"Eighteen months after we exposed this scandal, drivers are still paying for industry inaction with their health," said Dave Calfe, the union's general secretary since November 2025. "Enough excuses, rail bosses cannot continue failing the workforce on the most basic level. We need action, not another talking shop, and no more broken promises."

No formal ballot on welfare facilities has been called, and Aslef has stopped short of naming a strike threat — the boycott is a protest, not yet an escalation. But the union is not short of leverage elsewhere: its drivers at Avanti West Coast are separately being balloted this summer over a stalled 2026 pay offer, a reminder that relations between Aslef and operators are already running hot on more than one front.

What makes the toilets dispute worth a line in a markets diary is less the plumbing than the pattern: operators missed a regulator-endorsed deadline by eight months with no visible consequence, and the union's response was to stop showing up rather than escalate through the usual channels. That is a governance signal as much as a workplace one — a system where non-compliance carries no cost tends to keep not complying, until something forces the issue. The next marker is whether Aslef moves from boycott to ballot; until then, this stays a story about industry credibility rather than train timetables.
