New EU electricity and gas market rules take effect across the bloc

A high-voltage transmission pylon carries power across the grid that EU regulators are now requiring operators to run under tighter, faster-switching rules. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Revised EU energy rules on faster supplier-switching and grid security take effect for electricity from 17 July, with gas rules following 5 August.

Europe's power and gas markets entered a new regulatory phase this month, as the European Commission's revised electricity market rules became applicable across all 27 member states on 17 July, with a parallel overhaul of gas-market law following on 5 August. Together they amount to the bloc's most significant rewrite of energy-market plumbing since the price shocks of 2022, and they land squarely on the desks of utilities from EDF to E.ON to Iberdrola, which now have a hard deadline to rebuild switching infrastructure and tighten grid operations mid-cycle.

In one sentence: Revised EU energy rules aimed at cleaner, more secure supply and faster supplier-switching take effect from 17 July for electricity, with gas rules following on 5 August.

What changes, and when

The electricity package, built on the Electricity Directive, sets a hard target: by the end of 2026, the back-office process of switching supplier — verifying the customer, deregistering the old contract, registering the new one — must be completed within 24 hours across the bloc. The Commission has paired that with a common EU "reference model" for exchanging switching data between suppliers and distribution operators, developed with input from ENTSO-E and the EU DSO Entity, the bodies representing transmission and distribution grid operators respectively. Consumers also gain a guaranteed right to a fixed-price, fixed-term contract of at least a year, alongside dynamic-pricing options, plus stronger disconnection protections for vulnerable households and supplier-of-last-resort backstops if a provider collapses.

Gas follows a separate but related track. Directive (EU) 2024/1788 and its companion regulation, adopted in 2024 as part of the bloc's Hydrogen and Decarbonised Gas Market Package, must be transposed into national law by 5 August 2026 — and the rules apply from that date. They open gas networks to renewable and low-carbon fuels, cap new long-term unabated fossil-gas supply contracts at 2049, and extend free-supplier-choice and dispute-resolution protections that already exist in electricity to gas customers.

The compliance lift

The 24-hour switching mandate is the more operationally demanding piece for suppliers in the near term. It requires new data-interoperability standards between metering operators, distribution system operators and retail suppliers — infrastructure many national markets do not yet have in a standardised form, which is why the Commission is treating full compliance as a 2026-into-2027 build rather than a single cutover date. For large integrated utilities such as EDF, E.ON and Iberdrola, that means simultaneous investment in switching systems, grid-security protocols for renewable integration, and — in some member states — separate national compliance costs layered on top. France, for instance, is introducing a Universal Nuclear Payment, a levy on EDF's power revenues redistributed to consumers, running alongside the EU-level reforms rather than as part of them.

The asymmetry between the two implementation dates is the detail worth watching commercially. Electricity suppliers face switching and grid obligations now, while gas suppliers get another month and a technically distinct set of requirements centred on market access for hydrogen and low-carbon gases rather than switching speed. That staggering means power retailers absorb the first wave of compliance spending in isolation, at a moment when faster switching is designed explicitly to intensify price competition between them — a combination likely to compress margins for incumbents before it does anything for consumer bills.

What's next

The practical test comes in the second half of 2026, as national regulators certify that distribution operators and suppliers actually meet the 24-hour switching standard rather than merely publish a plan to. The Commission has committed to ongoing guidance and stakeholder engagement through the transition; the next formal checkpoint will be member states' transposition reports for the gas directive due around the 5 August deadline, followed by the Commission's own review of electricity-switching compliance as the 2026 year-end target approaches.
