ECB holds rates at 2.25% as war-driven inflation clouds outlook

The European Central Bank's headquarters in Frankfurt, where the Governing Council voted unanimously to hold rates on Thursday. — Daniel Vorndran (DXR), Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0
In one sentence
The ECB held its deposit rate at 2.25%, pausing weeks after its first hike since 2023, as war-driven energy costs keep inflation at 2.8% and growth forecasts fall to 0.8%.

The European Central Bank left its main deposit rate unchanged at 2.25% on Thursday, pausing barely six weeks after raising it for the first time since 2023 — a hold that tells its own story about how nervous the Governing Council has become about the eurozone economy.

The decision was unanimous, Christine Lagarde told reporters in Frankfurt, though she added a line that will get more attention than the vote itself: "There were some governors who asked themselves whether we should not consider a hike." That a hold needed defending at all says something about where the debate inside the ECB has moved. Inflation is still running at 2.8%, well above the bank's 2% target, and the reason has nothing to do with overheating demand — it is oil, and it is the war.

A pause, not a pivot

June's move — a 25 basis-point rise to 2.25%, the ECB's first hike in nearly three years — was itself a response to an energy shock, not a vote of confidence in the economy. Lagarde was careful on Thursday to frame it that way again, describing the increase as a reaction to "genuine inflation concerns rather than serving as a precautionary measure." The main refinancing rate stays at 2.4%, the marginal lending facility at 2.65%.

The headline inflation number is actually improving: 2.8% in June, down from 3.2% in May, the first deceleration this year. Core inflation, which strips out food and energy, eased to 2.4% from 2.6%. But energy inflation alone still sits at 8.5%, down only modestly from 10.8%, and staff projections now show the return to the 2% target slipping to late 2027 — a year later than the bank was pencilling in before the Middle East conflict escalated.

The war behind the numbers

The proximate cause is not subtle. Oil has traded above $90 a barrel since renewed US-Iran exchanges and fresh sanctions rattled markets, with the closure of shipping lanes through the Strait of Hormuz and strikes on Middle Eastern energy infrastructure squeezing supply. Houthi attacks on shipping routes have added a second layer of disruption on top of that. The ECB's own statement put it in characteristically dry central-bank language: energy prices remain "highly volatile" and "well above the levels recorded prior to the conflict," and the Governing Council is "closely monitoring the intensity and duration of the shock, as well as its indirect and second-round effects."

That phrase — second-round effects — is the crux of the policy dilemma. An energy shock that pushes up prices once is a supply problem; an energy shock that gets baked into wage demands and becomes self-perpetuating is a monetary problem, and monetary problems are what interest rates are for. So far, Lagarde said, the evidence points the right way: negotiated wage growth is running near 3.5% and moderating, and on the wage-price spiral question, her assessment was blunt: "We are not seeing it."

Growth takes the hit

What the ECB is seeing is weaker growth. The eurozone economy is now projected to expand just 0.8% in 2026, down from 0.9% three months earlier, with 2027 trimmed to 1.2% from 1.3%. Lagarde described activity as likely to "remain modest in the near term,” with some resilience in digital services — partly AI-driven — offsetting weakness elsewhere, while households and firms brace for a labour market that stays softer than before the conflict began.

That combination — inflation still above target, growth downgraded, and the cause sitting outside the bank's control — is the textbook definition of a central bank caught between two mandates it cannot satisfy with the same tool. Hike further, and a fragile eurozone economy tips closer to recession. Hold or cut, and an energy shock that hasn't fully worked through the pipeline gets more room to entrench itself in prices. Thursday's decision to sit still is, in that sense, less a resolution than a postponement.

What happens in September

Lagarde declined to offer forward guidance, insisting the Governing Council has not committed to a path either way. The next meeting will come with fresh staff projections and two additional inflation prints, giving policymakers a clearer read on whether the energy shock is fading or spreading. The ECB is, for now, the only major Western central bank to have actually raised rates this cycle — the Federal Reserve meets on 31 July and the Bank of England on 30 July, both expected to hold at 3.50–3.75% and 3.75% respectively, watching the same oil price with rather less urgency than Frankfurt is showing. Whether that gap widens or closes will depend less on eurozone data than on events several thousand miles away, in the Strait of Hormuz and the skies over Iran.
