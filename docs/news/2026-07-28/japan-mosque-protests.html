Japanese town wrestles with identity after protests over its first mosque

In one sentence
Thousands protested Fujisawa's first mosque, exposing how cultural resistance is colliding with Japan's need for foreign labour.

In Fujisawa, a seaside city about an hour south of Tokyo, a patch of grass on the site of a former factory has become the unlikely fault line in Japan's argument with itself over immigration. The 980-square-metre plot in the city's Miyahara district is earmarked for the area's first purpose-built mosque — and since April, it has drawn thousands of protesters, a 32,000-signature petition, and a national debate about what a more diverse Japan is supposed to look like.

The project began quietly in 2021, when Mohamed Khaleel, a 54-year-old Sri Lankan businessman who has lived in Fujisawa for years, and other local Muslims formed the Fujisawa Masjid NPO. They raised funds, bought the land and started the long process of securing planning permission for a mosque to serve a community that had been driving 20 kilometres to Ebina for Friday prayers. Completion is targeted for 2027. City officials say the plan meets all zoning and development rules and will proceed under standard planning law.

That has not settled the matter locally. On April 12, thousands of demonstrators gathered near Fujisawa Station carrying Japanese flags and placards, with riot police deployed to keep order. Objections ranged from the practical — traffic during prayer times, unfamiliarity with Islamic burial customs, a linked cemetery proposal — to the symbolic: critics noted the mosque's planned footprint would dwarf a nearby Shinto shrine, framing the project as a slight to local heritage rather than a place of worship.

"It makes me worry about how our community might change," said Noriko Izumizawa, a Fujisawa resident.

Not everyone in town agrees. "I think the first step is to learn about them," said Hiroki Suzuka, who manages a coffee shop nearby. "It begins with having an open mind."

An economic problem wearing a cultural face

Fujisawa's dispute is a local flashpoint, but it sits on top of a national contradiction. Japan's population is ageing and shrinking, and the government has leaned harder on foreign labour to fill the gap: more than four million foreign residents now live in the country, 2.57 million of them working. Japan's Muslim population alone has nearly doubled since 2019, from roughly 230,000 to about 420,000, growth driven largely by workers from South and Southeast Asia rather than conversion.

Politics has caught up with the demographics. Sanseito, the right-wing party that surged in the 2025 upper house election on a "Japanese First" platform warning of a "silent invasion," has raised objections to mosque and cemetery projects in parliament. In June, the government raised visa fees fivefold — the first such increase in roughly fifty years. Muslim community leaders argue the backlash is less organic than orchestrated, fed by social media misinformation and overseas coverage of Islam.

"Now, I feel that people are afraid of us," said Mohamed Mohideen, a Muslim businessman in Osaka.

The contradiction is stark: Japan needs the workers a mosque like Fujisawa's would serve, even as local politics makes each new one harder to build. Construction is due to begin once permits clear; how smoothly that goes — and whether other municipalities face similar resistance — will be an early signal of whether Japan's labour strategy and its cultural politics can be reconciled, or whether they are set on a collision course.
