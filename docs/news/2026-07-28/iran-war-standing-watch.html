Iran war watch: ceasefire talk cools oil, but airlines still feel the squeeze

Donald Trump and Benjamin Netanyahu at the White House, where Iran topped Tuesday's agenda. — Dan Scavino / Executive Office of the President (public domain, Wikimedia Commons)
In one sentence
Netanyahu's Washington visit yielded 'friendly' but inconclusive Iran talks, calming oil's wild week-long swings while Ryanair and easyJet still absorb a jet-fuel spike that hasn't unwound.

Benjamin Netanyahu landed in Washington on Monday under tightened security and walked into the Oval Office on Tuesday for a meeting billed, on both sides, as the moment to decide what comes next in the Iran war. Donald Trump came out of it sounding upbeat but careful not to promise anything. "We are having deep talks with Iran," he told Israel's News 12 before the sit-down, adding a warning alongside the warmth: "If they are not successful, we will return to powerful military action." Afterward, asked what had actually been settled, Trump was blunter still: "There was nothing definitive reached other than I insisted that negotiations with Iran continue to see whether or not a Deal can be consummated."

That word — inconclusive — is doing a lot of work for markets right now. Neither the US nor Iran has struck the other in three days, the longest lull in weeks, and mediators from Qatar and Pakistan, the latter reportedly backed by Beijing, are trying to revive an interim understanding that collapsed the last time a ceasefire looked close. Netanyahu, for his part, framed the visit as agenda-setting rather than deal-closing: "We will discuss all the issues on the table, with Iran at the forefront," he said before departure, with Lebanon and the West Bank also on the list. A pause is not a peace. It is a window, and traders are pricing it as exactly that.

Oil's whipsaw week

Crude has spent the past week doing what it does best when a war is this fluid: overreacting in both directions. Brent surged roughly 10% on reports of fresh escalation, then reversed hard — falling nearly 4% to around $96.80 a barrel on Friday as word spread that Pakistan and China were pushing to revive US-Iran talks, before sliding further, briefly below $90, once the strike pause itself was confirmed to be holding. That is not a market that has settled on an outcome. It is one flinching at every headline, which is itself the tell: four months into this conflict, nobody is willing to hold a directional position through a news cycle, let alone a Trump-Netanyahu meeting.

Airlines still paying for the spike

The oil desks can reprice by the hour. Airlines cannot, and that gap is where the real economic damage is sitting. European jet fuel has more than doubled since the war began, spot prices up over 120% to around $1,800 a metric tonne versus roughly $831 before the first strikes — and even Friday's crude relief will take weeks to show up in refined product. Ryanair and easyJet illustrate the two ways of surviving that: Ryanair has 80% of its fuel needs through March 2027 locked in at $67 a barrel, and used the last brief ceasefire window to hedge a further 15% of next year's requirements at $85; easyJet has 72% of this half's fuel hedged at $726 a tonne, leaving it more exposed each time it has to top up at spot. The results show the divergence. Ryanair's April-June pre-tax profit fell 34% to €593 million; easyJet's fell 70%, to £85 million from £286 million a year earlier, and its shares are down roughly a quarter since the war started. Both carriers say it is too early to issue a full-year forecast — an admission, in itself, that nobody in the industry believes this is over.

That is the thread running through all three stories. Oil traders, airline treasurers and now the White House are all working from the same premise: the shooting has paused, not stopped, and pricing anything — a barrel, a hedge, a diplomatic outcome — off the assumption that it has stopped would be a mistake. The next marker is less a date than a test: whether Qatar and Pakistan's mediators can lock in a firm date for resumed nuclear talks, or whether this lull, like the ones before it, expires quietly and the escalation trade comes back on.
