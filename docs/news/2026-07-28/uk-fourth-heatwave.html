Fourth UK heatwave of 2026 brings 35C and amber health alerts

Bottled water stocked ahead of an earlier 2026 heatwave at Leeds railway station. — Mtaylor848 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
UKHSA has issued amber heat-health alerts as the UK's fourth heatwave of 2026 pushes temperatures to 35C, straining a power grid already hit by three summer margin notices.

The UK Health Security Agency raised heat-health alerts to amber on Tuesday for London, the South East, the East of England and the East Midlands, with a yellow alert covering the West Midlands, South West and Yorkshire and the Humber. The Met Office expects Wednesday to bring the peak of the heat, with temperatures reaching 35C in parts of south-east England. It is the fourth spell of heatwave conditions the UK has recorded since May — a frequency without precedent in the modern weather record.

An amber alert is the second-highest tier UKHSA issues, meaning "significant impacts are likely across health and social care services," including a probable rise in deaths, concentrated among people over 65 and those with existing health conditions. The 35C peak forecast for this week sits well below the UK's all-time record of 40.3C, set at Coningsby, Lincolnshire, in July 2022. That gap matters less than it might seem: this is the fourth time in three months that the country has cleared 35C somewhere, a pattern the Met Office has already flagged as unprecedented for a single UK summer.

The toll from the earlier two heatwaves gives some sense of scale. Researchers estimate more than 2,700 excess deaths in England and Wales from the May and June events combined — roughly 550 during the May heatwave and 2,200 during June's, when temperatures hit 37.7C in Norfolk under the country's first-ever red heat alerts. Attribution analysis attached to those figures found that human-caused warming had pushed daytime maximums 3-4C higher than they would otherwise have been, and accounted for an estimated 42% of the heat-related deaths across both events.

A grid under repeated strain

The more immediate market signal is the electricity system. The National Energy System Operator has issued three separate summer electricity margin notices this year — a mechanism historically reserved for winter cold snaps — most recently on 8-9 July, when it forecast a shortfall of roughly 1,000-1,200MW during the 6.30pm-10.30pm demand peak. The mismatch is structural rather than incidental: heatwaves tend to arrive with the high-pressure, low-wind conditions that suppress output from Britain's wind fleet, just as air conditioning and fan use push demand higher in the evening, after solar generation has tailed off. Heat also degrades the efficiency of nuclear plants, gas stations and the water-cooling systems both depend on.

Rail operators have absorbed a parallel version of the same problem. June's heat forced speed restrictions and service cuts as rails expanded and overhead lines sagged, echoing a summer 2022 pattern that briefly halted East Coast services altogether. The Climate Change Committee's fourth risk assessment, published in May, concluded that UK infrastructure investment still leans toward carbon reduction rather than resilience — adaptation, it found, has been persistently underfunded relative to net-zero spending.

For markets, the question is less about any single alert and more about whether the response system holds up under repetition. A fourth amber warning in a season that has already produced two red alerts and thousands of excess deaths tests both public compliance — attention tends to dull with repeated warnings — and the physical margins of a grid built for a cooler climate. The next NESO margin notice, should one arrive this week, would be the fourth of the summer. The Met Office's extended outlook and UKHSA's alert dashboard remain the two indicators worth watching for whether this settles into a fifth event before autumn.
