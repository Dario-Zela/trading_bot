Unilever India profit slips as input costs bite

Hindustan Unilever's Garden Reach manufacturing facility in Kolkata. — Soumava2002 / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
HUL's quarterly profit fell as commodity costs outran price hikes, with volume growth lagging revenue growth 5% to 10% — a sign Indian consumers are pushing back.

Hindustan Unilever, the Mumbai-listed arm of the consumer goods giant that sells everything from Dove soap to Horlicks in India, reported a decline in quarterly profit on Tuesday even as revenue grew by double digits — a split that tells a more interesting story than either number alone.

Consolidated net profit attributable to shareholders fell 3 percent year-on-year to ₹2,673 crore (about $320m) for the quarter ended June 30, from ₹2,756 crore a year earlier, the company said. On a standalone basis the drop was steeper — 4 percent, to ₹2,631 crore from ₹2,732 crore. Net sales, by contrast, rose 10.1 percent to ₹17,341 crore. Shares fell as much as 5 percent on the print, touching a low of ₹2,064.30 on the National Stock Exchange.

Growth outran profit

HUL points partly to arithmetic: the year-ago quarter carried a one-off tax credit that inflated the comparison base, and pre-exceptional profit before tax was actually up 9.3 percent to ₹2,731 crore. But that doesn't explain away the operating picture. EBITDA rose 8 percent to ₹3,947 crore, yet the margin narrowed 40 basis points to 23 percent — inside the company's guided range, but moving the wrong direction. Underlying volume growth came in at 5 percent, roughly half the pace of revenue growth, meaning price increases did most of the heavy lifting on the top line rather than more units sold.

The company has been taking calibrated price hikes of 2 to 5 percent across its portfolio to offset commodity inflation, and named palm oil and packaging material as the chief culprits — palm oil in particular is a critical input across HUL's soaps, personal care and mass skin care lines, categories that make up a large share of its revenue. Reuters, citing the company, tied part of the raw-material pressure to knock-on effects from the Middle East war on input and freight costs.

Pricing power under strain

The more durable story sits underneath the quarter's noise. HUL has spent the past two years losing share to smaller domestic rivals such as Dabur, while quick-commerce platforms — Blinkit, Zepto, Instamart — chip away at the proprietary distribution network that long let large FMCG players set the terms of trade with retailers. Quick commerce is still a small slice of HUL's revenue, roughly 3 percent, but it has been doubling every quarter, and it compresses the margin HUL can extract at the point of sale even as it helps volumes.

That combination — commodity costs rising faster than the company can pass through, against a backdrop of eroding distribution leverage — is the more useful read for anyone using HUL as a proxy for emerging-market consumer health. A 40-basis-point margin slip is not a crisis. But it is evidence that Unilever's Indian consumers, price-sensitive by global standards even before this quarter, are pushing back against increases that a few years ago would have passed largely unnoticed.

The next test comes with the September-quarter print in October, when input-cost trends from the current commodity cycle will have had a full two quarters to work through pricing. Palm oil prices, still elevated through 2026 on the company's own guidance, are the number to watch between now and then.
