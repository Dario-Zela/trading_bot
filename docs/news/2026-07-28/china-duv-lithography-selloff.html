China's DUV lithography breakthrough triggers global chip-stock selloff

ASML's headquarters in Veldhoven, the Netherlands — the company holds a near-total monopoly on EUV lithography and dominates advanced DUV systems. — A ansems / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A report that a Chinese firm has begun mass-producing homegrown DUV chipmaking tools triggered a global chip-stock selloff, though analysts say the technology gap with ASML remains wide.

The chip-equipment sector took its sharpest one-day hit of the year on Monday after a report that a Chinese manufacturer has begun mass-producing its own deep ultraviolet lithography machines — the tool used to etch circuit patterns onto silicon wafers. ASML Holding, the Dutch company that until now supplied virtually all of the world's advanced lithography equipment, fell as much as 8% intraday before closing down roughly 6%. The damage spread well beyond Veldhoven: Applied Materials, Lam Research and KLA Corp all slid, Dutch supplier BE Semiconductor Industries dropped 9%, and chip designers Nvidia, AMD and Micron fell between 5% and 8%. SanDisk, more exposed to Chinese memory competition, lost 11%.

In one sentence: A report that a Chinese firm has begun mass-producing its own DUV chipmaking tools erased tens of billions in Western chip-sector value, even as analysts question how much ground has actually been closed.

What The Information reported

The trigger was a report from The Information that Shanghai Yuliangsheng Technology — a startup that consolidated lithography R&D teams from several Chinese firms and sits within the orbit of Huawei-linked chip-equipment group SiCarrier — has moved its homegrown immersion DUV scanner into low-volume production. The plan, per the report: five units delivered this year, rising to twenty in 2027, going to China's largest chipmakers — Semiconductor Manufacturing International Corp, Hua Hong Semiconductor and ChangXin Memory Technologies. The tool is designed for 28-nanometre-class production in a single exposure, the workhorse node for everything from car electronics to power management chips, though Chinese fabs have shown they can push older lithography further than expected using multi-patterning — the technique of layering several exposures to fake a finer feature size.

That detail matters because it is precisely the capability Washington has spent seven years trying to keep out of Chinese hands. ASML has been barred from selling its most advanced DUV systems to China since 2019, and immersion DUV — the version that uses a thin film of water to bend light and sharpen resolution — was the last major rung China had not climbed on its own.

How much ground has actually moved

Set against that headline, the caveats pile up quickly. Yuliangsheng's tool has been compared by industry analysts to ASML's Twinscan NXT:1950i, a machine ASML shipped in 2008 for 32-nanometre work — roughly two full product generations behind ASML's current NXT:2050i line, and nowhere near the extreme ultraviolet (EUV) machines that remain ASML's true monopoly. No credible Chinese alternative to EUV exists yet; most industry estimates still put that gap at the better part of a decade. Five tools delivered to three customers is also, bluntly, a pilot programme, not a production line — JPMorgan's Sandeep Deshpande noted that producing a handful of immersion DUV scanners is not the same as running them at the yields and reliability that high-volume chipmaking demands.

BofA's Didier Scemama went further, calling the sell-off a buying opportunity: even twenty domestic tools shipped next year, he estimated, would displace only about €1.4 billion of ASML's sales — 2.4% of projected group revenue. The Information's own reporting has not been independently corroborated by other outlets, and neither Yuliangsheng nor SMIC has confirmed the production numbers publicly.

Why the market flinched anyway

None of that stopped the sector from bleeding, and the reaction says as much about positioning as about the news itself. This is the second China-driven chip sell-off in ten days — a low-cost AI model from Moonshot AI, Kimi K3, knocked the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index down 11% in the week to July 17 and roughly 24% from its late-June peak, on fears that cheaper Chinese models would blunt demand for the priciest Western accelerators. Monday's news landed on a sector already primed to read any Chinese technical claim as evidence its moat is shallower than priced. That the rally into Monday had been built on easing Iran-related risk and a Wall Street Journal report that Nvidia is negotiating to guarantee $250 billion of financing for OpenAI data centres only sharpened the reversal — optimism about AI capital spending evaporating within hours on a single trade report out of Shanghai.

There is an irony sitting underneath the whole episode. Congress is currently advancing the MATCH Act, which would write a blanket ban on DUV lithography exports to China into statute — the House Foreign Affairs Committee called its April markup the largest export-control session in the chamber's history. The bill exists because Washington still assumes China needs Western tools to reach volume production. Monday's report, however thinly verified, is the first concrete case that the assumption may already be aging badly, and that tighter export controls could simply be accelerating the indigenisation they are meant to prevent.

What to watch

The report's real test comes at delivery, not announcement: whether Yuliangsheng actually ships five working tools to SMIC, Hua Hong and CXMT before year-end, and whether those fabs report usable yields on them in 2027. ASML reports third-quarter results in October, where China revenue trends and management's own read on the threat will carry more weight than a single trade report. And the MATCH Act's progress through the Senate — where a companion bill is still in committee — will show whether Monday's news hardens or softens appetite in Washington for the toughest DUV export regime yet proposed.
