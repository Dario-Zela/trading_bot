Royal Mail raises bulk-mail prices by a third, dealing blow to NHS mailouts

In one sentence
Royal Mail is raising bulk-mail tariffs by up to 36% from October, adding millions to NHS and HMRC postage bills with no real supplier alternative.

Somewhere in a sorting depot's pricing spreadsheet, a line item just moved by a third — and the people who will feel it are not the ones licking stamps at the kitchen table. From 5 October, Royal Mail is raising the wholesale tariffs it charges bulk-mail resellers — the companies that sort and inject letters into the network on behalf of banks, utilities and government departments — by an average of 25%, with some categories rising far more steeply. The cost of sending a bulk letter under 100g through Royal Mail's business economy service climbs 36.1%; large advertising mail rises 11.4%.

Those resellers — UK Mail, Whistl, Citipost among them — handle the letters that show up in millions of hallways as hospital appointment reminders, tax correspondence and benefits notices. The NHS alone spent more than £100m on postal services in 2024. A 25% increase, unevenly weighted toward the cheapest bulk categories the health service tends to use, could add several million pounds to that bill in a single year — money that either comes from elsewhere in NHS budgets or gets absorbed as a cost of doing business with a monopoly supplier that, for physical letters, has no real competitor.

Why Royal Mail says it has no choice

The company's own numbers explain the squeeze, if not excuse it. Access mail volumes — the bulk category at issue — have fallen from 6.3bn items a year in 2019-20 to about 4.2bn now, nearly a third gone in six years as email and app notifications replace paper. Royal Mail has racked up close to £800m in losses over the past four years. In a letter to bulk customers seen by the Guardian, it said the increase reflected "rising costs across our operation, including fuel and labour," adding that "the financial challenge of maintaining the UK's nationwide postal network supported by 130,000 colleagues remains significant." The Mail Users' Association called the rise "unprecedented" and warned it would "place considerable additional financial pressure on organisations that rely on mail."

A pattern, not an isolated hike

The timing does the company few favours. Daniel Křetínský, the Czech billionaire who took full control of Royal Mail in April 2025, was hauled before a select committee in March defending a delivery record that saw only 74.9% of first-class post arrive on time between April 2025 and January 2026, against a 93% target — a shortfall MPs linked directly to missed medical appointments. Ofcom has fined the company £37m since 2023 over missed targets, and last year loosened the universal service obligation itself, letting second-class post move to alternate-weekday delivery and trimming the official on-time targets. A price rise landing on top of a service that is already missing its own bar is the part critics are likeliest to seize on.

None of this moves markets in any direct sense — Royal Mail's parent, International Distribution Services, is privately held under Křetínský's EP Group, out of public-market reach since last year's takeover. But it is a small, clean illustration of cost inflation working through a piece of essential infrastructure that has no substitute good: prices up, service down, and a captive set of public-sector customers with nowhere else to send a letter. Watch for Ofcom's delayed review of postal pricing and affordability, which the regulator says will report before the existing second-class price cap expires in March 2027 — the point at which this argument about who absorbs rising costs gets formally revisited.
