FTSE hits highest close since February as AstraZeneca and Vodafone beat

Paternoster Square, next to the London Stock Exchange, in the City of London — Kaihsu Tai / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA)
In one sentence
AstraZeneca's 21% EPS beat and Vodafone's guidance raise pushed the FTSE 100 to its highest close since February, but the rally rests on two stocks and easing oil, not broad strength.

London's benchmark index closed at its highest level since the record set in February on Monday, adding 45.5 points to finish at 10,781.75 — a 0.4% gain built on two blue-chip earnings beats and a rare piece of good news on oil. AstraZeneca and Vodafone, both reporting before the opening bell, delivered exactly the kind of quarter fund managers had stopped expecting from either of them. Brent crude, meanwhile, slid below $90 a barrel as US and Iranian forces held off further strikes for a third straight night, trimming the inflation premium that has weighed on equities for weeks.

The rally's foundations, though, are narrower than the headline number suggests. Strip out the pharmaceutical giant and the telecoms operator, and Monday's advance for the wider index looks considerably thinner — a point worth holding in mind before reading too much into "highest since February" as a verdict on the UK economy.

Oncology carries the weight, cardiology drops it

AstraZeneca's second-quarter numbers were, by most measures, comfortably ahead of the City. Core earnings per share rose 21% year-on-year to $2.63, beating the consensus estimate of around $2.50, while group revenue climbed 5% to $15.38bn. Cancer drug sales — the company's single largest growth engine for the past decade — rose 15%, and rare-disease revenues, inherited largely through the 2021 Alexion acquisition, were up 8%. Shares in the FTSE 100's largest company by market value added close to 2% on the print.

Set against that was a setback that had been flagged as a risk for months and arrived anyway. The Phase III CARDIO-TTRansform trial for Wainua, developed with US partner Ionis Pharmaceuticals, failed to meet its primary endpoint — a composite of cardiovascular death and recurrent cardiac events — in patients with transthyretin-mediated amyloid cardiomyopathy, a rare and often fatal heart condition. A pre-specified subgroup of patients taking Wainua alone, without a background stabiliser drug, showed a nominally significant benefit, but the headline result was a miss on a drug that had been pencilled in as a meaningful contributor to AstraZeneca's next growth phase.

Chief executive Pascal Soriot did not try to explain the result away. "While we are disappointed by the CARDIO-TTRansform outcome, we are on track to deliver our $80bn total revenue ambition, which assumes successes and setbacks," he said, reaffirming the target the company set in 2024 for total annual revenue by 2030 — roughly double where it stands today. The company said it has no plans to accelerate dealmaking in response to the trial failure, pointing instead to more than twenty late-stage pipeline readouts expected over the next eighteen months. That framing is the tell: AstraZeneca is explicitly betting that a broad, risk-adjusted portfolio can absorb individual failures without denting the group target — a proposition that held on Monday because oncology did the heavy lifting, but one that has not yet been tested by a bad quarter in cancer as well.

Vodafone's guidance raise leans on Africa

Vodafone's update was the tidier story of the two. Organic service revenue grew 5.2% in the first quarter of its 2026/27 financial year, with adjusted EBITDA up 6.2%, and the group said it now expects to land at the upper end of its full-year guidance range — EBITDA of €13.0bn–€13.3bn and adjusted free cash flow of €2.6bn–€2.9bn. Shares were the best performers on the FTSE 100, gaining as much as 5% intraday.

The underlying growth was genuinely broad — Germany, the UK, other European markets and Türkiye all contributed, and Africa posted double-digit service-revenue growth. But the guidance raise itself is inseparable from a single corporate action: Vodafone's parent, Vodacom, completed the purchase of an additional 20% of Kenya's Safaricom on 30 June, lifting its stake to 55% and triggering full consolidation of Safaricom's results into Vodafone Group from 1 July. Total revenue for the quarter jumped almost 10% to €10.3bn, a figure flattered by that consolidation as much as by organic momentum in Europe. It is a real improvement in the underlying business, but investors reading the upgraded outlook as evidence of a turnaround in core European telecoms should note how much of the number is East African mobile-money and airtime revenue rather than German broadband subscribers.

What the rally actually says

Taken together, Monday's session is less a statement about the UK market's health than about two large-cap earnings beats landing on the same morning that oil prices happened to ease. AstraZeneca's result shows a company whose diversified pipeline can absorb a clinical failure without missing its own targets — so long as oncology keeps compounding at mid-teens rates. Vodafone's shows a telecoms group whose growth increasingly depends on consolidating fast-growing African assets rather than reviving saturated European ones. Both are legitimate wins for their respective managements. Neither, on its own, describes a broadening UK recovery.

The next test of that distinction comes fast. The Bank of England and the Federal Reserve both meet this week, with easing oil prices removing one obstacle to a less hawkish tone — a dynamic AstraZeneca and Vodafone's rally has already partly priced in. For AstraZeneca specifically, the calendar now points to that promised run of more than twenty pipeline readouts over the coming eighteen months: the mechanism by which the $80bn ambition either compounds or comes under renewed pressure.
