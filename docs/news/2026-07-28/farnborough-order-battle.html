Boeing edges Airbus in muted Farnborough order battle

A Boeing 737 MAX on a display flight — the jet at the center of SMBC Aviation Capital's 100-unit order at Farnborough. — Steve Lynes / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Boeing outpointed Airbus 186-157 in orders at a muted Farnborough, but both firms spent the week managing years-deep delivery backlogs rather than chasing new bookings.

Boeing left Farnborough with more orders than Airbus this year, but the number that mattered more to both companies was one nobody puts on a banner: the delivery slot.

By the time the Farnborough International Airshow closed on Friday, Boeing had logged 186 firm orders against Airbus's 157 — a 29-jet margin in a contest that, in years past, has been settled by the hundreds. The show's total haul, around 327 firm orders across all manufacturers, undershot the roughly 800 some analysts had floated beforehand and came in only modestly above the 300-plus organisers had predicted going in.

The week's single largest transaction did as much to even the field as anything either planemaker announced on its own. SMBC Aviation Capital, the Irish-based leasing giant, split a combined order for 200 narrowbodies straight down the middle: 100 Boeing 737 MAX jets — 60 of the largest 737-10 variant and 40 737-8s, the lessor's first-ever 737-10 order — and 100 Airbus A320neo-family aircraft, comprising 65 A321neos and 35 A320neos. Deliveries on both sides stretch into the early 2030s. That single deal accounted for well over half of the week's firm order count.

Backlogs, not bookings, set the tone

What made this Farnborough different wasn't the scoreline so much as the visible reluctance to chase one. Both manufacturers arrived with production essentially sold out for years — Airbus's narrowbody and widebody lines are booked into 2032–33, Boeing's into 2033–34 — and a combined backlog approaching 16,000 aircraft between them. Airlines and lessors, for their part, spent the week securing places in that queue rather than negotiating fresh discounts on speculative volume.

The pattern shows up in the year-to-date figures too: Airbus and Boeing had already booked 1,210 orders combined in the first half of 2026, above the 995-aircraft average for the same period from 2022 to 2025. Demand, in other words, has not cooled — it has simply stopped needing an airshow to prove itself. When a customer already knows it won't see a jet before 2033, there is little reason to wait for Farnborough's cameras to sign the contract.

For an industry that spent the years after the pandemic racing to out-order the other, that is a meaningful pivot. When backlogs run a decade deep, the investable question stops being who wins the order tally and becomes who executes the production ramp without slipping it — a much less forgiving test, and one that shows up in delivery numbers and margins long before it shows up in an airshow press release.

The next checkpoint is more mundane than a trade show: monthly delivery figures. Boeing's and Airbus's July and August tallies, along with any commentary on 737 MAX and A320neo production-rate increases, will show whether either manufacturer is actually closing the backlog gap or merely, like this Farnborough, adding to it.
