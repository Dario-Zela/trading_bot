China's disaster response faces new challenge from AI-generated fake videos

Fire and rescue crews respond to flooding in China — genuine emergency operations are increasingly competing online with AI-fabricated disaster footage. — Windmemories / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
AI-generated fake disaster videos are spreading in China faster than officials can verify them, complicating real flood and landslide response.

China's summer flood season has produced two disasters running in parallel. One is real: Typhoon Maysak and weeks of record rainfall have killed dozens across Guangxi, Hebei and Gansu, breached reservoirs and forced hundreds of thousands of evacuations. The other is synthetic: a wave of AI-generated video depicting collapses, floods and deaths that never happened, spreading through WeChat and short-video platforms faster than anyone can check them.

The clearest case so far comes from Hebei. In mid-July, a 45-year-old man surnamed Chen, from Chengde, used AI tools to generate a video purporting to show the ground collapsing in Kuancheng county during heavy rain, with cars sliding into the water. He posted it to WeChat, according to police, simply to draw followers. It worked — the clip triggered panic among residents and, more consequentially, diverted attention from actual flood-response coordination in the area. Chen was handed an administrative penalty. In Guangdong, a woman surnamed Li was punished for a separate fabricated claim that a stadium being used for flood control was "filled with bodies."

These are not isolated pranks. China's Ministry of Public Security has disclosed 20 such cases under a campaign called Clean Net 2026, run jointly by the Cyberspace Administration of China and the Ministry of Emergency Management. The tactics follow a pattern: old footage stripped of watermarks and re-dated to a current disaster, real clips relabelled as being from a different province, and increasingly, video generated wholesale by AI with no underlying event at all.

The verification gap

What makes this a structural problem rather than a policing one is timing. Emergency responders and fact-checkers still work at the speed of confirmation — dispatching someone to check a location, cross-referencing a timestamp, waiting for an official bulletin. Generative video tools work at the speed of a prompt. During the Guangxi flooding, which killed 39 people and left nine missing after a reservoir breach near Nanning in early July, genuine rescue footage was already competing online with recycled clips from unrelated floods years earlier. Layering AI fabrication on top of that recycling problem means a rumour can now originate from nothing at all, rather than merely being real footage wrongly labelled.

That asymmetry matters most in the first hours of a crisis, when authorities are trying to direct limited boats, drones and personnel to where people are actually trapped. A viral but false claim about a collapsed road or a submerged town can pull attention and resources toward the wrong place, exactly the disruption police cited in the Kuancheng case.

Beijing's response so far has been punitive and reactive — administrative fines, account takedowns, a naming-and-shaming campaign — rather than a faster verification pipeline. With China's flood season running into September and a fresh flash flood killing ten people in Gansu on July 27, the test is whether authorities can get ahead of the next fabricated clip before it outruns the truth again.
