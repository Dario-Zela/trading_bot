Adobe climbs even as IBM warning rattles software stocks

Adobe's headquarters in San Jose, California — Coolcaesar / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Adobe jumped alongside Salesforce and Workday in a broad software rebound, muddying claims its rise reflects company-specific shelter rather than a one-day rotation trade.

Adobe shares rose 6.9% on Monday to close at $240.56, one of the sharper single-day moves the stock has made all year — and a notably contrarian one, given that Adobe was among the names an IBM profit warning had knocked down just two weeks earlier. The bounce raises a fair question for anyone using price action to infer something about the business: is this evidence Adobe has found shelter from the AI-disruption story that has dogged it all year, or just a crowded trade unwinding on a quiet Monday?

In one sentence: Adobe jumped alongside Salesforce and Workday in a broad software-sector rebound, muddying claims that its rise reflects company-specific strength rather than a one-day rotation trade.

The IBM scare

The episode traces back to July 14, when IBM shocked investors with a preliminary earnings warning — adjusted profit of $2.93 a share on $17.2 billion in revenue, short of the $3.01 and $17.86 billion Wall Street wanted. The stock fell as much as 23% in premarket trading, its worst single session since Black Monday in 1987. CEO Arvind Krishna's explanation was specific: enterprise clients had redirected late-quarter budget toward memory chips and servers made scarce by the ongoing shortage, leaving software and consulting contracts unsigned. He later said roughly a third of those deferred agreements had already been signed in early July, framing it as timing rather than lost demand.

Markets didn't wait for that nuance. The warning dragged down the whole software complex on fears that IBM's budget-reshuffling problem was systemic rather than IBM's alone. ServiceNow fell 5.8%, Adobe dropped 4.3%, Salesforce lost 2.1%, and Workday, HubSpot and Datadog all slid with them.

The reversal

What happened next looked less like Adobe-specific vindication and more like the whole trade reversing at once. On July 27, Salesforce rose 7% to $176, ServiceNow rose 8% to $107 and Workday rose 10% to $149 — alongside Adobe's 6.9% — in what several trading desks described as a coordinated sector rotation rather than news-driven moves in any single name. All three of those peers had been sitting on steep year-to-date losses: Salesforce down 33%, ServiceNow off 45% over the trailing year, Workday down 30%. Adobe's own case is at least as extreme — the stock had fallen roughly 30% earlier in 2026 as investors repriced creative software for the risk that generative tools would erode Creative Cloud subscriptions, compounded by the announced departure of longtime CEO Shantanu Narayen. It now trades at a forward price-to-earnings ratio near a 10-year low.

The fundamental news that Monday came from ServiceNow, not Adobe. ServiceNow's second-quarter results, reported July 22, beat on both lines — 90 cents a share in adjusted earnings against $3.99 billion in revenue, with subscription revenue up 24.5% year-on-year and full-year subscription guidance raised to $15.76–15.78 billion. That's a genuine data point. Whether it says anything about Adobe's own subscription durability is a separate question, and the read-through logic — ServiceNow's enterprise workflow software did well, therefore Adobe's creative software is safer — is thinner than the price move implies.

Shelter or noise

Apollo's chief economist, Torsten Slok, has been making a version of this argument that cuts against Adobe rather than for it. Slok's framework splits software into platforms that AI consumes and depends on — security, infrastructure, the plumbing underneath model deployment — and legacy application vendors it threatens to disrupt or commoditize. The former group, he argues, has pulled away from the index; the latter has been repriced downward. Adobe sits squarely in the second camp: a subscription-software incumbent whose core product, generative image and video tooling, is precisely the category AI models are built to replace rather than support. That its stock rallied on the same day as three infrastructure-adjacent SaaS names, rather than diverging from them, is if anything mild evidence against a distinct Adobe narrative and in favor of a single crowded short unwinding across the sector.

None of this means Adobe's underlying business is in trouble. Firefly's annualized recurring revenue crossed $250 million last quarter, up 75% sequentially, and management raised full-year guidance on the back of AI-feature adoption inside Creative Cloud and Document Cloud — even as the same executives conceded Firefly is cannibalizing the traditional stock-photo business faster than expected. Those are the numbers that will actually settle the disruption argument. A single Monday of sector-wide short covering will not.

Adobe next reports fiscal third-quarter earnings in September, the first real chance to see whether Firefly's growth is offsetting seat-based subscription pressure or merely delaying it. Until then, the more useful signal than any one day's stock move is whether the IGV software ETF — which now holds Adobe alongside the same peers that moved in lockstep on July 27 — starts trading as a basket again or begins to split along the winners-and-losers line Slok has been drawing.
