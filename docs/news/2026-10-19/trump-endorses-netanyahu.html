Trump gives Netanyahu a 'complete and total endorsement' ahead of Israel's election

In one sentence
Trump endorsed Netanyahu on Truth Social nine days before Israel's 27 October election, tying US support on Iran to his victory while Netanyahu trails Eisenkot in polls.

President Donald Trump has publicly backed Benjamin Netanyahu's bid to stay on as Israel's prime minister, nine days before voters go to the polls. In a post on Truth Social on Sunday 18 October, he called it his "Complete and Total Endorsement of Bibi Netanyahu". The move reverses the position he took only days earlier. It also puts a question to Washington and Jerusalem: how openly can one democracy's leader campaign in another's election?

The endorsement

The post was built around Iran, not around Israeli domestic politics. According to the Jewish Telegraphic Agency's account, Trump called Netanyahu "a Great Warrior Prime Minister". He said the Iranian threat would persist unless Tehran was destroyed militarily and financially. He added that "we will soon finish the job", but only if Netanyahu remains in office.

The sharpest passage was a warning. If Israel chose "anyone other than Bibi", Trump wrote, America would have to do the work itself, and he was "not inclined to do so". That makes the endorsement a statement about US commitment as well as a statement of preference. Voters are being told that the choice of prime minister bears on how far Washington will keep fighting alongside Israel.

The timing matters too. Reports say Trump declined to back any candidate in a Newsmax interview the previous Thursday. In early October, Al Jazeera described him as having avoided an endorsement despite White House visits and public praise. His relations with Netanyahu have been uneven this year. Some outlets recall he had criticised the Israeli leader sharply in recent months, though the coverage seen for this article does not fix the details.

An election Netanyahu is not winning

Israel's Knesset election falls on 27 October. That is the end of a full four-year term, which Israeli outlets describe as a first since 1988. Recent surveys have shown Netanyahu's Likud trailing a party led by Gadi Eisenkot, the former chief of the general staff. Other polling has put Netanyahu's right-wing and religious bloc at roughly 49 to 53 seats, short of the 61 needed for a majority in the 120-seat chamber. Polls have not shown a clear route to power for his rivals either, so the outcome will depend on coalition arithmetic as much as on first place.

In that setting, a foreign endorsement is a practical resource. Netanyahu has leaned heavily on his association with Trump. On the eve of the endorsement he told i24News that an Eisenkot victory would let Iran obtain nuclear weapons, using the slogan "It's right or nuclear". He also spoke warmly of Trump on Fox News the same day.

Reaction

Eisenkot answered with some care. He called Trump a great friend of Israel but said the election was an internal Israeli matter. He said he had asked that there be no interference. He acknowledged meetings with the Trump administration without giving details.

The coalition's response was similar in form. Bezalel Smotrich, who leads the Religious Zionist Party, said the vote was a domestic matter but urged Israelis to "listen to the president". Both sides therefore cited non-interference while taking different lessons from it.

In the United States, Brian Romick, head of Democratic Majority for Israel, called on Trump to retract the endorsement, describing it as "irresponsible and reckless".

What it tests

Foreign endorsements are not new. Presidents and prime ministers have long signalled favourites, and Trump himself has weighed in on elections abroad. The Israeli case is different in degree. The two states are bound by security commitments and a shared campaign against Iran, and Trump has tied his own future involvement to the result.

There is a risk for Netanyahu as well. Israeli voters are sensitive to appearing to take instructions from Washington. Eisenkot's reply is aimed at exactly that sensitivity. Whether Trump's popularity in Israel outweighs it is an empirical question that only the count will settle.

One caution applies to the sourcing. The first brief on the endorsement came from Benzinga, a finance aggregator, which framed it partly around crypto betting markets. The wording quoted here is taken from the JTA's account of the Truth Social post, and other outlets reported the endorsement independently. The full post text was not retrieved directly for this article.

The next marker is 27 October. Final polls will show whether the endorsement moved any seats. After the count, coalition talks will begin, and with no bloc near a clear majority they may run for weeks. Trump's pledge, that the work on Iran continues only under Netanyahu, will face its first test there.
