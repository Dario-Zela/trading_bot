Burnham urged to launch inquiry into unequal health impacts of air pollution

In one sentence
A letter from 150 scientists, MPs and campaigners asks Prime Minister Andy Burnham for a national inquiry into how air pollution harms some communities more than others.

A letter signed by 150 scientists, MPs and campaigners asks Prime Minister Andy Burnham to set up a national inquiry into how air pollution harms some communities more than others. The signatories say the UK has no dedicated mechanism for examining those health inequalities. The signatories include the climate activist Greta Thunberg. The reporting available at the time of writing, an AOL syndication of the Guardian's account, does not record a reply from Mr Burnham.

What the letter says

The argument is about who breathes the worst air as well as how much pollution there is. The letter draws on research from the Institute for Fiscal Studies, which finds that lower-income areas of England have persistently higher pollution than wealthier ones. It also reports that minority-ethnic populations are exposed to levels about 6% higher than white populations. (Both figures are as reported; the underlying IFS paper was not reviewed for this article.)

The letter also leans on freedom of information requests made by Mums for Lungs, a campaign group. Its data indicate that in 2025 more than 200,000 children were taken to hospital with breathing difficulties across 11 English cities and areas, and more than 120,000 of them were babies and toddlers. In Manchester, admissions of babies and toddlers rose from 653 in 2023 to 1,072 in 2025.

These are hospital admission counts from a campaign group's requests. They do not show that pollution caused any given admission, and they do not adjust for changes in population, recording practice or other triggers such as infection. They do, however, show the direction of travel that the campaigners want examined.

The health case

Long-term exposure to polluted air is linked to heart disease, stroke, cancer and asthma, and to effects on children's lung development. Traffic emissions are the leading threat to clean air. The Royal College of Physicians, in evidence to a Commons Environmental Audit Committee inquiry, has similarly said that people in deprived areas and minoritised ethnic communities are more likely to face higher exposure and to be more vulnerable to its effects.

There is some evidence that policy can help. London's ultra-low emission zone has been linked to bigger, stronger lungs in children, according to the reporting. Parts of London, Glasgow and Bristol still exceeded legal pollution limits in 2025, according to new government data.

Rosamund Adoo-Kissi-Debrah, whose daughter Ella's death certificate cited air pollution, blamed a "failure of inaction by all the main political parties". Jemima Hartshorn, Mums for Lungs' founder, called the situation an "air pollution emergency" and said: "Behind every number is a child who is struggling to breathe and a family looking for help."

Mr Burnham's record and the government's position

The letter lands awkwardly for its addressee. Mums for Lungs notes that, as mayor of Greater Manchester, Mr Burnham scrapped plans for a clean air zone, which it says raises questions about whether enough is being done. The date given in the reporting (2023) differs from that in some other coverage, so it is best treated as approximate.

A Defra spokesperson said air quality had improved over the long term and that most standards were being met, but that more action was needed. The department pointed to new targets to cut exposure to harmful particles by nearly a third by 2030 and said it would keep working with local authorities through clean air plans and monitoring.

What to watch

This remains a campaign request, not a commissioned inquiry. As prime minister, Mr Burnham could order a review or back a parliamentary one, and a formal response to the letter would be the first sign of which. Whether the government accepts the premise, that exposure should be measured and addressed by community and not only by national average, will shape local clean air plans and the 2030 particle target.
