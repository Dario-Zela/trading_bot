Inside the anti-Putin sabotage networks operating within Russia

In one sentence
A BBC investigation reportedly examines anti-Putin sabotage networks inside Russia, but its findings could not be checked, and public claims about such groups remain largely unverified.

The headline promises a look inside the covert networks said to be working against the Kremlin from within Russia. The BBC investigation behind it could not be independently reviewed for this article, so its findings are not reported here. What can be set out is the wider picture of how such networks are described, and why every claim about them needs careful handling.

What is publicly claimed

Since the full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, a loose set of groups has claimed responsibility for acts of sabotage inside Russia. The targets are usually railway relays, military recruitment offices and infrastructure linked to the war effort. Some describe themselves as partisans, and some say they are opposed to Vladimir Putin's government rather than to Russia itself.

One recent example is a documentary by the journalist Jake Hanrahan, Russia's Anti-Putin Underground, covered by Radio Free Europe/Radio Liberty. In it, two Russian "partisans" meet the filmmaker in a forest near the border. They say about 30 cells operate across the country and are united, for now, by the aim of undermining the Kremlin. Those figures are the interviewees' own. Nothing found in the search for this article corroborates them.

Why the evidence is thin

Covert networks are hard to document. Participants have every reason to exaggerate their numbers and reach, and the Russian authorities have equal reason to inflate the threat, since it justifies prosecutions and tighter security. Foreign intelligence services, Ukraine's among them, are widely suspected of encouraging or directing some of the activity. Few of those claims can be tested from outside Russia.

Sabotage also runs in the other direction. Separate BBC reporting this year linked Russia to an arson campaign targeting property connected to the UK prime minister, Keir Starmer. Two men were convicted at the Old Bailey. British police said they had not proved the identity of the alleged handler or found evidence of a state-backed threat. The case is a reminder that the label "sabotage network" covers very different things, and that attribution is usually contested.

What to watch

The question for any account of anti-Kremlin networks is what it actually shows: named incidents, dates, court records or physical evidence, as opposed to testimony from masked interviewees. Russian court filings on sabotage and treason cases are the most checkable public source. Official reaction in Moscow to the BBC's reporting would also be a signal, as would any response from Kyiv. Until the BBC's material is available to read in full, the scale of these networks, and any outside hand in them, remains unestablished.
