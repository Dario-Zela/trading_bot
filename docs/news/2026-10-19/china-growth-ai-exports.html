China's growth picks up on AI exports while domestic demand stays weak

In one sentence
China's third-quarter growth edged up to 4.5%, led by AI-related exports, while property investment fell 20.6% and retail sales rose only 1.1%.

China's economy grew 4.5% in the third quarter from a year earlier. That was a little faster than the 4.3% of the second quarter, which had been the weakest pace in more than three years, and a little ahead of the 4.4% forecast in a Reuters poll. The National Bureau of Statistics (NBS) published the figures on Monday. Most of the lift came from factories selling technology goods abroad. Households and builders at home did little to help.

The numbers

On a quarter-on-quarter basis, output rose 1.2%, against 1.1% expected and 0.9% in the second quarter. Growth for the first nine months stands at 4.6%, inside Beijing's full-year target range of 4.5% to 5%. The bottom of that range is close enough that the target is not yet at risk, but there is not much margin above it.

September activity data show the same pattern:

  • Industrial output rose 5.5% year on year, up from 5.2% in August and the fastest in six months.
  • Retail sales rose 1.1%, against about 0.5% expected and 0.4% in August. That is the quickest since March, though still very low growth for a consumer economy.
  • Fixed-asset investment fell 7.2% in January to September. Property investment fell 20.6% and infrastructure investment fell 4.2%.
  • New home prices are still falling, though more slowly than before.

An export story

The strength in factory output was tied to exports of AI-related technology goods. The trade surplus widened by more than expected in September. It is on course to exceed last year's record of $1.2 trillion, according to market reports of the release.

The composition of the export boom deserves some caution. Earlier in the year, Capital Economics attributed June's 27% jump in exports mainly to higher semiconductor prices. One analyst noted that the volume of chip exports actually fell year on year that month. Later data showed semiconductor export values more than doubling while volumes rose only 4.1%. Part of the headline strength is therefore price, not units shipped, and it depends on how long the AI-driven pricing holds.

The headline growth rate still looks like an external-demand rebound rather than a consumer-led recovery. The retail figure is the only domestic number that surprised on the upside, and a 1.1% rise is not a recovery. Investment is contracting at a pace that would, in most economies, be a recession signal. The NBS release and the market reports of it do not, on the evidence available, show household spending or construction picking up.

What follows

Two things matter now. The first is whether Beijing adds support for domestic demand or treats the export lift as enough to meet the target. Stimulus has already been rolling out, in the words of one headline on the data, and the property and investment figures suggest it has not yet reached the economy at scale. The second is trade friction. A surplus heading above $1.2 trillion has already drawn complaints, including from the EU over hybrid vehicle shipments, and a larger surplus raises the odds of further tariff action.

The next checkpoints are the October trade data in early November, and the fourth-quarter GDP release in January, which will show whether the full-year figure lands inside the 4.5% to 5% range.
