Burnham invites Swinney to work together on 'deeper Scottish devolution'

In one sentence
Andy Burnham has invited John Swinney to work together on 'deeper Scottish devolution', but as a mayor he has no formal role in the Scottish settlement, so its weight depends on Labour backing.

Andy Burnham, the Mayor of Greater Manchester, has invited Scotland's First Minister, John Swinney, to work with him on what he calls 'deeper Scottish devolution'. The offer, first reported by BBC Politics, is short on public detail. Whether it is a real intergovernmental proposal or a piece of political positioning depends mostly on who is behind it.

An offer from an unusual quarter

Swinney has led the SNP and the Scottish government since May 2024, so the invitation goes to the head of a devolved administration. Burnham's office is a different kind of thing. A metro mayor is an English local leader with powers over transport, housing and some skills and economic policy, agreed with Whitehall through bespoke 'devolution deals'. The post has no formal part in how powers are shared between London and Edinburgh. Changes to the Scottish settlement are made by the UK government and Parliament, usually after talks with Holyrood.

That makes standing the first question. A UK government offer would be a statement of intent from the party that governs Westminster. An invitation from one mayor binds nobody. It would still be a signal, because Burnham has long been among Labour's most prominent regional voices and has argued for more power to move away from Westminster. Coverage seen so far does not establish that he speaks for ministers or for the party leadership. This article does not assume he does.

Reading the move

There are two plausible readings.

The first is that this is a gesture. Burnham has built his profile on the case for devolution within England, and extending the argument to Scotland is a natural step. It also puts a question to the SNP: whether it would cooperate with a Labour figure on powers short of independence. Swinney's party exists to pursue independence, so any reply is likely to be careful. Accepting without conditions risks looking like a retreat from that goal. Rejecting the offer outright risks looking churlish when more powers could be on the table.

The second reading is that it tests ground for a wider Labour approach. If the party's leadership wanted to explore a new settlement, a mayor with his own mandate could float ideas that ministers could not yet endorse. There is no public evidence for this in what has been reported. Several things would be telling: any statement from Downing Street or the Scotland Office, a reference to a specific power such as employment law, borrowing or energy, or a reply from Swinney's government that goes beyond politeness.

What to watch

The first marker is the response from Edinburgh. A formal reply, or an invitation to talks, would suggest the SNP sees something worth pursuing. A pointed dismissal would suggest it sees a party-political move. The second is whether Labour's national leadership endorses, qualifies or distances itself from the offer, since silence from ministers would also be informative. Until one of those arrives, the invitation is best treated as a statement of Burnham's own position rather than a change in the UK's constitutional settlement.

The details of what Burnham proposed, and the precise wording of the invitation, were not independently verified when this article was written.
