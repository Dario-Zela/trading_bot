How to protect yourself from AI scams

In one sentence
UK APP fraud losses rose 19% to £576.4m in 2025, banks cite AI as a factor, and the fight is over whether tech firms should share the refund bill.

Advice on spotting AI scams is easy to find. What is harder to find is evidence that the problem is growing, and who pays when it works. The latest industry figures give a partial answer: losses to the kind of fraud that AI makes easier are rising, banks pay back most of the in-scope cases, and the banks want technology and telecoms companies to share the bill.

What the numbers show

UK Finance, the banks' trade body, published its annual fraud report in June, covering 2025. Losses from authorised push payment (APP) fraud, where the victim is tricked into sending money themselves, rose 19% to £576.4m, the highest since 2021. Personal customers lost £500.8m and businesses £57.6m. Reported APP cases rose 7% to 248,070.

The growth was concentrated in scams that depend on persuasion. Investment fraud rose 40% to £221.5m and romance fraud rose 23% to £39.2m. Impersonation fraud fell 12%. Two-thirds of APP cases began online, and those cases accounted for more than 30% of losses.

Total payment fraud was about £1.2bn–£1.28bn, depending on how the report's categories are counted, and coverage of the report differs on whether that total was up or down on the year. The APP figure is the clearer one, and it is rising.

UK Finance does not say how much of the loss is AI-enabled. It does say AI is a factor. "Particularly with the advent of AI, it lowers the barrier to entry for criminals," said Ruth Ray, its managing director of economic crime. The argument is that AI makes scam messages more convincing, lets criminals send them in bulk, and lets them pose as celebrities, friends or relatives, including through cloned voices. That is an industry assessment, not a measured share of losses, and the report offers no such split.

Who pays

Since October 2024, banks and payment firms have had to refund APP fraud victims up to £85,000 unless the customer was grossly negligent. The Payment Systems Regulator reported that 88% of in-scope APP fraud was reimbursed in the rules' first year. Banks returned £354.3m in 2025. One press summary of the report puts the reimbursed share of all APP losses at 61%, which suggests a sizeable slice falls outside the rules or is not repaid. Those customers carry the loss themselves.

The evidence does not show banks pushing costs onto customers. The rules point the other way, and the UK is the only country to mandate reimbursement. What the banks are pushing is a wider split. Ray said "we cannot be the only line of defence" and called for "stronger, enforceable responsibilities" on technology platforms and telecoms firms, since most APP fraud starts on their services. TechUK, which represents the tech sector, opposes this, arguing that compulsory contributions would undermine voluntary cooperation that is already producing results.

The advice, briefly

The standard guidance still applies, and it works because it does not depend on spotting a fake:

  • Treat any urgent request for money as suspect, however familiar the voice or face. Hang up and call back on a number already saved or printed on a card.
  • Agree a code word with close family for emergency requests.
  • Be wary of investment or romance approaches that start online, the two categories growing fastest.
  • Report scams to Action Fraud and tell the bank at once, because reimbursement depends on reporting and on the customer not having been grossly negligent.

What next

The argument to watch is whether the government makes technology and telecoms firms contribute to reimbursement, which UK Finance wants and techUK resists. The next annual report, covering 2026, will show whether APP losses kept rising.
