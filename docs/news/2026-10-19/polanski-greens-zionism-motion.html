Polanski will not overturn Green Party 'Zionism is racism' motion

In one sentence
Zack Polanski has declined to reverse a conference motion declaring Zionism racism, which passed 1,022 to 813 and has left the Greens at odds with Jewish groups and most of their own MPs.

The leader of the Green Party of England and Wales has said he will not overturn a conference motion declaring that "Zionism is racism", according to BBC reporting. The motion has split his party and drawn condemnation from Jewish organisations. The BBC report is the only source for the refusal itself. The wording of Polanski's statement and his stated reasons could not be independently confirmed, and the account below relies on earlier coverage of the vote and his reaction to it.

The vote

The motion passed at the party's conference in Brighton by 1,022 votes to 813. It says Zionism — defined as the movement supporting a Jewish state — should be "treated as any other form of racism". It also backs replacing Israel with a single state with "equal rights for all". Three amendments were rejected. One of them would have said the policy should not be used to "pressure, proscribe or discipline members based on affiliation with Jewish community institutions".

Zack Polanski, who is Jewish, was not in the hall. He had left to campaign in the Holborn and St Pancras by-election, which ITV reports he went on to lose to Labour. In a BBC Breakfast interview he said he would not have backed the motion in its current form, calling it "imperfect". He agreed with its central argument but objected to dictating the shape of a future state. He has also said he is an anti-Zionist who believes Zionism is racist. He said afterwards that he would "not accept any exclusion, or refusal to engage with anyone, because of the religious or communal body they are part of".

The reaction

The response was swift. Chief Rabbi Sir Ephraim Mirvis called the motion "poisonous antisemitism". The Movement for Progressive Judaism said the party had made antisemitism party policy. Nine Labour MPs referred the party to the Equality and Human Rights Commission. The European Green Party said it would "unequivocally distance" itself from the resolution. Israel's foreign minister, Gideon Sa'ar, barred Polanski from entering the country.

The party is not united behind the text. Four of its five MPs opposed it, as did the former leader Caroline Lucas, and its two deputy leaders split. Some Jewish Greens have privately said the policy could make it hard for them to stay.

Why the refusal matters

The Greens have grown quickly under Polanski, and many of the new members now shape conference outcomes. The party's rules give conference, not the leader, the final say on policy. That means Polanski has little room to reverse the motion unilaterally. ITV reported last week that he had signalled leaders could be given more power over policy. That suggests he sees the structure as the problem, though it is not a route to undoing this vote.

The motion is also not binding in the way it may appear. Reporting on the vote noted that it does not necessarily carry into the next manifesto and that MPs are not obliged to support it. This leaves a gap between formal policy and what the party's elected representatives will say and do. That gap is where the dispute is likely to continue.

The leader's position is awkward. He shares the motion's core claim but not its drafting, and he is declining to undo it. That puts him at odds with most of his MPs and with the communal bodies he says he wants to keep talking to.

What to watch

Three things will show whether this settles or widens. The first is whether the Equality and Human Rights Commission acts on the Labour MPs' referral. The second is whether the Green leadership brings forward any rule change on policymaking, or a revised motion, at a later conference. The third is whether the party's MPs, or Jewish members, begin to leave. A defection or a formal split would be a bigger story than the refusal itself.
