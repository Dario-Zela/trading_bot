'Postcode lottery' is driving children's inequality, commissioner warns

In one sentence
England's children's commissioner says where a child lives increasingly determines their outcomes, raising the question of whether the state delivers equal childhoods.

England's children's commissioner has warned that where a child grows up is playing a growing part in how their life turns out, reviving a long-running complaint about a "postcode lottery" in public services and recasting it as a question about whether the state delivers anything like an equal childhood.

The warning, reported by BBC News, comes from the office of the children's commissioner for England, a statutory post held by Dame Rachel de Souza. The commissioner's role is to speak for children to government and to publish evidence on how they fare. The central claim is that geography has become a stronger predictor of outcomes for children, not a weaker one.

The full report and its underlying figures were not available when this article was written. The specific measures, the areas compared and the size of the gaps are therefore not set out here, and the headline's "postcode lottery" phrasing should be read as shorthand for the commissioner's broader finding rather than a verified quotation.

Why the claim matters

"Postcode lottery" is an old phrase in British politics, usually attached to a single service: a cancer drug available in one health authority and not the next, or a school-places scramble that differs from one council to its neighbour. Those complaints are about local variation in delivery, and the usual answer is better local management.

The commissioner's claim is different in kind. If place increasingly determines outcomes across a child's life, the explanation is unlikely to be one badly run service. It points to something structural: uneven funding, uneven local economies, or national policies whose effects land differently depending on where a family lives.

That is also a claim about direction of travel. The test for any government that promises to "level up" or narrow regional gaps is whether the gaps close over time. A finding that they are tightening their grip on children's prospects would be an uncomfortable verdict on that record, whichever party holds office.

The policy argument

The finding adds weight to calls for place-based funding, in which money follows local need rather than national formulas alone. Councils have argued for years that demand for children's social care, special educational needs support and early help has risen fastest in places with the least capacity to pay for it. A commissioner's evidence that geography matters more would strengthen that case.

There is a counter-argument. Local differences in outcomes can reflect differences in family income, housing and employment as much as the quality of children's services, and spending alone does not always change them. Disentangling the two is what the detail of the commissioner's data will have to do, and it is where ministers are likely to challenge the conclusions.

The story is domestic social policy with little direct bearing on markets, but it sits alongside other debates about regional unevenness, including youth joblessness and unequal health burdens from pollution, which are covered elsewhere in this edition.

What to watch

The first test is the government's response. Ministers will be asked whether they accept that the gap is widening and whether any funding settlement for councils or schools will be redirected towards the areas the commissioner identifies. The second is the detail of the report itself: which outcomes, which places, and over what period. Without those, the headline claim is a serious warning but not yet one that can be measured.
