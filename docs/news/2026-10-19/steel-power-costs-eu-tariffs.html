Steel under squeeze: UK power costs widen the gap to EU rivals while EU tariffs on China catch Ukrainian mills

In one sentence
UK steelmakers' electricity costs have slipped to their worst position against EU rivals in three years, while EU steel tariffs aimed at China are also catching Ukrainian mills.

UK steelmakers are paying more for electricity relative to their European competitors than at any point in three years, according to a Guardian report this week. At the same time, a steel executive told the BBC that EU measures aimed at Chinese steel are catching Ukrainian producers. The two stories have different causes. Both show an industry whose margins depend on decisions made in capitals, not on its own plants.

The Guardian's exact comparison — the size of the gap, the period it covers and which EU countries it uses — was not available to this desk at the time of writing, and the figures below come from earlier published work. The BBC's account is likewise a single-source report.

The power-price gap

Steel is among the most electricity-hungry things Britain makes. That matters more now than it did a decade ago, because the sector is moving from blast furnaces to electric arc furnaces, the route Tata Steel and British Steel have both chosen. Arc furnaces melt scrap with electricity, and they could roughly double a steelmaker's power use. Power prices therefore decide whether the new plants can compete.

The gap is not new. In 2018 UK Steel, the trade body, put the average price paid by UK steelmakers at about £65 per megawatt hour, against about £43 in Germany and £31 in France. By its 2023/24 figures the UK average was about £113/MWh, against around £61 in Germany and France. In mid-2024, wholesale power in Britain averaged nearly £66/MWh over a three-month stretch, while France and Spain were near £27 and £28. UK Steel said at the time that the government's Industrial Supercharger scheme, which discounts network charges for energy-intensive industries, had not closed the gap.

The Guardian's finding that the position is now the worst in three years therefore describes a gap that has widened again after a period of narrowing. It does not describe a new problem.

Tariffs that reach past China

The second thread concerns Europe's own steel. The EU has been tightening its defences against surplus steel, much of it blamed on Chinese overcapacity. According to trade-press coverage from May and June this year, Brussels planned to cut duty-free import quotas by country and apply a 50% tariff to volumes above them, up from 25%. Because the quotas apply to trading partners generally, they cover Ukraine, which has a free-trade agreement with the bloc.

Ukrainian executives have protested. Interpipe's chief executive, Luca Zanotti, said Ukraine would face country-specific limits even though the EU had granted it an exemption until June 2028 less than a year earlier. Metinvest's chief executive, Yuriy Ryzhenkov, warned the measures could "kill" the Ukrainian steel industry. Steel products make up about 15% of Ukraine's exports, and the EU takes about four-fifths of that volume. The BBC report on which this section rests was not retrieved, so the unnamed executive's specific claims are unverified here.

What links them

The two threads meet in the question of what a protected European steel market is protecting. The EU's quotas are meant to give mills inside the bloc room to invest in cleaner production. UK mills are outside that wall, and they also face the wider power-cost gap to the mills inside it. A producer in Ukraine is a war-damaged exporter outside the bloc's inner circle, caught by rules written with another country in mind.

The UK's difficulty is more direct. A cost disadvantage on a major input is hard for a steelmaker to absorb, and the costs flow downstream. Construction, rail and manufacturing buy British steel, and a plant that closes or never converts to arc furnaces leaves them dependent on imports. That is why the gap matters beyond the sector. Whether UK industrial electricity pricing can keep pace with the bloc Britain left is, in practice, a question about whether a domestic steel industry survives the shift to electric furnaces.

The next tests are concrete. UK Steel's updated price comparison and any government change to industrial power charges will show whether the gap is being addressed. On the EU side, the final quota allocations will show how much room Ukrainian mills are left with.
