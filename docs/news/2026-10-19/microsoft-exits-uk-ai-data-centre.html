Microsoft pulls out of the UK's largest planned AI data centre

In one sentence
Microsoft has withdrawn from Nscale's planned Loughton AI data centre in Essex, reportedly over grid power that may not arrive until the 2030s, though Microsoft has not said why.

Microsoft has pulled out of a planned AI data centre at Loughton in Essex, a site that had been presented as the largest of its kind in the UK and as a cornerstone of the government's plan to build AI computing capacity at home. The BBC reported the withdrawal on 18 October. The Telegraph reported it too. Microsoft has not publicly explained its decision, and that gap matters. The explanation that fits the reporting best is electricity: the site may not get a grid connection until the 2030s.

What has been withdrawn

The project belongs to Nscale, a British AI infrastructure company. It bought the Loughton site in January 2025. In September 2025 it announced an agreement under which the site would serve Microsoft's Azure cloud. A month later the two companies expanded their arrangement to cover about 200,000 Nvidia GB300 chips across several sites, and Loughton was reaffirmed as part of it.

As described in one detailed account of the plans, the Loughton site was to start at 50 megawatts of electrical capacity, expandable to 90 megawatts, and to hold 23,040 GB300 processors. The original timetable called for the site to open in the fourth quarter of 2026 and for chips to be installed in the first quarter of 2027. Neither date now looks achievable. Microsoft was the expected anchor tenant, meaning the customer whose long-term commitment makes the rest of the financing workable.

The figures are worth treating with care. I could not reach the BBC's own text, and the Telegraph piece gives no capacity or cost numbers. No contract value for Loughton has been published in the material I could check.

The stated reason, and the missing one

Microsoft has not commented publicly. The Telegraph did not establish a specific reason for the withdrawal. It did report that the project has struggled to secure power, and that authorities told Nscale the site might not receive a grid connection until the 2030s. Other outlets, including the Guardian as relayed by a third party, attribute the exit to power-supply concerns. That is an inference from the reporting, not a statement by Microsoft.

The distinction decides how far the story travels. If the cause is one site's grid queue, it is a local planning and infrastructure problem that a different location, or on-site generation, might solve. Nscale says it is exploring alternative power sources. If the cause were Microsoft rethinking how much capacity it wants in Britain, the problem would be much larger.

There is precedent for the second reading. In April 2025 Bloomberg reported that Microsoft had halted talks or delayed sites in several countries, including the UK, and analysts at TD Cowen tied that to oversupply relative to Microsoft's demand forecasts. Microsoft said at the time that it planned its capacity years ahead and remained on track to spend about $80bn on data centres that financial year. It has also pressed ahead elsewhere in Britain, agreeing to buy 48 acres south-east of Leeds for a hyperscale site, with completion expected in 2026. Nothing published so far shows that this week's decision belongs to the retrenchment pattern rather than to Loughton's power problem.

Why the grid matters to ministers

The government has made AI computing capacity a pillar of its growth strategy, and Loughton was one of the projects held up as evidence that private money would build it. A withdrawal by a customer of Microsoft's size leaves a gap in that story, whatever the reason. If a flagship project near London cannot get power until the 2030s, the question for ministers shifts from whether companies want to build in Britain to whether the grid can serve them in time. I found no government response in the coverage available.

Nscale's own position

Nscale says it remains committed to Loughton and that its relationship with Microsoft is strong. It also says the electricity difficulties will not affect its other contracts. The timing is awkward for the company. It is reported to be preparing a US listing that could raise as much as $3bn, possibly this month, and its IPO filing shows Microsoft and Anthropic account for about 85% of its contracts. Losing the anchor tenant at a flagship site invites investor scrutiny of how concentrated that book is.

There are other pressure points. The Telegraph reports that Nscale's flagship Norway site is about six months behind schedule, with 6.4 megawatts of a planned 30 operational. The wider market has been jumpy: CoreWeave shares fell about 25% last week on worries over borrowing and data centre financing, and Australia's Firmus abandoned its IPO after failing to attract enough demand at its proposed valuation.

What to watch

Three things will settle the question. The first is whether Microsoft says anything on the record, and whether it names power or demand. The second is Nscale's IPO paperwork and pricing, which will show how investors value a company that has lost its anchor tenant at its flagship UK site. The third is whether any alternative power arrangement at Loughton can be shown to work on a timetable earlier than the 2030s.
