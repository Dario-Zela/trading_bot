Verdict due for Kenyan pastor accused over Shakahola starvation deaths

In one sentence
A Mombasa court is due to rule on 191 murder counts against Paul Mackenzie over the Shakahola starvation deaths, a case that also exposes how little the state did to stop him.

A court in Mombasa is due to deliver a verdict on Monday in the trial of Paul Mackenzie, the self-styled pastor whose followers starved to death in the Shakahola forest in Kenya's coastal interior. The ruling covers 191 counts of murder, most of them involving children. Mackenzie and his co-accused pleaded not guilty in 2024. The outcome was not known at the time of writing.

What the court is weighing

The case concerns an 800-acre ranch near Malindi where Mackenzie, who founded the Good News International Church in 2003, gathered followers. Prosecutors say he urged them to fast to death in order to meet Jesus. They describe a "structured hierarchy of death" in which children were the first to be starved. More than 120 witnesses have testified about events between 2021 and 2023.

The defence says the case leans heavily on indirect evidence and on forensic findings it calls inconclusive. That argument has some footing in the scale of the exhumations. Of 429 bodies exhumed by September 2023, a Nation report attributed 214 deaths to starvation, 39 to asphyxia and 14 to head injury. For 115, the cause was not ascertained. In January 2024, when the murder charges were brought against Mackenzie and 30 others, 180 of the 191 child victims were still unidentified.

The verdict will not close the matter in full. Mackenzie faces separate manslaughter and terrorism cases, which he denies. Further charges were filed in January in connection with 52 bodies found in a nearby village. The death toll has also kept rising: Wikipedia's tally reached 457 by August 2025, with hundreds more people reported missing.

The longer question

The trial answers whether one man can be held criminally responsible. The harder question is why it took so long for anyone to stop him.

Mackenzie was no unknown. He was acquitted of radicalisation charges in 2017 and was arrested again in 2019 after two children died. He was free to resume preaching, and the starvation regime ran for months more. It came to light in March 2023 only after a man reported that his wife and daughter had gone to the forest and not come back. Police found emaciated survivors and shallow graves. Four of the first 15 people rescued died before reaching hospital.

The state response since has been largely regulatory. President William Ruto appointed a commission of inquiry and a task force to review rules on religious organisations. Interior Minister Kithure Kindiki called for tighter regulation of places of worship. Kenya has thousands of churches, many of them small and loosely supervised, and the line between protecting freedom of worship and policing abusive groups is politically delicate. The pattern is not unique to Kenya, and neighbouring states with fast-growing independent churches will be watching how far the reforms go.

Some of the state's own conduct has drawn criticism. In 2023, 65 survivors were arraigned on charges of attempted suicide. One suspect died in custody after a hunger strike.

What to watch

The immediate question is the verdict and, if there are convictions, the sentencing hearing that follows. After that, the task force's proposals on registering and monitoring religious groups will show whether the case changes oversight or only the outcome for one defendant. The pending manslaughter and terrorism trials mean Mackenzie remains in court well beyond Monday.
