Australia moves to give users an 'off switch' for recommendation algorithms

In one sentence
Australia has proposed a 'My Feed, My Way' law letting users switch off recommendation algorithms, with fines up to A$109.2 million, but the bill is still in draft.

Australia's government has proposed a rule that would let people switch off the recommendation algorithms that decide what appears in their social media feeds. The draft is called "My Feed, My Way". It is still a proposal: nothing has changed for users yet, and the bill has not passed.

Prime Minister Anthony Albanese announced the plan on 8 September 2026. Under the draft, platforms would have to notify users and let them choose how their default feed is built. A user could opt in to personalised content picked by an algorithm. Or they could opt out and see only posts from accounts they follow.

"This is sensible, pragmatic, practical reform," Albanese said. "It's not about giving government control. It's about giving people control."

From who to what

The plan follows Australia's ban on social media accounts for under-16s, which took effect on 10 December 2025. That law requires platforms to take "reasonable steps" to keep younger children off, on pain of fines of up to A$49.5 million. It does not define "reasonable steps". The explanatory memorandum says, at a minimum, platforms must use some form of age assurance. The eSafety Commissioner's guidance adds that platforms must find and remove existing accounts and stop new ones being created. By the regulator's own count, platforms blocked 4.7 million underage accounts.

The new proposal asks a different question. The under-16 law is about who may use a platform. This one is about what the platform's ranking systems do to everyone who does. It treats the feed as something the user controls, where until now it has been a setting the platform controls.

What the draft would require

Beyond the feed choice, platforms would have to document the steps they take against identified risks of harm to Australian users, and show those steps stay effective over time. The eSafety regulator would enforce the rules. It would also gain powers to order the quick removal of harmful, nudity-related and illegal content.

Penalties for non-compliance could reach A$109.2 million (about US$79 million). That is more than double the maximum in the under-16 law. The government says it will seek feedback from platforms, industry groups, civil society and advocates on the draft. The bill is expected to go to Parliament this year, though no dates have been given.

Precedent, and open questions

Australia would not be the first to try this. The EU's Digital Services Act has required large platforms since 2024 to offer a way to switch off profiling-based recommendations. Regulators there complained that platforms made the option hard to find. Australia's draft, as reported, centres on a notice and an explicit choice. That addresses the visibility problem, though the details will matter.

Two other questions hang over it. One is behavioural. Most users stay with default settings even when alternatives exist, so the size of any shift depends on what the default is and how the choice is presented. The reporting does not say which way the default would lean. The other is political. Free speech advocates have warned the wider package could lead to censorship. Meta and Google had not responded to requests for comment when the plan was announced.

The next marker is the published bill text and the consultation responses. These will show how the feed choice is worded, whether the default favours the algorithm, and whether the penalty level survives. If the rule passes, it would give other regulators a second working example to copy, alongside the EU's.
