Solent seabird numbers rebound after 2022 avian flu wipeout

In one sentence
A Solent tern colony abandoned during the 2022 bird flu outbreak now holds at least 756 breeding pairs, though recovery elsewhere in Britain's seabirds is uneven.

A seabird colony on the Solent coast that bird flu emptied in 2022 has filled again. At Pagham Harbour, in West Sussex, at least 756 pairs of Sandwich terns bred this year, according to the RSPB. That makes it one of the largest colonies of the species in England. In 2022, at the height of the highly pathogenic avian influenza (H5N1) outbreak, the birds abandoned the site entirely.

The figure is a useful data point. Few studies have followed whether seabird colonies recover after H5N1 mass die-offs, so a verified rebound at one site is evidence of what is possible. It does not show what is typical.

What the numbers show

The Pagham count is the firmest figure available. Several outlets, citing the RSPB, report the 756 pairs as a record for the reserve. The reserve's managers attribute the 2022 desertion to bird flu, which also struck black-headed and Mediterranean gulls nesting alongside the terns. Reports differ on the length of the recovery. Most say the birds returned four breeding seasons after the abandonment. The Sussex Express says the colony grew from zero to 756 pairs in three years. The sources do not reconcile the two framings, and the gap may simply reflect whether 2022 or 2023 is taken as the starting point.

Smaller numbers elsewhere in the reporting are less certain. One account gives 504 black-headed gull fledglings this year, up from 51 in 2025, and 34 little tern fledglings, up from 21. A separate trade report, whose date I could not confirm, gives figures of 473 black-headed gull and 269 Mediterranean gull fledglings, and 26 little terns. These may refer to different seasons. The same report noted reduced fledging rates in the colonial species after a suspected bird flu outbreak. Until the RSPB publishes a full season summary, those fledgling counts should be treated as provisional.

I could not locate the Guardian report that prompted this piece, so the figures here rest on RSPB statements as relayed by other outlets.

Recovery by design

The RSPB credits human intervention as well as the birds' own resilience. Staff and volunteers spent hundreds of hours controlling vegetation, maintaining exclusion fencing, running biosecurity checks and monitoring the colony around the clock during nesting. The reserve also created a new breeding island, which a trade report links to some of the success of the little terns.

That management matters for interpretation. A colony that rebounds with intensive help does not show that unmanaged colonies will do the same. Wildlife managers weighing where to spend limited money will want to know how much of the Pagham result came from the work, and how much from birds moving in from elsewhere.

A mixed national picture

The wider evidence is uneven. National Trust reporting describes England's key tern colonies as recovering after years of bird flu, flooding and weather pressure, but not uniformly: arctic tern numbers on the Farne Islands, off Northumberland, fell 27%. Research on gannets at Grassholm and Bass Rock forecasts that those colonies may not regain pre-outbreak numbers until 2041. The authors describe that as probably a best case.

The Solent result therefore sits at the optimistic end of a range. Terns, which breed quickly and move between sites, may recover faster than long-lived, slow-breeding species such as gannets.

The test will be next summer. A second strong season at Pagham, with fledging rates published alongside breeding pairs, would indicate that the colony is self-sustaining. A new bird flu wave in the spring would show how fragile the gain is.
