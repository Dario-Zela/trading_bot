Another Reform UK councillor quits over offensive remarks

In one sentence
Another Reform UK councillor has resigned over offensive comments, following Solihull and other cases that have put the party's candidate vetting under scrutiny.

Another Reform UK councillor has resigned over offensive comments, according to BBC Politics. The resignation adds to a run of departures that has put the party's candidate vetting under scrutiny. The BBC report as summarised to this desk does not name the councillor, the council or the remarks, and no second outlet could be found carrying the same account. Those details are not set out here.

A single exit, a familiar shape

On its own, one councillor leaving over remarks is a local matter. A by-election follows, the party issues a statement, and the story passes. The word "another" is what makes it more than that, because it points to earlier cases. Reporting from the past week and from earlier in the year suggests those cases are real.

The most prominent is in Solihull. An ITV undercover investigation, published on 14 October, filmed Reform councillors and candidates making racist and sexually explicit remarks. Three councillors have since resigned: Chris Whelan, Alan Feeney and Jamie Campbell. All three were elected to represent Castle Bromwich in May. Their departures trigger three by-elections. Feeney apologised, calling his comments "wrong, offensive and completely unacceptable". Reform initially defended Campbell's remarks as a joke in poor taste before he, too, apologised.

There are other examples. Stuart Prior resigned from Essex County Council and Rochford District Council within days of being elected in May. Reform said he left for personal reasons and revoked his membership; he had denied making racist posts. In Staffordshire, a councillor left the party and sits as an independent after posts about Black people and Africans were reported. Wayne Titley, another Staffordshire councillor, stepped down after old social media posts about migrants. In North Northamptonshire, Robert Bloom is understood to have resigned after a standards complaint about offensive language.

What the pattern does and does not show

These cases are not identical. Some involve old social media posts that surfaced after election. Others, like Solihull, involve remarks made while the councillors held office. Not every departure was for the reason first given, and some of the people involved dispute the accounts.

The common thread is that the material was often found by journalists or the public, not by the party before nomination. That is the argument for a vetting problem. A party that grew quickly and fielded many first-time candidates in May's local elections will have had fewer chances to check each one. Reform has not, in the reporting reviewed here, set out what checks it applies. Whether the latest resignation fits the same shape depends on what the remarks were and when they were made. That is the part of the BBC report this desk could not confirm.

What to watch

The test for the party is how it responds to the Solihull fallout: whether it publishes any change to how candidates are screened, and whether further cases follow ahead of the by-elections. Each new resignation adds to the case that this is systemic. The by-elections in Castle Bromwich will show how much it matters to voters.
