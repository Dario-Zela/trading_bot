World Food Programme cuts Gaza aid as funding runs short

In one sentence
The WFP is cutting about 500,000 people from its Gaza food assistance because donor funding has run out, its acting chief says, even as 1.4 million face acute hunger under a ceasefire.

The World Food Programme is cutting the number of people it feeds in Gaza by about 500,000 in the coming weeks. Its acting head says the reason is money, not access: donors have not paid enough to keep the operation at its current size.

Carl Skau, the WFP's acting executive director, spoke from Jerusalem in an NPR interview aired on 18 October, on his eighth trip to Gaza since the war began. Asked why people were being dropped from assistance, he said: "It's because the funding is no longer there." He described conditions in the enclave as "absolutely brutal".

What is being cut

Skau said the agency reaches about 2 million people a month and will remove roughly half a million of them. The figures in circulation differ, and the differences matter. A WFP official quoted last month described planned reductions from 1 October. Monthly beneficiaries would fall from about 1.5 million to 1.1 million. Recipients of food would fall from 1.1 million to about 600,000. Daily hot meals would fall from 250,000 to 150,000. A separate video report on Skau's remarks gave a starting figure of 1.8 million. The WFP has not published a single reconciled set of numbers, so the scale of the cut is clear in direction and approximate in size.

The cuts follow earlier ones. In July the WFP reduced the cash value of its assistance to 375,000 people in Gaza by 40 percent. It has also halved aid in the West Bank. In early September the agency said it needed an additional $386 million over six months to serve around two million food-insecure people across Gaza and the West Bank. No confirmed tally of how much has been pledged was available.

Need has not fallen

The cuts come during a ceasefire brokered by the United States between Israel and Hamas, which might be expected to ease pressure on aid. They have not done so. Skau said about 1.4 million people, more than two-thirds of Gaza's population, face crisis-level acute food insecurity. Famine was declared in parts of Gaza more than a year ago. He said the progress made since then is fragile.

He pointed to other constraints. His team records about 50 security incidents a day. Commercial goods had not entered Gaza for five days before his visit, though some trucks were moving again when he left. Many recently displaced residents lack cooking gas, fresh vegetables and adequate meals. He also said restrictions on "dual-use" items, which can have civilian and military applications, are blocking UNICEF and its partners from restoring water and sanitation services.

These obstacles are real, but Skau did not present them as the cause of the cuts. In his account, food can be brought in, and what is missing is the money to buy and distribute enough of it. That makes this a different problem from a blockade, and a more ordinary one: a humanitarian operation sized to a budget that has shrunk while the need has not.

What the agency wants instead

Skau argued for a change of method as well as more money. He favours moving from direct food distribution towards cash assistance, which he said is cheaper and lets people choose what they buy. That depends on markets recovering, and on early recovery work, such as shelter, starting before winter. He also urged the international community to push the political process towards a lasting settlement, since an operation this costly cannot be funded indefinitely.

The figures here rest largely on the WFP's own statements, and the agency has not given a donor-by-donor breakdown. The tests to watch are whether pledges against the $386 million appeal arrive before the cold weather, and whether the WFP publishes final numbers for who has been removed from its rolls.
