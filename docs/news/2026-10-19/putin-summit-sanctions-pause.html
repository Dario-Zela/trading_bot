Putin proposes a summit with Trump and Xi as Washington pauses Russia sanctions to keep Ukraine talks alive

In one sentence
The WSJ reports Putin has proposed a Trump–Xi–Putin summit on Ukraine, possibly at APEC in Shenzhen; Washington hasn't agreed, and Trump defends pausing Russia sanctions to keep talks alive.

A Wall Street Journal report that Vladimir Putin has proposed a three-way summit with Donald Trump and Xi Jinping on Ukraine would, if it holds, write China into the negotiating architecture of the war. It is an offer, not a meeting. Neither Washington nor Beijing has accepted it, and the Kremlin describes the idea as hypothetical. The report arrived as Trump publicly defended a suspension of Russia sanctions, saying the pause is meant to keep the peace talks going.

What has been reported

According to the Journal, as carried by U.S. News, the Korea Times and others, Putin raised the idea of a meeting with Trump and Xi. The paper cites a former Russian intelligence officer briefed on the matter, who places the suggestion in an October 9 telephone call between Putin and Trump. That call also reportedly produced a diesel arrangement between the two countries. Moscow is said to want the encounter on the sidelines of the APEC gathering in Shenzhen, scheduled for 18–19 November.

The Journal's account, as relayed by other outlets, also says Russian diplomats put the proposal to Chinese officials. They reportedly signalled they would not object so long as Trump agreed to take part. A senior U.S. official said Trump had not agreed to a summit but might look on the idea favourably, given his stated wish to end the war. The format, as reported, excludes Volodymyr Zelensky, which would worry Kyiv and European capitals.

The sourcing is thin and should be weighed accordingly. This account rests on the Journal's anonymous sourcing and on syndicated copies of it; the original article could not be read in full. The White House and the Chinese embassy had not responded to requests for comment when the wire copy went out. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov did acknowledge that contacts about a possible meeting are under way. He said there is no clear understanding yet on the meeting or its agenda, though all three sides accept that it could in principle happen.

Why Beijing's seat matters

The idea of Chinese involvement is not new. In February 2025 the Journal reported the opposite direction of travel: that Beijing had floated a Trump–Putin summit through intermediaries. A White House official reportedly dismissed that offer as "not viable at all", and China's foreign ministry said it had no information to offer.

What would be different now is the sponsor. A proposal from Moscow, not from Beijing, with China as a named party, would turn China from a quiet economic backstop for Russia into a participant in settlement talks. Several things follow if that is what the Kremlin intends.

  • It would suggest Moscow sees Chinese buy-in as necessary for any deal to hold, whether as a guarantor or as the source of the economic lifeline that sustains Russia's side of it.
  • It would give Putin a forum in which Washington and Beijing, not Kyiv or Brussels, shape the outcome.
  • It would hand Xi a role at low cost: reporting suggests he would attend only if Trump does, so Beijing risks little by saying yes.

These are inferences from the reported structure of the offer, not statements by any of the three governments. The Journal's own sourcing does not say what Putin wants China to guarantee.

The sanctions pause

The second thread is Washington's. Trump has defended a suspension of Russia sanctions, writing that he does not want to do anything that would push Moscow away from the bargaining table, according to the BBC report as reprinted and to other coverage. Those accounts describe a waiver issued under national-interest provisions, and say Trump also linked the decision to cooperation with Moscow amid tensions with Iran. He also criticised Ukrainian strikes on Russian diesel refineries for disrupting global fuel markets, according to BusinessToday. Some reports describe criticism from members of Congress, including Republicans. The exact measures suspended, and any Russian commitment offered in return, are not clear from the material available.

The pattern is familiar. In May 2025 Trump held off new sanctions after a call with Putin, and Marco Rubio argued that more penalties would cost Washington the ability to talk to Moscow. In December 2025 the Treasury partially suspended measures announced in October of that year. Each time, relief was justified as preserving a channel rather than as a reward.

The two stories connect. A sanctions pause and a summit offer are both ways of keeping Washington engaged. The pause lowers the price of staying in the room for Moscow; the summit offer raises the stature of the room. Read together, they suggest a Kremlin strategy of making talks themselves the product, with Kyiv's seat the first thing to be negotiated away. That reading is interpretation, and a Russian denial or an American refusal could undercut it quickly.

What to watch

The first test is confirmation. The Journal's account needs a statement from the Kremlin, the White House or the Chinese foreign ministry that goes beyond Peskov's hedged acknowledgement. The second is the calendar: Shenzhen's APEC meeting is under a month away, so a decision on attendance would have to come soon if the venue is real. The third is Kyiv and the Europeans, whose reaction to a format that leaves Ukraine outside will indicate how much weight the proposal carries. Last is Congress, where criticism of the sanctions waiver could limit how far Trump can extend it. Until at least one of those moves, the proposal remains a report of an offer.
