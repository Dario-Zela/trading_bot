How one rule change is altering one-day cricket

In one sentence
The ICC now lets ODI fielding sides keep both new balls until over 34 and pick one for the rest, which in theory favours spin and reverse swing late on, though hard evidence is thin.

For fifteen years, a one-day international has been played with two white balls, one from each end, both discarded before they could age. That regime has now been trimmed. Under revised ICC playing conditions, the bowling side keeps both new balls until the end of the 34th over, then picks one to use for overs 35 to 50. The other goes into the pocket of the umpire's coat.

It is a small edit with large consequences, because the last 15 overs are where 50-over matches are won. The ICC's stated aim was to "readdress the balance between bat and ball".

What changed

The 2011 rules put a fresh ball at each end for the whole innings. Bowlers could not get a ball old enough to reverse-swing, and batters in the closing overs were hitting a hard, bright ball that came onto the bat. Sachin Tendulkar was among the best-known critics, arguing the format had become a hitter's game.

The ICC's men's cricket committee recommended the 34-over compromise, and the chief executives' committee passed it as a playing-condition change, so it did not need a full board vote. The ICC announced the tweak in 2025, with the ODI change due from 2 July that year. Some later explainers give a different start date for ICC events. The date should be checked against the ICC's own playing-conditions document before it is relied on.

Why tactics shift

The logic runs in three steps. A ball that has been in use for 34 overs is scuffed and softer, so it grips for spin and can be made to reverse. The fielding captain now has a real decision at the 35th over, because the choice between the two balls is theirs. And the batting side can no longer assume a hard ball for the slog.

The expected consequences follow from that:

  • Spin matters later. Overs 35 to 50 are where a second-string spinner can be most useful, rather than a bowler to be got through.
  • Wickets in hand are worth more. A side that loses early wickets can no longer count on late hitting against a new ball.
  • The middle overs are less of a lull. Batting sides have to decide earlier whether to push on or hold back for the final stretch.

These are the predictions of commentators and the ICC's own reasoning, not measured results. The search behind this article found no published scoring-rate or strike-rate comparison for overs 35 to 50 under the new rule, and no coach or captain on record describing a specific change of plan. Until such figures exist, the effect is plausible but unproven.

The sceptics

R. Ashwin has argued that two new balls, along with fielding restrictions, still favour batters. He has said the art of reverse swing has faded and that death-overs bowling has become predictable. His point is that 34 overs of two-ball cricket may not age a ball enough, and that a 50-over innings was once designed around an old ball.

There is also history to weigh. Batting and bowling powerplays, the supersub and a mid-innings ball change all arrived with similar promises and were later dropped.

What to watch

The test is in the scorecards. The figures to compare are runs per over and wickets lost in overs 35 to 50 before and after the change, along with how often captains choose the older ball and how much spin they bowl late. The next full ODI series will give the first sizeable sample, and a tidy pattern would be a stronger argument than any prediction.
