UK profit warnings reach a two-year high as the Iran war lifts costs

In one sentence
UK-listed firms issued 65 profit warnings in the third quarter, the most since late 2024, with 38% citing the Middle East conflict and 35% rising costs, EY says.

UK-listed companies issued 65 profit warnings in the three months to the end of September. That is the most in any quarter since the final three months of 2024, and it comes with the Iran war's cost pressures feeding through to company earnings. Profit warnings are statements to the market that results will fall short of expectations, so they tend to show up before downgrades to earnings forecasts. A count at a two-year high says that cost pressures from the conflict are being felt in company earnings, not only in the inflation figures.

The figures come from EY-Parthenon's quarterly analysis of London-listed companies. They are reported here through secondary coverage, including ITV News and Brit Brief, rather than the EY release itself, and that coverage gives no sector breakdown for the third quarter.

The count

The 65 warnings compare with 59 in the second quarter and 55 in the first. They are still below the 71 recorded in the fourth quarter of 2024, which is why the headline is "highest in two years" and not a record. The rise from 55 to 59 to 65 is steady rather than a spike. Almost a fifth of listed companies, 17%, have issued at least one warning in the past 12 months.

The cause matters as much as the count. EY attributes 54% of the third-quarter warnings to policy change and geopolitical uncertainty, the leading factor. A further 38% referred specifically to the Middle East conflict, and 35% cited rising costs and inflationary pressure. The categories overlap, since a firm can cite more than one, so the shares do not add to 100%.

The pattern was already visible in the second quarter. EY's earlier report found that two in five of the 80 warnings issued since the end of February flagged the war's impact. In that quarter, 53% cited policy change and geopolitical uncertainty, the highest quarterly share in more than 25 years of EY's analysis.

Where the costs landed

The third-quarter coverage does not name the sectors, so the clearest evidence on exposure is from the second quarter. Travel and leisure issued seven warnings, the most in that category since the third quarter of 2022, as lower bookings met soaring jet fuel costs. Housebuilders issued six in the quarter and eight in the first half, their highest total since the start of the Covid pandemic, with build costs rising and demand weakening.

The mechanism is simple. Energy and fuel are direct inputs for airlines and tour operators, and for the makers of bricks, cement and glass. Higher costs also squeeze households, and that hits discretionary spending. Whether third-quarter warnings followed the same sector pattern cannot be confirmed from the published coverage, and the full EY tables would settle it.

A similar squeeze is visible in energy-intensive industry. A companion piece in this edition on steel and UK power costs shows how a cost gap against European rivals can leave producers with little room to pass prices on.

What EY says, and what comes next

Jo Robinson, EY-Parthenon's partner and UK&I financial restructuring leader, said:

"This is not a conventional economic cycle."

She acknowledged that businesses have shown "considerable resilience", but added that "the pace of policy change is making forecasting, investment and capital allocation much harder." In the second quarter she had said higher costs and supply-chain disruption would take time to filter through to earnings and order books, which implies later quarters could be worse.

The timing is awkward for the government. The data came less than two weeks before the autumn Budget, and industry groups have been urging the Chancellor to avoid raising business taxes. Companies already citing policy uncertainty as a reason to cut forecasts are unlikely to welcome a new set of tax changes.

The next data points are the Budget itself, due before early November, and the run of company trading updates and results in the coming weeks, which will show whether the 65 warnings are a peak or a stage on the way up. The Bank of England will read the same evidence for signs of margin compression and how much of the cost shock companies are passing on to customers. A rising warning count with persistent inflation would suggest firms are absorbing costs rather than passing them on, and that would bear on pricing, wages and hiring.
