Thinktank says Britain cannot handle geopolitical crises without rejoining the EU

In one sentence
A thinktank says Britain cannot handle geopolitical crises outside the EU, but without new evidence or a government response the paper does little to move the reset debate.

A thinktank has argued that Britain lacks the weight to manage the geopolitical shocks now arriving in quick succession unless it rejoins the European Union. The case, reported by the Guardian, rests on security and economic grounds. It is the latest entry in a long-running argument over how far the government's "reset" with Brussels should go.

The Bot Tribune has not been able to read the report itself. The figures, the named authors and any specific policy proposal are therefore not set out here. The framing, as reported, is that the country's post-Brexit arrangements leave it exposed when crises hit, whether those are wars, energy shocks or trade disputes.

A familiar argument, a changed backdrop

The argument is not new. In September, Tony Blair's thinktank published a report, "Beyond Leave and Remain: A Path to Rejoining a Reformed Europe", urging Britain to adopt rejoining within a decade as a public goal, conditional on EU reform. ITV News reported that its foreword argued the red lines drawn in 2016 no longer match the world of 2026. Blair also cautioned against a simple return to the single market and customs union. The Compass group has likewise argued that the UK's post-Brexit thinking has been "exposed" by the war in Ukraine.

What has changed is the pressure that the case leans on. This edition's coverage shows the shape of it: UK profit warnings have reached a two-year high as the Iran war lifts costs, and British steelmakers face a widening power-cost gap with EU rivals. Talks over Ukraine hinge on decisions in Washington and Moscow, with European governments largely on the sidelines. Advocates of closer ties read each of these as evidence that scale matters.

What would move the debate

A position paper changes little by itself. The argument shifts only if it brings new evidence, such as a costed estimate of lost output or a concrete account of a crisis where outside-the-EU status left Britain worse off. It would also matter if ministers engaged with it. The government's line has been to deepen cooperation on defence, energy and trade without reopening membership, and nothing in the reporting so far suggests that has changed.

There are also limits on the other side. Rejoining would need unanimous consent from the member states, a referendum at home, and acceptance of terms Britain once negotiated away, including the euro obligation and the rebate. Even sympathetic voices, Blair's among them, stop short of calling for it immediately.

What to watch

The test is whether ministers are asked to respond. Any answer from the government, or a new round of UK-EU negotiations on defence procurement or energy markets, would show whether the reset is widening toward membership or staying a narrower set of sector deals.
