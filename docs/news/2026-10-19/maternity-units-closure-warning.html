Up to 20 maternity units in England may have to shut, NHS leader warns

In one sentence
An NHS Alliance director reportedly says up to 20 of England's 155 consultant-led maternity units may close or change role, citing doctor shortages and rising caesareans.

Up to 20 of England's 155 consultant-led maternity units could close or lose their current role, a senior NHS figure has warned. The reason given is a shortage of specialist doctors, together with a steady rise in caesarean births. The estimate has been reported only at second hand so far. The original Guardian article could not be read, and this account relies on a summary of it published by UA.News on 19 October 2026. The statements below are paraphrases, not verbatim quotes.

The warning

The 20-unit figure is attributed to Matthew Hopkins, described as director of acute care at the NHS Alliance. According to the summary, he argues that concentrating maternity care in fewer hospitals is unavoidable if care is to be safer and sustainable.

The units he sees as most vulnerable are small ones, handling roughly 1,500 to 2,000 births a year. Many are in rural or coastal areas, where recruiting obstetricians and other senior doctors is hardest.

The summary does not say whether "up to 20" is a forecast, a worst case or a planning assumption. Nor does it name the trusts or units concerned. "Up to" sets a ceiling, not an expected number, and the figure should be read that way until the original text is checked.

An earlier trade-press report supports the general direction. On 20 August 2026 the Health Service Journal reported that a national NHS leader expected more closures, and that 26 of 155 consultant-led units had recorded fewer than 2,000 deliveries in the previous year. That article is paywalled. Only part of it could be read, and the visible text did not name its source. It is not established that the HSJ's unnamed leader and the Guardian's source are the same person.

What is driving it

The summary gives two pressures. One is staffing: small units struggle to keep enough senior doctors to run round-the-clock obstetric cover.

The other is the caesarean rate. The summary says caesareans rose from 33% of births in England in 2020 to 45% last year. Caesareans need surgical teams and operating theatres, so a higher rate raises the staffing a unit must have.

The MBRRACE-UK confidential enquiry adds a safety dimension. As summarised, it found that some women who died in childbirth in 2022–2024 faced delays to a caesarean because no obstetric surgeon or free theatre was available. The summary does not claim this caused those deaths, and the report itself has not been checked here.

A recent case shows what a closure looks like in practice. North Devon District Hospital in Barnstaple temporarily stopped births in August because of staff shortages. An estimated 194 women gave birth in Exeter instead, 43 miles and about 70 minutes away by road. The Royal Devon University Healthcare Trust was due to decide whether the unit had enough doctors to reopen safely.

Reaction

The professional bodies accept the staffing problem but resist closure as a first response.

  • Hannah Leonard, chief policy officer at the Royal College of Midwives, is reported as saying families rely on local units, so high-quality care must stay close to home.
  • Alison Wright, president of the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists, is reported as saying other ways of solving staffing problems should be considered before any unit closes.
  • Professor Marian Knight of the University of Oxford has previously argued that consolidation should be considered. No new statement from her is reported.

A separate report on 10bmnews says NHS leaders have described how maternity care is organised as "not fit for purpose". It says the Department of Health and Social Care replied that a government taskforce will deliver the reforms needed. That item was seen only in a search summary and has not been checked.

What to watch

Three things would settle the story. The first is the original Guardian text, to confirm Hopkins's title and exactly what he said about the figure. The second is whether any trust or integrated care board names units under review, since closures are normally preceded by local consultation. The third is the Royal Devon decision on Barnstaple, the nearest live test of whether a suspended unit can reopen. Until trust-level evidence appears, the figure of 20 is a leader's estimate of the ceiling, not a list of units.
