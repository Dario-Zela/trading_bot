Gaza ceasefire nears 'point of no return', peace envoy warns

Nickolay Mladenov, the Board of Peace's high representative for Gaza, has led the effort to implement the Trump administration's ceasefire plan. — Ottokars / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
UN peace envoy Nickolay Mladenov has warned the Security Council that continued Israeli strikes and a stalled disarmament plan risk pushing Gaza's ceasefire past a 'point of no return'.

The ceasefire that was supposed to end the Gaza war eleven months ago is still, technically, in force. Israeli tanks are not rolling toward Gaza City. Hamas has not resumed its rocket fire in earnest. But the man Washington appointed to steward the peace into being told the UN Security Council this week that the gap between a truce on paper and a truce on the ground has grown wide enough to swallow the whole project.

"That would not be a setback," Nickolay Mladenov, the Board of Peace's high representative for Gaza, told the Council on August 26. "It would be a point of no return for everyone." If the process collapses now, he said, there would be "no roadmap to return to, there will be no administration to deploy and there will be nothing left on the ground to rebuild."

That is an unusually stark formulation from a diplomat whose job depends on projecting forward momentum. It is also, for anyone pricing risk in the Middle East, the clearest signal yet that the calm markets built into oil and equities since October is more fragile than the headline ceasefire suggests.

A truce that never fully took hold

The ceasefire took effect in October 2025, built around a 20-point plan brokered by the Trump administration after two years of war that followed Hamas's October 7, 2023 attack, which killed roughly 1,200 Israelis and saw about 240 taken captive. Since then, by Mladenov's own account to the Council, 1,288 Palestinians have been killed and 4,290 wounded in Gaza — a fraction of the 73,422 killed since the war began, but a toll that keeps accumulating in a territory ostensibly at peace.

The pattern has continued into the past week. An Israeli strike near a bakery in Deir al-Balah on August 30 killed a man and a three-year-old boy, Gaza health officials said. Four days earlier, strikes and tank fire killed at least four people, including two children, in central Gaza — coming, NBC News reported, just a week after a US delegation had asked Israeli officials to curb the pace of attacks. Israeli officials generally describe such strikes as responses to militant activity near the boundary of the withdrawal line; Mladenov's rejoinder to the Council was pointed: "Will another strike on a munitions depot stop Hamas from rearming or tightening its grip?"

The disarmament plan that hasn't started

The structural problem sits one level below the strikes. Hamas agreed on July 30 to a roadmap under which it would surrender weapons to an International Stabilization Force — headed by a US major general, Jasper Jeffers — and hand governing authority to a new Palestinian administrative committee recognised by the UN. That was meant to trigger a 14-day negotiation period to sequence the handover. Mladenov told the Council that period still has not begun.

Part of the reason, he suggested, is Israeli reluctance. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu has pushed back on elements of the framework, insisting on full Hamas disarmament before any further Israeli withdrawal, and Israeli forces have not made full use of the deconfliction channels set up to prevent exactly the kind of strikes killing civilians. Mladenov also flagged a narrower but telling complaint: with winter approaching, Israel's restrictions on shelter materials entering Gaza remain, in his word, "unacceptable." Kushner and Mladenov met Netanyahu in Jerusalem on August 17 to press the case directly; ten days later, the envoy was warning the Security Council in public that the plan had stalled at the political level even as he insisted it had "moved from the negotiating table to the engineering table" on the ground.

Every side has its own reason to slow-walk. Hamas has incentives to retain leverage — and weapons — for as long as a final territorial and governance settlement remains unresolved. Israel's government, facing a Knesset election on October 27, has domestic incentives to appear tough rather than accommodating. Mladenov's own description of the dynamic was blunt: "every weapon carried, every tunnel still worked on and every convoy still obstructed sets the process back."

Why traders should be watching

The market read on Gaza has, until now, run mostly through oil. Brent crude spent much of the summer unwinding the risk premium built up during the broader regional escalation — the price fell from the high $80s toward $80 a barrel in early August as ceasefire progress and, separately, easing tension around the Strait of Hormuz took supply-disruption fears off the table. That premium never fully disappeared; traders described it as partially corrected rather than gone, because the "underlying conflict architecture" across the region remained intact.

Mladenov's warning is a reminder of why. A ceasefire that fails after implementation has begun — rather than one that never starts — is arguably the more dangerous scenario for markets, because it means walking back commitments already priced as durable: an international stabilization force partially stood up, a disarmament framework nominally agreed, capital tentatively flowing toward reconstruction pledged at $70 billion. Unwinding that is a bigger shock than a stalled negotiation, and it would land in the same news cycle as the US military's recent strikes on Iranian rocket launchers near Hormuz, another reminder that the region's separate flashpoints do not stay separate for long.

Equity investors with regional or defense-sector exposure, and portfolios hedged against emerging-market contagion from a wider Middle East conflict, have mostly treated Gaza as background noise since October. Mladenov's Security Council appearance argues that treatment is no longer safe. The next test comes quickly: whether Israel and Hamas actually open the 14-day disarmament negotiation window that was supposed to have started weeks ago, and whether strikes like the one that killed a three-year-old in Deir al-Balah on Sunday keep recurring at a pace that makes the ceasefire look, in practice, indistinguishable from a low-intensity continuation of the war it was meant to end.
