Ukraine pitches drone tech to US in exchange for Patriot defences

Mykhailo Fedorov, Ukraine's ousted defense minister and former digital transformation chief, now pitching a drone-for-Patriots exchange to Washington. — Wikimedia Commons / Dubetskyi-Ph, CC BY 4.0
In one sentence
Ousted ex-defense minister Fedorov offers to help the US build drones in exchange for Patriot interceptors, betting Ukraine's tech is now worth trading for.

Mykhailo Fedorov spent six months as Ukraine's defense minister and most of that time trying to convince the world that Kyiv's drone industry was not a wartime improvisation but a genuine technology base. He lost the job in July, ousted by President Volodymyr Zelensky after a rift with the then-armed forces commander-in-chief, Oleksandr Syrsky. Now, touring Washington and Rome as an ousted-minister-turned-informal envoy, he is making the same pitch to a different audience — and offering to trade, not ask.

In one sentence: Ousted ex-defense minister Mykhailo Fedorov is offering to help the US build its own drone fleet in return for Patriot interceptors, betting Ukraine's battlefield tech is now valuable enough to swap rather than beg for.

In an interview published by the Financial Times on 30 August, Fedorov said he was prepared to help Washington "build an army of drones" of its own, in direct exchange for Patriot interceptor missiles Ukraine needs to survive a third wartime winter of Russian missile barrages. "I'm ready to make that trade," he said. Alongside it, he's canvassing venture capitalists, private investors and US technology firms to back a dedicated fund for Ukrainian defense startups — pitched not as charity but as an investment thesis.

A minister without a ministry

The framing matters partly because of who is doing the asking. Fedorov built his profile as Ukraine's tech-forward digital transformation minister before Zelensky moved him into defense in January, a job he held only until July. His firing triggered public protests and turned him, by several private polls, into a plausible future presidential contender — a trajectory that makes this US and Italy tour look as much like political positioning as diplomacy. He is currently an unpaid adviser to Italy's defense minister, Guido Crosetto, with no formal Ukrainian government mandate to negotiate on Washington's behalf. That gives the pitch salesmanship rather than treaty weight, though it doesn't make the underlying case any less real.

That case rests on numbers. Ukrainian firms were producing roughly 1,000 interceptor drones a day by early this year, according to Ukrainian defense-industry figures, doubling the total received by front-line units in the first four months of 2026 compared with all of 2025. The homegrown Sting interceptor, at $1,000 to $2,500 apiece, is credited with the bulk of a record 1,704 Shahed drones destroyed in a single month in January — most by drone-on-drone intercepts rather than guns or missiles. Fedorov's fund would extend that model into battlefield robotics, faster jet-powered interceptors to counter Russia's newer strike drones, and cheap AI-guided missiles.

Patriots remain the scarcer asset

The swap only makes sense because Patriots are the item Ukraine cannot make itself and the US struggles to spare. The 12-day war with Iran in June is estimated to have drained roughly two-thirds of Washington's pre-conflict Patriot interceptor stockpile, and a parallel push to let Ukraine co-produce the system under license has stalled for months. NATO ambassador Matthew Whitaker has said any such deal won't be concluded before winter, and a Republican Capitol Hill aide told Military Times the real holdup isn't technology-transfer risk but contractor anxiety — Lockheed Martin and Raytheon reportedly fear Ukrainian engineers could improve on the Patriot design and undercut them on price. A State Department memorandum on a related exchange has reportedly sat unsigned since May.

That backdrop is what gives Fedorov's offer its edge: he isn't proposing to wait out a stalled bureaucratic process, but to route around it by making Ukraine's cheap, combat-tested drone know-how the thing Washington wants enough to trade for. Whether the Pentagon or the White House takes him up on it is a separate question — he holds no office that can bind either government, and Kyiv's actual defense ministry, now run by Yevhen Khmara, is not a party to the pitch.

Watch for whether the stalled Patriot co-production memorandum gets a signature this autumn, and whether Fedorov's fund attracts a named US investor — either would turn a minister's pitch into policy.
