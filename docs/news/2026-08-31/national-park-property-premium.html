Homes in UK national parks command 24% price premium

Mam Tor in the Peak District National Park — one of England's protected landscapes where restricted development hasn't dented buyer demand — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Homes inside England's national parks sell for 24% more than similar homes outside them, a premium that has held steady for two years despite tight planning restrictions.

Buy a house inside one of England's national parks and you will, on average, pay roughly a quarter more than for an equivalent property just beyond the boundary line. That is the headline finding of new research from Nationwide Building Society, which puts the premium at 24% — a figure that has barely moved in two years despite a planning system in these areas that is, by design, among the most restrictive in the country.

The finding sits at an odd angle to how real estate is usually priced. Planning restriction is normally treated as a drag on value — it caps supply, yes, but it also signals friction: slower renovations, tighter listed-building rules, a harder path to extending the kitchen. National parks carry all of that friction. They command the premium anyway.

The numbers

Nationwide's analysis, an update to research it has run periodically since the mid-2010s, found homes within park boundaries selling for 24% more than comparable properties outside them — down slightly from 25% in its 2024 study, which the lender's economists describe as "remarkably consistent" given the intervening rate cycle. The effect fades but doesn't vanish with distance: homes within three miles of a park still fetch a 6% premium over those further out.

The most expensive park to buy in is the New Forest, where the average property costs £563,000, more than double the UK average. The South Downs follows at £485,000, then the Peak District at £450,000. A parallel tier of protection — national landscapes, the rebranded Areas of Outstanding Natural Beauty, which include the Cotswolds, the Chilterns and Surrey Hills — carries a smaller but still meaningful 14% premium. Surrey Hills tops that list at £710,000 on average.

Why restriction doesn't repel buyers

Nationwide's own explanation is the straightforward one: scarcity value compounds with amenity value. National parks restrict new housing construction almost by definition — that is the point of the designation — so the supply of homes inside the boundary grows slowly if at all, even as demand for a house with a view of open moorland or ancient woodland does not. Add the fact that a park boundary is, functionally, a permanent guarantee against the thing many buyers fear most about the countryside: a housing estate going up next door.

That combination — a hard supply cap paired with an amenity that can't be replicated outside the line — is a fairly clean textbook case of inelastic supply meeting durable demand. It also means the premium is unlikely to be arbitraged away by more building, since more building is precisely what the designation exists to prevent.

What it says about pricing scarcity

The wider point, for anyone building comparable-property models, is that regulatory burden and price are not simply inversely related. A planning regime that makes a home harder to build, extend or alter can still leave it worth more, if the same regime is also what preserves the thing buyers are paying for. Treating "restricted" as a discount factor, without asking what the restriction is protecting, risks systematically mispricing exactly this kind of property.

The next test of whether the premium holds will come with Nationwide's next house price index update and, more consequentially, in the run-up to the October Budget, where speculation over property tax changes has already been denting transaction volumes nationally. Whether a national park postcode insulates against that chill, or simply delays it, is the open question.
