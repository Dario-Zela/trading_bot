Abrdn faces first FTSE 100 exit since 2017 merger after 40% share slump

In one sentence
Aberdeen Group's shares, still down roughly 40% since the 2017 Standard Life-Aberdeen merger, are close to the FTSE 100 cut-off just weeks after rejoining the index.

It has been a strange kind of homecoming. Aberdeen Group — the Edinburgh fund manager most people still call by its old name, abrdn — returned to the FTSE 100 only in June, restored to the blue-chip index after a rally in its shares finally lifted its market value back above the cut-off line. Ten weeks later, it is once again hovering at the bottom of the table, its stock still trading roughly 40% below the level at which Standard Life Aberdeen first listed after the 2017 merger that created it, and market watchers are asking whether the next reshuffle sends it back down to the FTSE 250.

The London Stock Exchange's index compiler, FTSE Russell, rebalances the FTSE 100 every quarter, culling any constituent that slips out of the market's top 110 companies by value and promoting the largest FTSE 250 names in their place. Aberdeen's re-entry in June came alongside Computacenter and Investec, as Berkeley Group, Mondi and Rightmove dropped out. With the next quarterly review due in September, Aberdeen's roughly £4.3bn market capitalisation sits close enough to the boundary that another swap looks plausible.

A serial relegation candidate

If Aberdeen does drop out again, it would not be a novelty so much as a pattern resuming. The firm has bounced between the two indices repeatedly since Standard Life and Aberdeen Asset Management completed their £11bn all-share merger in August 2017, when the combined group was valued well above £10bn and its shares changed hands at close to 390p. It was relegated to the FTSE 250 in September 2022, promoted back in December that year, relegated again in September 2023, and has spent much of the time since bouncing around the second tier before this year's return. Shares now trade around 240p — up about a fifth so far in 2026, but still roughly 40% below where the merged company started out nine years ago.

That long decline reflects a business that has struggled to find its footing since the deal was signed. Lloyds Banking Group pulled roughly £109bn of assets from Aberdeen's funds almost as soon as the merger completed, in a dispute that set the tone for years of net withdrawals. In 2021 the company rebranded itself "abrdn" — a change of style that drew ridicule from investors and commentators, including descriptions of it as "corporate insanity" — before reversing course in March 2025 and reverting to Aberdeen Group plc.

Outflows keep outpacing the turnaround

Under chief executive Jason Windsor, a former Aviva and Persimmon finance chief appointed in September 2024, the company has pursued a cost-cutting and simplification plan, including around 500 job cuts and a push to concentrate on Aberdeen Investments, its adviser platform business and Interactive Investor, the retail trading platform it bought for £1.49bn in 2022. Half-year results published on 29 July showed the strategy delivering some numbers that read well in isolation: adjusted operating profit up 21% year-on-year, with management still targeting more than £300m in adjusted operating profit and roughly £300m of net capital generation for the year. Interactive Investor logged record net inflows of £6.8bn and grew its customer base 14% to 525,000.

But the core asset-management business tells a different story. Net outflows across the group tripled to £3.9bn in 2025, from £1.1bn the year before, a deterioration the company partly blamed on uncertainty ahead of the November budget and on a single large client withdrawal. In the adviser arm specifically, net outflows worsened to £1.3bn from £0.9bn a year earlier, and management has said returning that business to net inflows will take longer than previously hoped. Over the two years before that, outflows exceeded £10bn annually. Retail platform growth is masking, rather than reversing, the pressure on the traditional fund business.

Why the index seat still matters

That pressure is structural rather than specific to Aberdeen. Mid-sized active managers across the UK have been squeezed for years by cheaper index-tracking products and rising costs, a trend that has thinned margins across the sector and pushed several rivals into mergers or into private ownership altogether. Aberdeen's index status is more than a scoreboard entry: FTSE 100 membership determines which passive tracker funds are obliged to hold a stock, and by how much, so a relegation typically triggers a wave of mechanical selling by index funds as they rebalance, followed by any active demand that develops from investors newly able to buy FTSE 250 names. A promotion works in reverse. For a company already fighting to persuade clients its underlying business is stabilising, another swing through the exit door would be an unhelpful signal layered on top of the fund flow numbers themselves.

The next scheduled test comes with FTSE Russell's indicative changes for the September quarterly review, typically published in the third week of August based on a Tuesday closing-price snapshot, with the final list confirmed roughly a week later and changes taking effect after the close on the third Friday of the month. Aberdeen's fate will likely be decided by whether its share price can hold its 2026 gains through that measurement point — a narrower, more mechanical test than the underlying question of whether Windsor's turnaround can outrun the outflows it is still trying to stem.
