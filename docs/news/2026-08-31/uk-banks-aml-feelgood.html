UK regulators push banks to tell 'feelgood stories' on dirty money

HM Treasury's entrance at 1 Horse Guards Road, London, which issued the call for evidence. — Jdforrester (James Forrester) / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0
In one sentence
HM Treasury is asking banks and law firms to submit anecdotes of blocked dirty money ahead of a 2027 FATF review, even as UK financial-crime fines have fallen 78% in five years.

HM Treasury has asked banks, law firms and accountants to send in their best anecdotes about stopping dirty money — real-life examples, officials specified, "with clear, demonstrable results" — as Britain prepares its evidence file for the next global assessment of its anti-money-laundering defences.

The request, made through a call for evidence issued this month, asks firms for cases from 2022 onward: clients they refused to take on after spotting red flags, accounts closed once a customer's money turned out to be suspect, interventions that led to a police investigation or prosecution. The Treasury's own wording, cited by the Guardian, frames the ask plainly: "we are looking for real-life examples with clear, demonstrable results that illustrate how the UK's anti-money laundering, counter-terrorist financing and sanctions frameworks operate effectively in practice."

A test the UK failed once already

The deadline is not abstract. The Financial Action Task Force, the Paris-based body that grades countries on money-laundering enforcement, evaluates the UK again in its next mutual assessment cycle, with an on-the-ground visit by FATF examiners expected next summer. London submits its own evidence packet to the taskforce in October — which is why the call for testimonials, however anecdotal, is landing now rather than later.

The UK has reason to be nervous. FATF's 2018 assessment was unflattering enough to feed years of commentary about London as a laundering hub for the world's kleptocrats and oligarchs, a reputation the government has spent the years since trying to shake. The legal sector, meanwhile, has been rated "high risk" for money laundering in the UK's own national risk assessment every year since 2017 — hardly the backdrop against which a folder of feelgood anecdotes settles the argument.

The numbers regulators aren't asking about

What the call for evidence does not request is any accounting of the trend in the other direction. The Bureau of Investigative Journalism reported in March that fines levied by the Financial Conduct Authority for financial crime failings had fallen 78% over five years, to £124m last year — a decline it called "pitiful" set against the scale of the problem. The Home Office's own estimate puts money laundered through the UK at roughly £100bn a year, a figure that has proven strikingly durable across successive crackdowns.

That is not to say enforcement has vanished. The FCA fined Barclays £42m this year over financial-crime risk-management failures spanning two separate client relationships — one involving the collapsed wealth manager WealthTek, where clients deposited £34m into an account Barclays could have flagged with a basic register check, and a second, larger, £39.3m the previous year over its handling of Stunt & Co, a gold bullion dealer. Nationwide was fined £44m for stale customer due diligence, in one case missing a customer who funnelled £27.3m in fraudulent Covid furlough payments through personal accounts, £26m of it in an eight-day stretch. These are the enforcement actions regulators can point to. They are not, obviously, the material banks have been invited to submit.

Substance versus story

Transparency International UK, which has tracked the gap between Britain's anti-money-laundering rhetoric and its supervisory practice for years, argues the current regime for overseeing lawyers, accountants and company-formation agents is fragmented enough that it is "not fit for purpose." A Treasury consolidation plan — folding oversight of professional-services firms into the FCA — is meant to address exactly that criticism, but it will not be fully in place before assessors arrive.

None of this makes the request for good examples illegitimate on its own terms; assessors do want to see frameworks working in practice, not just fine notices. But asking industry to supply its own highlight reel, on a deadline set by an external grader, while fine totals shrink and the estimated laundering flow holds roughly steady, invites the obvious question: whether October's submission will describe a system getting better, or one getting better at describing itself.

The next concrete marker is the UK's evidence submission to FATF in October, followed by the assessors' on-site visit next summer — the point at which anecdote meets audit.
