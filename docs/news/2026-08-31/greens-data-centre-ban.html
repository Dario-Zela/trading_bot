Greens call for temporary ban on new UK data centres

Green Party leader Zack Polanski, who is calling for a temporary halt on new UK data-centre construction. — Gary Lawson Photography / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
Green Party leader Zack Polanski wants new UK data centres paused over drought-era water and grid strain; Labour says a freeze would drive investment abroad.

Zack Polanski wants Britain to stop building data centres — for now, at least. The Green Party leader is calling on the government to impose what amounts to a moratorium on new server farms, exempting only small, community-scale facilities, until ministers can show the sites won't drain water supplies or overload the electricity grid.

"We need to slam the brakes on these energy-guzzling, water-guzzling data centers," Polanski said, speaking near the site of a proposed facility in east London. Asked what he meant in practice, he was blunt: "basically a moratorium, other than local-scale data centers for the local community."

The timing is pointed. Roughly three-quarters of England is currently classed as being in drought, with more than 27 million people under hosepipe bans or other restrictions. Data centres don't consume enormous volumes of water by national standards — the Water Research Centre puts the figure at around 1.9 billion litres a year in England, about 0.2% of the non-household market — but it notes a "clear upward trend" as the sector expands. More than 500 data centres already operate in the UK, with at least 183 more in the pipeline, and a single large facility can draw as much power as a medium-sized town.

Critical infrastructure, or critical overreach?

The Greens' sharper target is the status Whitehall gave data centres in September 2024, when then-technology secretary Peter Kyle designated them Critical National Infrastructure — the same tier as energy and water networks, and the first new sector to join that list in almost a decade. The designation entitles operators to priority government support during cyberattacks or extreme weather.

Polanski wants that reversed. "Critical infrastructure is water, electricity, hospitals — it isn't AI for tech billionaires," he said, arguing the label effectively puts server farms ahead of households in the queue for scarce resources during a drought. The government disputes that reading, insisting data centres do not get automatic priority access to water.

The electricity side of the argument rests on harder numbers. Data centres currently draw around 2.5% of UK electricity supply, a share forecast to roughly quadruple by 2030 as AI workloads scale up. Grid operators are already sitting on connection requests totalling some 125 gigawatts — nearly three times the country's 45GW peak demand — a queue that can leave new projects, of any kind, waiting years to plug in.

Labour holds the line

Downing Street shows no sign of budging. A government spokesperson said a construction pause "would simply send investment and good jobs abroad, while leaving Britain dependent on overseas computing for the processing of sensitive health and defense data," calling the idea "a disaster for jobs and national security." The Greens aren't alone in pushing back, though: Friends of the Earth has backed a similar pause pending proper environmental assessment, and campaigners in Scotland are pressing Holyrood for the same, with local opposition to specific sites building there too.

None of this changes planning policy today — the Green Party holds four Westminster seats and no formal veto over data-centre approvals. But the standoff marks out where the next fight over Britain's AI buildout will happen: not in Whitehall's growth targets, but in the physical limits of the grid and the reservoirs feeding it. The next test comes as the Environment Agency updates its drought status this autumn, and as individual data-centre planning applications — including the East London site Polanski visited — reach local authorities for decision.
