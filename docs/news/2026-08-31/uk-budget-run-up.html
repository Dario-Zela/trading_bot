Healey heads into October Budget with a growth boost, a £22bn gap and a North Sea backlash

Chancellor John Healey faces a narrowing set of options ahead of his first Budget on 28 October. — Lauren Hurley / No 10 Downing Street via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Stronger UK services growth and two-year-high consumer confidence hand Healey a better story — but they also raise the odds the BoE holds or hikes, squeezing his Budget room.

Andy Burnham's government caught a break this week that it did not build itself. UK services activity grew faster than economists expected in August, consumer confidence touched a two-year high, and both numbers landed on Chancellor John Healey's desk eight weeks before he stands up to deliver his first Budget. The trouble is that the same resilience is exactly what could stop the Bank of England easing the way Downing Street would like — and that matters more to the Budget's arithmetic than any single growth print.

The numbers behind the surprise

The S&P Global UK services PMI rose to 52.8 in August from 52.1 in July, beating a consensus forecast of 51.8 and marking the fastest pace of expansion since February. S&P Global said the reading was consistent with GDP growth of around 0.3% in the third quarter — close to the outturn in the second. Separately, GfK's consumer confidence index climbed to -14 in August from -17, its best level in two years, with households more upbeat about personal finances over the next twelve months and more inclined to view now as a good time for a major purchase — that sub-index hit its highest since December 2021. GfK credited the rebound partly to Burnham's arrival as prime minister in July and to easing energy prices.

Why resilience is a mixed blessing

A stronger economy is good news for a new government trying to convince voters it can deliver — but it complicates the case for interest-rate cuts. The Bank of England has now held Bank Rate at 3.75% for five consecutive meetings, and at its 30 July decision the vote split 6-3, with three MPC members — Huw Pill, Megan Greene and Catherine Mann — pushing to raise rates to 4% on concern that higher energy costs are feeding into inflation. CPI hit 2.9% in July, a four-month high, and forecasters expect it to reach roughly 3.5% by year-end. Markets are now pricing a rise to 4% by November and 4.25% in early 2027, not a cut. The next MPC decision falls on 17 September. Gilt yields have moved accordingly: the 10-year has stayed above 5% and the 30-year touched a two-month high near 5.75% after Burnham signalled he would seek more flexibility within the fiscal rules. Higher borrowing costs eat directly into the headroom Healey has to spend or cut taxes on 28 October.

A Budget with narrowing options

That headroom was already tight. Analysts estimate a fiscal gap of at least £22bn against the government's rules, and Labour's manifesto rules out raising income tax, national insurance or VAT — the three levers that raise the most money with the least administrative mess. Treasury officials have instead been examining windfall levies on banks and oil and gas producers, including extending the Energy Profits Levy beyond its scheduled March 2030 expiry or raising its rate, as a way of closing a roughly £4.7bn shortfall without touching the manifesto pledges. Banking bosses have pushed back hard: JPMorgan's Jamie Dimon and Citigroup's Jane Fraser have both warned Healey against a new bank tax, and UK Finance has written to the Treasury flagging the risk to the sector.

The North Sea reaction has been sharper still. Russell Borthwick of the Aberdeen and Grampian Chamber of Commerce called the government's approach "reckless, wrong and economically ruinous," warning the industry is being pushed toward "game over" territory, and more than 7,000 businesses and workers have signed a letter urging ministers to fast-track a new fiscal regime before more jobs go. The current levy already pushes the marginal tax rate on North Sea profits as high as 78% when combined with existing charges — a rate industry groups say is accelerating decline in a basin the government still wants for energy security.

What ties it together

The growth and confidence data give Healey a better story to tell on 28 October than he had a month ago. But better data also means a Bank of England less inclined to cut — and possibly inclined to hike — which keeps gilt yields elevated and shrinks the fiscal headroom the growth numbers were supposed to create. Combined with a manifesto that rules out the biggest revenue levers and an oil sector warning against the levy options left on the table, Healey's room for manoeuvre is narrower than the headline PMI and confidence figures suggest. The next test comes on 17 September, when the MPC decides whether resilience has tipped from reassuring to inflationary.
