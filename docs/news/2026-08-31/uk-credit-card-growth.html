UK credit card borrowing grows at fastest pace in 17 years

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, whose monthly Money and Credit release tracks UK household borrowing — George Rex / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
UK credit card borrowing grew 12.5% annually in July, the fastest pace since 2009, as households added £1.4bn in new debt in a single month.

British households reached for the plastic in July at a rate not seen since before the financial crisis. Annual growth in credit card borrowing held at 12.5%, the Bank of England's Money and Credit figures showed — a pace last matched in 2009 — as consumers added £1.4bn in fresh borrowing over the month, split almost exactly down the middle between credit cards and other forms of consumer credit such as personal loans and car finance.

The number caps a steady climb through the year. Credit card growth ran at 12.1% in January, ticked up to 12.3% by March, eased slightly, then reaccelerated to 12.5% in June and held there into July — a trajectory that has taken the annual rate roughly back to where it stood in the aftermath of the 2008 crash, when consumers leaned on cards to smooth spending through a very different kind of shock. Total consumer credit growth, which includes cards, has also been climbing, hitting 9.1% in June, the fastest since July 2018.

Debt filling the income gap

The simplest reading is that borrowing is doing work income used to do. The household savings ratio fell to 8.9% in the first quarter of 2026, down from 9.6%, as spending on housing, utilities and eating out kept rising even as pay growth cooled. The Financial Conduct Authority has separately found that one in ten adults hold no cash savings at all, and a further 21% have less than £1,000 to fall back on. Against that backdrop, £1.4bn more borrowed in a single month reads less as confidence and more as a stopgap — the average UK adult now carries roughly £4,000 in unsecured debt, at rates that remain well above anything seen in the 2010s despite the Bank's easing cycle.

That easing has been cautious. The Monetary Policy Committee held the base rate at 3.75% at its 30 July meeting, with six members voting to hold and three pushing for a rise to 4% — a split that signals lingering unease about inflation even as growth data soften. Card borrowing at a 17-year-high pace complicates that calculus further: it flatters near-term retail and consumption figures without saying much about the households actually generating them, or their capacity to keep servicing the debt if rates stay firm for longer.

What it masks

Debt-funded spending has a way of making an economy look sturdier than it is. It keeps retailers' tills ringing and headline growth numbers respectable, but it also means that any deterioration in incomes or employment lands with more force than usual — arrears and defaults tend to move with a lag, not in step with the borrowing that preceded them. Consumer-facing sectors, from retail to hospitality, are the most exposed if that lag closes; lenders themselves face rising impairment charges rather than an immediate credit crunch, at least while employment holds up.

The next Money and Credit release, due in early October, will show whether July's pace was a peak or the start of a steeper climb into the autumn — timed, awkwardly for the Treasury, just weeks before Chancellor Rachel Reeves's successor at the Budget dispatch box addresses a fiscal gap of his own. The MPC's next rate decision, on 17 September, will be reading the same data with half an eye on whether households borrowing more is a sign of confidence or of running out of road.
