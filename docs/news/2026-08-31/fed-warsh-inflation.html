Fed's Warsh says central bank still has 'work to do' on inflation

Kevin Warsh, confirmed as Federal Reserve Chair in May 2026, delivered his first Jackson Hole address as central bank chief. — Federal Reserve / Wikimedia Commons (official portrait)
In one sentence
Fed Chair Kevin Warsh said underlying inflation hasn't improved, pushing September rate-hike odds to 57% and dashing 2026 cut hopes.

Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh used his first Jackson Hole address as central bank chief to deliver a blunt message: the inflation fight is not over, and the Fed is not yet ready to declare victory. The remarks, delivered Friday at the Kansas City Fed's annual symposium in Wyoming, pushed traders to price in higher odds of a rate increase next month rather than the cut many had hoped for.

"We must be confident that underlying inflation is moving to our objective, clearly and at sufficient speed," Warsh told the assembled central bankers and economists. "Otherwise, we have work to do. That's our job, our mandate, and our charge to keep."

The numbers behind the warning

Warsh leaned on hard data rather than sentiment. The 12-month change in the Fed's preferred inflation gauge, the personal consumption expenditures price index, stands at 3.7%, he noted, with the six-month annualised rate running hotter still at 4.1%. More than half of the goods and services in the PCE basket — 54%, by his count — are seeing price increases above 3%. Both figures sit well clear of the Fed's 2% target, and Warsh was explicit that recent, better-than-expected summer readings had not changed his view. "They do not tell me that underlying trends have meaningfully improved," he said, adding that "the responsibility for 65 months of sustained, elevated inflation sits squarely with the central bank."

The federal funds rate has sat at 3.50%–3.75% since the Fed's spring meetings, with no cut approved or clearly signalled at any point in 2026. Warsh, confirmed to the chairmanship in a 54–45 Senate vote in May after Jerome Powell's departure, notably declined to commit to a specific policy path. "I stand here today committed to a discipline, not to a decision," he said, arguing that forward guidance "risks creating ambiguity in the name of clarity."

Markets reprice, modestly

The ambiguity didn't stop traders from moving. Fed funds futures put the odds of a quarter-point hike at the September 16 meeting at roughly 57%, up sharply from about 35% before Warsh spoke. Treasuries sold off in a bear-flattener pattern: two-year yields rose about 8 basis points, the ten-year added 4 basis points to 4.72%, and the 30-year climbed 2 basis points to 5.21% — the short end moving furthest, consistent with markets bringing near-term tightening forward rather than revising their long-run inflation view.

Equities took the news calmly by comparison. The S&P 500 slipped 0.25% and the Nasdaq fell 0.52% on the day, both indexes still finishing the week modestly higher. The muted reaction, smaller than after Warsh's June and July comments, suggests investors had already discounted a hawkish tilt heading into the speech.

A quieter friction with the Treasury

Warsh's line also lands awkwardly next to the Treasury's own market operations. Secretary Scott Bessent has been leaning against rising long-term yields, and the department said it will at least double the size of its weekly buybacks of off-the-run debt starting September 9. The moves point in different directions — Warsh signalling tolerance for higher borrowing costs if inflation demands it, Bessent working to cap them — and a CNBC survey found 77% of economists doubt the Treasury's buyback push will succeed in holding yields down regardless.

For now, the practical effect on households and businesses is continuity rather than change: the wait for cheaper borrowing extends further into an already-long stretch of elevated rates, with the mortgage and corporate-credit markets that had begun pricing in relief now recalibrating toward a longer hold — or a hike.

The next test comes September 16, when the FOMC delivers its rate decision alongside fresh economic projections and a dot plot that will show whether Warsh's Jackson Hole tone reflects the committee as a whole or just its chair.
