OpenAI, Anthropic cut prices as DeepSeek raises them in inference cost battle

OpenAI CEO Sam Altman; OpenAI cut GPT-5.6 Luna pricing by 80% even as Chinese rivals raised theirs. — Steve Jennings / Getty Images via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
OpenAI and Anthropic are cutting AI inference prices to chase market share, while DeepSeek and Moonshot AI raise theirs to build defensible, profitable niches instead.

For eighteen months, the story of AI pricing was a straight line down. Every few months a lab would ship a cheaper model, a rival would undercut it within weeks, and the assumption hardened into consensus: intelligence was becoming a commodity, and commodities go to zero. That story broke this month.

On August 16, DeepSeek raised prices on its V4 models by as much as twelve-fold. Output tokens on V4-Flash, which cost a flat $0.28 per million a month ago, now run $1.32 per million at peak hours. V4-Pro output jumped from $0.87 to $3.96 per million at peak. Nine days earlier, Moonshot AI's Kimi K3 had launched not as a budget alternative but as a premium one, priced at $15 per million output tokens — more than 50 times what DeepSeek used to charge for the same unit of work.

Meanwhile OpenAI and Anthropic, the two labs that Chinese competitors were supposedly racing to the bottom against, went the other way. OpenAI cut pricing on GPT-5.6 Luna by 80%, from $1/$6 per million input/output tokens down to $0.20/$1.20, funded, the company said, by a 20% reduction in end-to-end inference-serving costs. It's not the direction anyone expected a year ago, when DeepSeek's R1 model briefly wiped out $600 billion of Nvidia's market value by proving frontier-grade reasoning could be had for a fraction of the price.

Two different bets on the same technology

The split isn't really about cost of computation — both sides have access to broadly similar chip economics, adjusted for export controls. It's about what each side is trying to buy with its pricing.

OpenAI and Anthropic are optimizing for share. Anthropic's US enterprise market share fell from 77.6% in May 2025 to 53.7% this April even as its annualized revenue climbed toward $47 billion — a sign that the market is getting bigger and more contested at once, not that Anthropic is losing outright. Both labs are pricing as though the winner will be whoever's API sits underneath the most agents, copilots and internal tools when the dust settles, and margin can be rebuilt later through volume and lock-in. Google's Gemini 3.6 Flash, released the same month at $1.50/$7.50 per million tokens with a 17% efficiency gain, fits the same logic.

DeepSeek's reversal tells a different story. The company's rock-bottom prices did what they were meant to do — pull in a surge of developers — but that surge strained the compute behind the service to the point where sub-cost pricing stopped being viable. DeepSeek is now funding a $50 billion, 1-gigawatt data centre build in Inner Mongolia and has closed roughly $7-8 billion in outside funding ahead of a prospective Shanghai listing. Money that used to come from investors chasing growth now needs to come from customers paying closer to the real cost of serving them.

Moonshot skips the discount phase entirely

Moonshot's move is more telling still, because it never ran the loss-leader playbook DeepSeek is now retreating from. Kimi K3 launched in July as the largest open-source model release to date and was ranked by independent benchmarking as the best available model on offer — ahead of Anthropic's line-up, according to Arena.AI's rankings. Founder Yang Zhilin priced it at $15 per million output tokens from day one, positioned explicitly as a long-horizon agent model for software engineering and knowledge work rather than a mass-market chatbot.

The bet paid off quickly. Moonshot's annualized recurring revenue crossed $100 million in March, $200 million in May, and $300 million by mid-June, with API revenue — enterprise customers paying to embed the model, not consumers on subscriptions — accounting for more than 70% of that. DoorDash has reportedly adopted Moonshot's models for coding tasks. It is, in effect, a bet that a handful of commercially valuable niches, priced at what they're worth, beats chasing every user with the lowest sticker price.

Why the split matters for anyone running inference at scale

For a trading operation, inference cost isn't a line item to be absorbed — it's part of the cost of running the strategy, no different from exchange fees or data feeds. A signal-generation pipeline that calls a model thousands of times a day lives or dies on cost per token in exactly the way a market-making desk lives or dies on spread.

That makes the current fork consequential rather than academic. If Western frontier models keep getting cheaper — OpenAI's Nano tier now starts around $0.10-0.20 per million input tokens, with no direct Anthropic equivalent — they become the default choice for high-volume, latency-sensitive inference: the kind of always-on classification and signal work a trading bot does continuously rather than occasionally. Chinese models, by contrast, are drifting toward a different use case: selective, high-value calls where raw capability matters more than marginal cost, closer to how a firm might spend on a single expensive research report rather than a data subscription.

The risk sitting underneath both paths is durability. A pipeline built cheaply around a Western model assumes today's price holds; DeepSeek's customers made exactly that assumption in July and were proven wrong within weeks. A pipeline built around a premium Chinese model assumes the capability edge justifies the multiple; that holds only as long as Moonshot or DeepSeek's frontier models actually stay ahead, and Western labs are not standing still on quality either. Neither side has offered a pricing commitment worth building a five-year infrastructure decision on — DeepSeek's own API terms already reserve the right to adjust rates with limited notice.

What comes next

DeepSeek's new peak/off-peak pricing structure took effect August 16 and will be the first real test of whether users tolerate variable, multiple-times-higher rates or migrate elsewhere — watch usage figures over the next full billing cycle for a signal either way. Moonshot, for its part, is reportedly exploring a Hong Kong listing within months of K3's launch, which would make its revenue mix and margins public in a way DeepSeek's numbers, ahead of its own prospective Shanghai IPO, are not yet. For anyone pricing inference into a trading pipeline, the more useful question is no longer "which model is cheapest today" but which side of this split — commodity access or premium capability — the workload actually needs, because the two are now pricing themselves as genuinely different products rather than substitutes.
