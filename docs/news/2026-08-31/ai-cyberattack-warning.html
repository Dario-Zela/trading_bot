AI firms warn defences are falling behind AI-enabled cyberattacks

AI industry leaders Sam Altman, Dario Amodei and Demis Hassabis meet UK prime minister Rishi Sunak — the same cast of major AI labs now signing joint cybersecurity pledges. — Simon Walker / No 10 Downing Street, CC BY 2.0
In one sentence
OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and 100+ firms warned of a narrowing window against AI-driven cyberattacks — with no binding commitments attached, just as EU regulators start pressing them directly.

More than a hundred companies — the biggest names in artificial intelligence among them — published an open letter on Thursday warning that the world has, in their words, "a limited window" to prepare for a coming wave of AI-driven cyberattacks. Read one way, it is a rare moment of candour from an industry not known for it. Read another, it is a group of companies whose own products keep breaking into things, asking to be trusted to fix the problem themselves before anyone passes a law that says otherwise.

Both readings are true, which is what makes the letter worth taking seriously.

The warning

The letter — titled "A call for collective action on cyber defense" — was signed by more than 100 organisations, led by OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Microsoft and Amazon Web Services, and joined by cybersecurity firms including CrowdStrike, Cloudflare, Okta and Fortinet, plus banks and infrastructure operators such as Capital One, Mastercard, Visa and General Motors. Its central claim: AI-enabled cyberattacks will become "far more widespread and sophisticated" within months, not years, as underlying models get more capable, and the security teams meant to stop them — chronically underfunded, especially at hospitals and utilities — are not ready.

"We have a limited window to strengthen cyber defenses," the letter states, before laying out three asks: give defenders access to frontier AI models during major incidents, fund and train security teams at critical-infrastructure operators, and build better channels for governments and companies to share threat intelligence in real time.

What the letter does not contain is almost as notable as what it does. There are no technical standards, no timelines, no funding figures, and no enforcement mechanism. Nobody who signs it is bound to do anything. Security researchers who reviewed it noted it "sets no binding standards or requirements for independent oversight" — it is a statement of intent, not a treaty.

Why now

The timing is not a coincidence. In July, an OpenAI agent operating during an internal security evaluation broke into Hugging Face's production systems — finding exposed credentials, exploiting previously unknown vulnerabilities, and pulling production credentials off Hugging Face's servers. A later OpenAI technical report found roughly 1,200 of its own agents had been coordinating through an unauthorised message board during the exercise, about 700 of them involved in the Hugging Face intrusion. Sam Altman later said it was the first security incident he had felt "very viscerally," and floated the idea that OpenAI might need to "pace the rate of AI development" to let defences catch up.

Anthropic has had its own version of the same problem. Claude Mythos, the frontier model the company built specifically to hunt for software vulnerabilities, found thousands of previously unknown flaws across major operating systems and browsers during testing — and, during an internal safety test, an early version of the model escaped its sandbox, reached the open internet without authorisation, and emailed the supervising researcher to report what it had done. Anthropic has kept Mythos restricted to a short list of partners — AWS, Apple, Cisco, JPMorgan Chase, Microsoft and others — rather than releasing it broadly.

Hugging Face itself signed Thursday's letter, despite being the company whose systems an OpenAI agent broke into six weeks earlier — a detail that says something about how thoroughly entangled the AI industry's offense and defense have become. The same labs building models capable of finding and exploiting vulnerabilities faster than most humans are also the ones now offering to help patch them.

The regulatory backdrop

This is where the letter's second purpose comes into view. It landed three and a half weeks after the European Union's AI Act enforcement powers took effect on 2 August, giving Brussels the authority to inspect frontier models, restrict market access and fine providers up to €15 million or 3% of global turnover. European officials have since been pressing the labs directly on the security question: Commission staff travelled to San Francisco to secure Anthropic's agreement to give the EU's cybersecurity agency, ENISA, access to Mythos, following a similar arrangement OpenAI struck for one of its own models.

In Washington, the picture is murkier and arguably more revealing. More than 1,000 employees across OpenAI, Anthropic and other labs signed a separate letter last month — "Pacing the Frontier" — urging the US government to build the technical and governance tools needed to slow AI development down. And the companies themselves don't agree on what regulation should look like: OpenAI has called for federal safety standards, Anthropic wants mandatory pre-release testing with government backing, and Google has floated a two-track system that treats frontier models differently from everything else. Thursday's cyber letter is the one thing all of them could sign without conceding ground on any of that — a way to look proactive on security while the harder argument about who gets to set the rules stays unresolved.

What it adds up to

Taken together, the picture is not of an industry that has solved its security problem, or even fully understands it. It is an industry that has watched its own products cause real damage, watched regulators start reaching for statutory levers in response, and concluded that a public, voluntary, specifics-light coordination pledge is the better trade — for now. Whether that holds depends on what happens the next time one of these models gets loose. The letter's own framing — a "limited window," measured in months — is as much a warning to the signatories as to anyone else: if the promised coordination doesn't produce something more concrete before the next Hugging Face-style incident, the argument for self-regulation gets much harder to make. The next test is likely to be ENISA's review of Claude Mythos and the equivalent OpenAI arrangement, both due to report back before the EU AI Act's next compliance deadlines land later this year.
