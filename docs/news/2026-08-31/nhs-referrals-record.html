NHS mental health referrals and A&E attendances hit record highs

An NHS accident and emergency entrance — A&E attendances hit a record 2.49 million in July 2026 — Panhard / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0
In one sentence
NHS mental health referrals (2.37m) and A&E attendances (2.49m) both hit record highs in the May–July 2026 data, with no clear peak in sight.

The National Health Service logged two grim milestones in the same data cycle this month. NHS England figures published in mid-August show 2.37 million people had an open referral to mental health services at the end of July — the highest number since records began. In the same release, A&E attendances hit 2.49 million in July, also a record, capping what statisticians now describe as the three busiest months for emergency departments ever recorded, May through July.[^1][^2]

Neither figure is a one-month blip. Mental health referrals have been climbing steadily through the year: 2.37 million people were in the system at the end of May, rising through June and into July's record. Care contacts — actual appointments delivered, not just referrals sitting in a queue — rose 11.1% between May and June alone, to 2.54 million, which suggests services are working harder, not simply falling further behind.[^1] Whether the extra activity is closing the gap is a separate question, and the data available so far cannot answer it.

Two systems, one summer

The A&E numbers have a more legible cause. Sarah Scobie, deputy director of research at the Nuffield Trust, put it plainly: "There has been little respite for the NHS this summer, with one heatwave following another."[^2] Successive heatwaves drove up demand from heat-related illness, exacerbated existing respiratory and cardiac conditions, and pushed more people outdoors into situations that end in an ambulance call. Ambulance services answered 898,503 emergency calls in July, up 6.5% on a year earlier, and responded to 829,675 incidents — the fourth-highest monthly total on record.[^2]

Set against that backdrop, the NHS can point to genuine gains: 75.4% of A&E patients were seen, treated or discharged within four hours in July, up from June, and Health and Social Care Secretary Yvette Cooper noted a record 2.65 million diagnostic tests and checks were carried out in the same period.[^3] Professor Frankie Swords, NHS national medical director, credited staff for "outstanding professionalism, flexibility and hard work in the face of this summer surge."[^3] Even so, more than 150,000 patients waited over 12 hours in A&E in July, and average daily attendances passed 80,000 for the first time in June — a threshold once associated only with winter crises.[^2][^4]

The harder question on mental health

The mental health backlog resists a similarly tidy explanation. There is no heatwave equivalent driving it — no single, dateable shock. Instead, referral volumes have been on a multi-year upward path, and the causes cited by clinicians and researchers span workforce shortages, post-pandemic aftereffects, and rising demand for autism and ADHD assessments, which the government commissioned an independent review into in December 2025.[^5] For the quarter ending May 2026, the median wait between referral and second contact stood at 37 days nationally — but the 90th percentile wait was 169 days, meaning a meaningful share of patients waited nearly six months just to be seen a second time.[^1]

Children's services show the same pattern in miniature: 538,766 children and young people had an open referral at the end of June, and separate figures compiled by the Royal College of Psychiatrists put more than 550,000 children on mental health waiting lists as of late 2025, with 30% waiting over two years.[^5][^6] The college has warned that lengthy delays risk pushing children toward unregulated AI chatbots in place of clinical support — a symptom, if not a cause, of a system running short of capacity relative to demand.

What the numbers don't settle

NHS England's own commentary urges caution in reading a single month's data as a trend, and the Health and Social Care Committee's own reviewers have made the same point about the broader waiting-list picture: gains in one metric — the falling overall waiting list, now 7.27 million — sit uneasily alongside record demand elsewhere in the system.[^3][^7] Sarah Woolnough of the King's Fund noted that the government met its interim 18-week elective target in March, only for waiting lists to rise by nearly 170,000 since — a sign, she said, of how fragile the progress has been.[^7]

What neither figure resolves is whether rising demand reflects a population getting sicker, a public more willing to seek help, or a system whose earlier capacity to absorb referrals quietly has simply run out. NHS England's next Mental Health Services Monthly Statistics release and the September A&E figures, both due in the second week of October, will show whether July's records were a peak or another rung on a longer climb.
