Wall Street heads for best August since May despite Friday's inflation-driven dip

The New York Stock Exchange's trading floor, where August's monthly gains gave way to a jittery Friday session. — Eduard Hueber, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
The Dow's fifth straight monthly gain and best month since May for the S&P and Nasdaq masked a Friday selloff after Fed Chair Warsh's hawkish inflation remarks.

Wall Street closed out August with its best month for stocks since May, capping a run that has now stretched into a fifth consecutive winning month for the Dow Jones Industrial Average. But the manner of the finish told its own story: a Friday selloff triggered by a single paragraph of Federal Reserve rhetoric, on a week when the underlying economic data had actually come in soft.

By Friday's close the S&P 500 had added roughly 3% for August and the Nasdaq Composite about 4%, putting both indexes on pace for their strongest month since May, when a nine-week weekly winning streak in the S&P and double-digit gains in tech had set a high bar. The Dow, less tech-heavy and less volatile, is up around 2% for the month — enough to extend its win streak to five straight months, according to CNBC's tracking of the session. All three indexes remain within a few percentage points of their August highs.

A month built on cooling inflation

The rally's engine was straightforward: inflation data that came in soft enough, for long enough, to keep alive hopes that the Fed's next move is a cut rather than a hike. A July consumer price reading of 3.4% annual inflation — in line with forecasts and down from June's 3.5% — helped push the S&P 500 to a fresh record on August 12. Falling oil prices did some of the same work, easing pressure on both headline inflation and corporate input costs.

That backdrop was enough to carry the market through a choppy middle stretch of the month, including a mid-August pullback tied to rising Iran tensions and a run of losing weeks. By the week ending August 28, all three indexes had strung together a positive week — the Dow's first winning week in three — and August looked set to close as a clean, unambiguous up month.

Friday's reversal

Then came Jackson Hole. Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh, delivering his first keynote address at the Kansas City Fed's annual symposium on August 28, acknowledged that summer inflation readings had come in "better than expected" — but declined to declare victory.

"They do not tell me that underlying trends have meaningfully improved," Warsh said. "Otherwise, we have work to do."

Markets had opened Friday modestly higher, expecting Warsh to affirm the disinflation narrative the rally had been built on. Instead, traders read the speech as hawkish, and the reaction was immediate: the S&P 500 gave back its gains to close down about a quarter of a percent at 7,711.76, while the Nasdaq fell roughly half a percent to 26,402.42, dragged lower by chipmakers including Nvidia and Intel. Treasury yields jumped in response — the 2-year note rose to 4.35%, the 10-year to 4.72% — as fed funds futures pushed the implied odds of a September rate hike to roughly a coin flip, up from a much lower starting point earlier in the week. That move came on top of the Fed's August 24 decision to hold its benchmark rate at 3.50%–3.75%, a meeting at which three officials had already dissented in favor of a hike.

The weekend brought a second jolt: U.S. strikes on Iranian rocket launchers near the Strait of Hormuz pushed stock futures lower again heading into Monday's session, layering geopolitical risk on top of the rate uncertainty — an unwelcome reminder that August's gains were resting on more than one uncertain pillar.

Thin conviction, wide gains

The gap between the month's headline numbers and Friday's reaction is the real story. A single sentence from one Fed official was enough to erase a day's gains and reprice rate expectations across the curve — not because the data changed, but because investors' confidence that the disinflation trend is secure turned out to be shallower than the month's returns implied. Five straight up months for the Dow is a genuine signal of sustained risk appetite; a quarter-point index swing on a hawkish word choice is a signal that the appetite is running on a thin cushion of conviction, not conviction that could withstand a real data surprise.

That asymmetry — big monthly gains, fragile daily reactions — is what traders will be weighing as September opens. The next Consumer Price Index and Personal Consumption Expenditures reports, due before the Fed's September 15–16 meeting, will do more to settle the argument than anything said at a podium in Wyoming. Markets are currently pricing roughly even odds on a rate hike at that meeting, a level of uncertainty unusual for a market that spent most of August grinding higher. Whichever way the data breaks, it will land on a market that has shown, twice now, how quickly a month of gains can turn into a day of nerves.
