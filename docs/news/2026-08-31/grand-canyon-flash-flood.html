Grand Canyon flash flood leaves roughly 20 hikers missing

Phantom Ranch canteen, near the confluence of Bright Angel Creek and the Colorado River at the bottom of the Grand Canyon. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A flash flood on fire-scarred ground destroyed footbridges and the water line at the Grand Canyon's Phantom Ranch, leaving about 15 hikers missing after 62 were airlifted out.

A flash flood tore through the bottom of the Grand Canyon on Saturday afternoon, destroying footbridges over Bright Angel Creek and forcing the air evacuation of 62 hikers and campers from Phantom Ranch, one of the most visited backcountry destinations in the US national park system.

The water arrived fast. National Weather Service meteorologist Justin Johndrow said the canyon's inner corridor took roughly half an inch to a full inch of rain in about 30 minutes on Saturday — enough, on rock and steep grades that shed water rather than absorb it, to turn Bright Angel Creek into a torrent. "Most of it ran off instantly," he said. By Sunday evening, Grand Canyon National Park said as many as 20 people were initially unaccounted for; that figure had narrowed to roughly 15 as rangers worked through backcountry permits and campground reservations to identify who had actually been in the flood zone. One body, a 46-year-old man recovered near Crystal Rapids, has been found; officials have not confirmed whether his death is linked to the flooding.

A burn scar, then a downpour

The flood's severity traces back a year, to the Dragon Bravo Fire, which burned through more than 145,000 acres of the North Rim and Kaibab Plateau in the summer of 2025, gutting the historic Grand Canyon Lodge in the process. Fire strips slopes of the vegetation and root structure that would normally slow runoff; scorched soil also becomes hydrophobic, repelling rather than absorbing rain. A storm that a healthy watershed might shrug off instead sheets straight into the drainages below — in this case, the North Kaibab Trail corridor feeding into Bright Angel Creek. It is a pattern familiar across the burn-scarred US Southwest, where monsoon downpours followed by flash floods have become a near-annual hazard on freshly burned terrain, and one that is not going away: models generally point to sharper monsoon bursts and, cumulatively, larger and more frequent fires as the region warms.

The practical toll at the canyon floor is severe on its own terms. Nearly every footbridge across Bright Angel Creek was destroyed, cutting hikers off from the Black and Silver suspension bridges that carry foot traffic over the Colorado River itself. The Transcanyon Waterline, which supplies drinking water to both rims, was also knocked out, leaving the park running on limited reserves. Phantom Ranch, its canteen, the Bright Angel Campground and the North Kaibab Trail are all closed until further notice. Park spokesperson Joelle Baird said those still missing were largely backcountry permit holders or day hikers along the North Kaibab and Phantom Ranch corridor — a population rangers can partially trace through paperwork, but not locate until the debris-choked terrain is searched on foot.

A flash flood watch remains in effect into Monday evening as more thunderstorms move over Arizona and Utah, raising the risk of renewed flooding on ground already stripped of the footbridges and trail infrastructure that would normally support a search. The next marker is simpler than any of that: how many of the roughly 15 unaccounted-for hikers are found safe once crews can reach the inner canyon.
