Actors including Matt Lucas, Hugh Bonneville call for UK AI voice-cloning law

Hugh Bonneville is among more than 80 performers backing the Save Our Voices Now campaign. — Martin Kraft / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Over 80 British performers, including Hugh Bonneville and Matt Lucas, have written to the PM demanding a statutory right to own their own voice against AI cloning.

Three seconds. That is roughly how much of your voice a modern AI model needs to build a convincing clone of it — enough to make you say, in your own cadence and timbre, whatever a prompt asks for. It is this arithmetic that has pulled more than 80 British performers, among them Hugh Bonneville, Matt Lucas and Nicola Coughlan, into an open letter to Downing Street this week demanding a law that gives every person in the country legal ownership of their own voice.

The campaign, called Save Our Voices Now, was founded by the audiobook narrator Alice Sockett and the performer Peter Caulfield, and its signatory list reads like a cross-section of British film, TV and theatre: Bonneville and Lucas alongside Siobhán McSweeney, Luke Evans, Jen Brister and Pearl Mackie. Their letter to Prime Minister Andy Burnham is narrow in its ask, which is part of the point — it seeks a single statutory right, held by every citizen rather than just working actors, over the commercial and unauthorised use of their voice. A companion petition has gone up on the government's petitions site, needing 10,000 signatures for a formal response and 100,000 to be considered for a Commons debate.

Why actors, and why now

The technical trigger is speed. Cloning tools that once needed hours of clean studio audio can now produce a passable copy from a few seconds of speech, and near-broadcast quality from longer clips. For narrators and character actors — people whose entire professional asset is a distinctive, recognisable voice — that collapses the barrier between a legitimate voice-double booking and a synthetic replacement nobody was paid for. Sockett has described the shift as "an existential threat to our entire industry," arguing that unauthorised voice cloning is increasing week on week, from dubbed adverts to audiobook narration nobody sanctioned.

The letter frames this as a consumer-protection issue too, not just a labour dispute: convincing voice clones make scam calls, fraud and impersonation easier for anyone with a laptop and an internet connection, actor or not.

Where Whitehall stands

The government's answer, for now, is procedural rather than legislative. A spokesperson said only that ministers "value and protect human creators" and would "launch a consultation on how we address these harms, while protecting legitimate innovation" — language that commits Whitehall to a process, not a bill. That mirrors the pattern set by the earlier, broader fight over AI and copyright, where a promised government report on AI and copyright has yet to translate into statute despite months of lobbying from publishers, musicians and now performers.

The drafting problem is real enough to explain some of the caution: any voice-ownership right has to distinguish clearly authorised commercial licensing — dubbing, AI narration a performer has sold rights to, legitimate voice-double work — from unauthorised replication, without handing broadcasters and studios an unworkable compliance burden or creating a right so porous it protects no one.

What's next

There is no bill on the table, and the actors' letter does not pretend otherwise — it is pressure applied ahead of a consultation that has not yet been scheduled. The petition's climb toward the 10,000-signature response threshold is the first concrete marker to watch; a debate would need ten times that. For an industry watching AI reshape production costs elsewhere, this is a smaller and more personal fight than the sector-wide copyright question, but it tests the same premise: whether Britain moves to regulate generative AI's inputs before the market settles the question itself.
