Russia pounds Kyiv with drones for third straight night

Damage from a Russian drone strike on the Kyiv Pechersk Lavra earlier this year — part of a bombardment campaign that has since intensified further. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Russia fired nearly 2,000 drones at Ukraine in a week, hitting Kyiv for a third straight night and damaging a PepsiCo plant in Mykolaiv Oblast.

Kyiv woke to its third consecutive morning of drone strikes on Sunday, part of a Russian campaign that has fired nearly 2,000 attack drones at Ukraine in a single week — alongside more than 1,600 glide bombs and 31 missiles, several of them North Korean-made ballistic weapons, according to figures President Volodymyr Zelensky posted on Telegram on August 30. It is one of the most sustained bombardments of the war, and it has now drawn in a name familiar to shoppers far from the front line: PepsiCo.

Four days, no let-up

Russia has shifted tactics over the past week, moving from strikes concentrated after dark to prolonged daytime raids that keep air-raid sirens sounding across the capital for hours at a stretch. Kyiv residents have now endured four straight days of near-constant alerts. A strike on August 29 killed a two-year-old girl and injured a dozen others; a day earlier, an attack on an ammunition warehouse in the village of Myla killed at least 38 people. Zelensky said roughly 800 of the drones fired in the past four days were the faster, jet-powered variant Russia has increasingly deployed this year, a shift that gives Ukrainian air defences less time to react.

The Dnipropetrovsk region, in central Ukraine, has taken a parallel battering: more than ten strikes in 24 hours hit a construction store and a petrol station in Pavlohrad, while drones, artillery and glide bombs struck four districts including Nikopol and Zelenodolsk. The region sits at the heart of Ukraine's grain and steel belt, and its repeated targeting — alongside earlier strikes this month on a shopping centre in Kryvyi Rih that killed 16 — has become a proxy for how deep Russia is willing to reach beyond the immediate front.

A familiar name gets hit

Among the sites damaged this week was a PepsiCo production facility in Mykolaiv Oblast, struck on August 29 in an attack that Ukraine's State Emergency Service said also set fire to a food-industry warehouse. PepsiCo said no employees were injured but that it was "assessing the full scale of the consequences and taking steps to restore supplies" to Ukrainian customers. It is not the company's first brush with the war: a 2023 missile strike destroyed a dairy warehouse at a separate PepsiCo site in Vyshneve, near Kyiv, after the company had already relocated some production there specifically to get away from the front line.

That a company can move production away from artillery range and still lose a facility to a drone with a range measured in hundreds of kilometres is the operational reality multinationals in the region are now pricing in. Drones are cheap, plentiful and increasingly precise strike tools — not the kind of risk that can be managed by simply sitting further back from the contact line.

The wider read

None of this week's individual strikes moved commodity markets on its own. But the pattern — a grain-and-steel region under sustained bombardment, a Western consumer-goods plant among the casualties, and drone volumes that Ukrainian officials describe as unprecedented — is the kind of accumulation that reprices tail risk rather than triggering it in one move. Insurers covering assets in government-held Ukraine, and the multinationals still operating there, will be watching whether this week's tempo is a temporary surge tied to a specific Russian push or the new baseline. Grain traders have similar cause for attention: Dnipropetrovsk's fields sit well behind the front, and disruption there would test the assumption that Ukraine's core farmland is insulated from strikes concentrated on ports and export terminals further south.

The next marker is whether the daytime tempo holds into September. Ukrainian officials have tied the intensity to a broader Russian push to exhaust air defences ahead of autumn; a sustained fifth and sixth day of raids, rather than a step back to the previous nightly pattern, would be the clearer signal that this is now the operating environment rather than a spike.
