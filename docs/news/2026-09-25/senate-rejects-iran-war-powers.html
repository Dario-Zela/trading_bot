US Senate rejects resolution to curb Trump's Iran war powers

The U.S. Senate chamber, where lawmakers voted 49-50 against a resolution to curb President Trump's authority to continue military operations against Iran. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The Senate voted 49-50 against a resolution to limit Trump's Iran war powers, leaving military operations unconstrained by Congress as the conflict enters its eighth month.

The U.S. Senate voted 49-50 on Thursday to reject a resolution that would have forced President Trump to end military operations against Iran without new congressional authorization, stripping away what remained of Capitol Hill's leverage over a war now in its seventh month.

The defeat is notable less for its margin than for its finality. This was not the first such vote, nor even the first to fail — it was, by most counts, the ninth attempt since fighting began on February 28, and it came after Republicans had, in June, briefly allowed a version of this same measure to pass. That it failed now, with midterms six weeks away and Trump openly floating the annihilation of the Iranian state at the United Nations three days earlier, tells its own story about where the political center of gravity in the Senate has settled.

A resolution years — and votes — in the making

The measure came from Sen. Raphael Warnock (D-Ga.), who has introduced some version of a war-powers resolution roughly every two months since the war's opening strikes. "After 109 days of a failed war, and now a fragile, temporary, but welcome truce, will my Republican colleagues choose today to finally stand up to this president?" he asked from the floor before an earlier vote in June — a line that, updated for the calendar, could have served just as well on Thursday.

That June vote is the one that makes Thursday's defeat sting. The Senate then adopted a House-passed concurrent resolution 50-48, directing Trump to remove U.S. forces from hostilities against Iran absent a formal declaration of war. Four Republicans — Susan Collins, Lisa Murkowski, Rand Paul and Bill Cassidy — crossed over. Trump dismissed the vote on Truth Social as "poorly timed and meaningless," arguing his administration was not, in the legal sense the resolution required, engaged in "hostilities" at all. Because a concurrent resolution carries no presidential signature and the White House simply asserted it didn't apply, the war continued regardless. It was a rebuke with no teeth, and the administration made a point of showing that.

Thursday's vote removed even that symbolic gesture. The tally: Collins, Murkowski, Paul and Thom Tillis of North Carolina — the only Republican voting for a resolution like this for the first time — joined 46 Democrats in favor. John Fetterman was the sole Democrat to vote no; Angela Alsobrooks did not vote. Cassidy, after a White House briefing from Vice President JD Vance and envoy Steve Witkoff that he said "address[ed] many of my concerns," flipped back to opposition. Senate Majority Whip John Barrasso is credited by several accounts with holding the rest of the caucus together.

The senators who crossed over framed it in institutional rather than partisan terms. "The Constitution gives Congress an essential role in matters of war and peace," Collins said in a statement. "Sustained combat operations require full engagement with Congress." Tillis, who is retiring in January, was blunter about the war's drift: "We're reaching a point to where we just need answers. We got to know what success looks like. There's never been any way to measure what success looks like." Murkowski's objection ran the other way — she warned that an abrupt pullback "would not leave any Americans — soldiers, diplomats, or civilians — in the Middle East in a safer position," a rationale that has anchored most Republican no votes since February.

Annihilation, on the record

The vote landed two days after Trump told the UN General Assembly he faced a binary choice on Iran: a negotiated deal, or something starker. "Do I annihilate the Islamic Republic and do it quickly, never giving them a chance to kill and destroy people and countries again?" he said, adding, "Do I drive them into hell with no chance of survival?" The Iranian delegation walked out partway through. Iran's Armed Forces General Staff responded by warning it was prepared to answer with attacks "crushing" — more severe, it said, than anything in the war's first phase. Trump also told the chamber he expected a peace agreement only after the November 3 midterms, an admission that the war's near-term trajectory is now tied to domestic political timing rather than battlefield conditions.

A UN fact-finding mission had, days before that speech, said it found reasonable grounds to believe U.S. forces committed war crimes in two strikes on Iran, including one that hit a primary school in Minab. None of it moved enough Republican votes.

What the market is actually pricing

For traders, Thursday's vote is best read as a regime confirmation rather than a surprise. Brent crude has already round-tripped violently through the war — from roughly $80 in the opening days of March, back down near $70 by midsummer as a partial Hormuz-reopening deal seemed close, and up again toward the high-$90s and beyond in September as strikes on the strait intensified. The International Energy Agency has called the resulting disruption the largest in the history of the global oil market, with the Strait of Hormuz — the conduit for roughly a fifth of global oil trade — running at a fraction of normal transit volumes for extended stretches since the spring.

The practical effect of Thursday's vote is to confirm that the war's duration is now a function of one man's negotiating calendar rather than a congressionally bounded timeline. That matters for how the risk premium in oil, defense equities and Gulf-exposed currencies should be modeled: not as a discrete event risk with a legislative off-ramp, but as an open-ended variable tied to UN General Assembly rhetoric, midterm politics and whatever Witkoff is negotiating with Tehran out of public view. Defense primes have traded higher on sustained appropriations expectations through the year; energy markets have taken their cues almost entirely from Hormuz transit data rather than Washington.

What's next

Warnock and his co-sponsors are expected to force another vote within weeks — the cadence has held at roughly six-to-eight week intervals since February, and nothing in Thursday's result changes the political math that has sustained it. The more consequential dates are external to Congress: the November 3 midterms, which Trump has explicitly tied to his own timeline for a deal, and whatever comes of the Witkoff-led back channel that pulled Cassidy off the resolution once already. Until one of those produces a change in Tehran's or Washington's posture, the Strait of Hormuz transit numbers — not the Senate roll call — remain the indicator worth watching.
