Texas AG Paxton launches probe into data centres' water usage amid drought pressure

In one sentence
Texas AG Ken Paxton opened a compliance probe into hundreds of data centres' water-use reporting as drought now covers 69% of the state.

Ken Paxton's office spent Thursday doing something Texas hasn't really done before: treating a data centre's water bill as a legal liability, not a utility footnote.

The Texas Attorney General announced what his office is calling a "landmark" investigation into hundreds of data centre developments across the state, examining whether operators have complied with water-use reporting requirements as the state grapples with a drought that has swallowed 69% of Texas in the space of a month. Statewide reservoir storage has slipped to about 74% full, several points below normal for late September, according to the Texas Water Development Board's own tracking. Paxton's framing was blunt: "Texas resources, water, and energy supply cannot be sacrificed," he said, adding that his office would keep pressing the issue.

It is a narrow legal hook — a reporting-compliance probe, not (yet) a shutdown order — but it lands at a moment when the sheer scale of Texas's data-centre build-out has outrun the paperwork meant to track it.

A boom nobody finished counting

Texas law already requires the Texas Water Development Board to survey any entity drawing significant ground or surface water for industrial, municipal or power-generation use. Data centre operators are supposed to be part of that annual count. In practice, they mostly aren't: only 17% of the 341 data centres operating in Texas last year responded to the board's survey, and the agency has no power to compel an answer. A parallel Public Utilities Commission survey did better, but still only pulled responses from 28 companies covering 92 facilities — a fraction of the fleet.

The number that explains why anyone cares is the growth curve underneath it. Texas had 22 data centres in 2023. It had 341 last year. Independent estimates of what they're drinking vary widely — a Houston Advanced Research Center analysis puts existing facilities at roughly 25 billion gallons a year, while other tallies put 2024 consumption above 50 billion gallons, enough to keep a city the size of Austin supplied for months. State projections cited alongside the investigation put statewide data-centre water use at roughly 49 billion gallons this year, rising toward 399 billion gallons annually by 2030 if the pipeline of announced projects gets built as planned.

None of that made it into the state's own long-range planning. The draft 2027 State Water Plan, built on historical usage patterns, doesn't account for the data-centre surge at all — a gap regional water planners and reporters have flagged for months before Paxton's announcement gave it a legal edge.

Where the probe is pointing

The initial sweep names counties rather than companies: Harris, Montgomery, Travis, Dallas, Tarrant, Smith, Bexar, Tom Green, Potter, Randall, Lubbock, Midland, Ector, Wichita, Victoria, Brazos, Nueces and El Paso — a list that runs from the Houston and Dallas–Fort Worth metros through the Permian Basin and out to El Paso, tracking almost exactly where hyperscale capacity has been sited over the past three years. Tom Green County, home to a cluster of projects around San Angelo, has drawn particular attention in local coverage as a test case for how the investigation plays out on the ground.

Some operators are already pointing to cleaner cooling technology as a defence. QTS, one of the larger colocation developers active in the state, says every facility it has built since 2019 uses closed-loop cooling — systems that circulate water through sealed piping rather than losing it to evaporation, cutting daily withdrawal sharply compared with older open-loop or evaporative-cooling designs. That distinction is likely to matter a great deal as the investigation proceeds: a facility recycling its water internally is a very different regulatory target from one drawing fresh groundwater and evaporating it into the Texas sky.

The backdrop to Paxton's move is a state government that has already started tightening the screws elsewhere. Governor Greg Abbott directed the state's grid operator and utility regulator in August to audit data-centre proposals for their electricity and resource demands before approving new connections — the water investigation is very much a companion action, not a standalone one.

The capital-allocation problem

For the hyperscalers and colocation developers building out AI infrastructure, the immediate exposure here is compliance risk, not shutdown risk — nobody is threatening to pull a permit yet. But investigations of this kind have a way of becoming templates. If Texas can show that a state attorney general has real leverage over where and how a data centre draws its water, other drought-exposed states — Arizona, Nevada, parts of the Southeast — have a ready-made playbook to borrow.

That matters because water, unlike power, is much harder to route around. A data centre that can't get grid capacity in one county can often find it in the next; a data centre that can't get a reliable water allocation in a drought-stressed basin has fewer places to go, and the sites left over tend to be more expensive to build and cool. Closed-loop systems reduce the exposure but add capital cost, and retrofitting them into projects already under construction is not free. Investors pricing data-centre-adjacent real estate, utilities and colocation REITs have mostly modeled power constraints as the binding limit on AI capacity growth this cycle; this investigation is an early signal that water deserves the same billing.

What comes next

Paxton's office has not set a public deadline for the compliance sweep, and his office's statement leaves open whether non-responsive operators face fines, subpoenas, or simply reputational pressure to comply. The more consequential date is procedural rather than legal: the Texas Water Development Board's 2027 State Water Plan is due for further revision, and whether the board finally folds data-centre demand into its baseline assumptions will determine whether this investigation was a one-off political gesture or the opening move in a genuine capacity constraint on where Texas lets the AI buildout land next.
