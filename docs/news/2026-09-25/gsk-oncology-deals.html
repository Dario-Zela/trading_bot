GSK strikes two Asian oncology licensing deals worth up to $2bn combined

GlaxoSmithKline's former Brentford headquarters in London. — Jim Linwood / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
GSK licensed two preclinical Asian oncology assets for up to $2bn combined — a KRAS-EGFR conjugate from Hutchmed and a T-cell engager from Chimagen — both years from market.

GSK signed two oncology licensing deals with Asian biotechs this week worth up to $2 billion combined, neither of which will generate a cent of revenue for years. That is not a criticism — it is the business model. Big pharma has largely stopped trying to discover early-stage cancer drugs in-house and is instead paying smaller, often China-based, biotechs to do the risky preclinical work, then buying in once the biology looks promising enough to justify the bet.

The larger of the two agreements, disclosed this week, sends up to $1.295 billion to Hong Kong-listed Hutchmed for HMPL-A830, a preclinical antibody conjugate that targets both the KRAS and EGFR pathways in solid tumours. GSK will pay $110 million upfront and the rest in development, regulatory and commercial milestones plus royalties. Two days later GSK disclosed a second, smaller deal: up to $750 million for a trispecific T-cell engager from Chimagen Biosciences, aimed at multiple myeloma, structured the same way — an unspecified upfront fee followed by milestone payments tied to success.

The KRAS problem, attacked from two sides

KRAS mutations sit behind roughly a quarter of all human cancers, and drugging the protein directly has been a decades-long slog — it was once dismissed as "undruggable" until Amgen's Lumakras broke through in 2021, followed by Bristol Myers Squibb's Krazati. Both are single-target KRAS inhibitors, and both have run into resistance and modest response rates in later trials, including a recent BMS setback in colorectal cancer.

Hutchmed's HMPL-A830 tries a different route. Rather than a conventional cytotoxic antibody-drug conjugate, it pairs an anti-EGFR antibody with a KRAS-inhibiting payload, using EGFR both as an entry point into tumour cells and as a second signalling target in its own right. GSK calls it an ATTC — antibody targeted therapeutic conjugate — rather than an ADC, a distinction meant to signal it is not simply delivering chemotherapy more precisely but blocking two oncogenic drivers at once. Hesham Abdullah, GSK's senior vice president for global oncology R&D, said the dual mechanism "has the potential to significantly improve upon current standard of care." Hutchmed will run a global Phase 1 trial, expected to start later this year, focused on colorectal, pancreatic and lung cancers — the tumour types where KRAS alterations are most common. GSK takes over development and commercialisation after that, everywhere except mainland China, Hong Kong, Macau and Taiwan, which Hutchmed retains.

A second bet, one rung down the pipeline

The Chimagen deal sits earlier still. T-cell engagers — antibodies that physically tether a patient's T-cells to cancer cells — have produced striking results in multiple myeloma but have also been dogged by toxicity, including cytokine release syndrome, that limits how broadly and how early doctors are willing to use them. Chimagen's asset is trispecific: it binds T-cells while simultaneously latching onto two separate tumour antigens, a design GSK argues could deepen and prolong responses while easing the tolerability problems that have kept existing engagers as later-line options. Phase 1 trials are not expected before 2027 — a full year behind the Hutchmed asset. It is also not GSK's first pass at Chimagen; the two struck an earlier deal in 2024, worth up to $850 million, for a separate T-cell engager targeting B-cell conditions. The multiple myeloma T-cell engager market in the US alone is projected to exceed $10 billion by 2032, against roughly 180,000 new diagnoses a year globally and a disease that remains incurable.

What the pattern says

Taken together, the two deals describe a strategy rather than a pair of one-offs: GSK is filling gaps in blood and solid-tumour oncology by importing dual-targeting biology from Chinese and Hong Kong biotechs at the preclinical stage, before competitors bid the price up. It is a cheaper way to build a pipeline than in-house discovery or late-stage M&A, but it pushes essentially all the clinical risk into the future — both assets are still years from a regulatory filing, and most drugs that enter Phase 1 never reach the market. The near-term financial impact for GSK is limited to the combined upfront payments, a small fraction of the headline $2 billion. The reads that matter are further out: Hutchmed's Phase 1 data for HMPL-A830, expected to start generating results after the trial opens later this year, and the first patients dosed with Chimagen's engager once its Phase 1 begins in 2027.
