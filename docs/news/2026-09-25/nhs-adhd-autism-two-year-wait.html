NHS bodies in England impose two-year minimum wait for ADHD and autism assessments

An NHS hospital entrance in Norfolk. Integrated care boards across England are now formally rationing ADHD and autism assessments rather than quietly letting waits grow. — FrancisTyers / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Four NHS integrated care boards have formalised at least a two-year minimum wait for ADHD and autism assessments, as 960,000 people queue in England.

For years, the story of NHS neurodiversity care in England has been one of quiet, creeping delay — assessments taking longer, waiting lists growing, nobody quite admitting the system had stopped coping. That pretence has ended. Four NHS integrated care boards have now formally adopted minimum waits of two years or more before anyone referred for an ADHD or autism assessment can be seen, turning what was an informal failure into declared policy.[^1]

The clearest example is West Yorkshire. The integrated care board (ICB) there — which commissions care for 2.4 million people across Leeds, Bradford, Kirklees, Calderdale and Wakefield — has told independent providers that no one referred for an ADHD or autism assessment will be seen in under two years from the date of referral, regardless of clinical urgency.[^1] Three other ICBs have adopted comparably strict minimums, and in total 15 ICBs covering roughly 19 million people — nearly a third of England — have introduced some form of rationing, whether through minimum waits, prioritisation criteria, or caps on how many assessments a provider may deliver.[^1][^2]

The numbers behind the policy are stark. England-wide, close to 960,000 people are currently waiting for an ADHD assessment, with average waits already exceeding a year — about 64 weeks — before any formal rationing is applied. A further 295,000 are waiting for an autism assessment.[^1][^3]

Why the bill got too big to pay

West Yorkshire's ICB says its hand was forced by cost. Spending on independent ADHD and autism assessments in the area rose from £7m in 2023–24 to £21m the following year — a threefold increase in twelve months.[^1] It is not an isolated case: one ICB elsewhere reported monthly spend rising from £146,000 to £1.5m over a similar period, and another projected its adult ADHD spending would climb from £11m to £51m in a single financial year.[^2]

Part of the problem is structural. Patients have a "Right to Choose" any qualified provider for an NHS-funded assessment, and because there is no national tariff, prices for what is nominally the same service have varied wildly — from roughly £300 to £3,000 depending on the provider.[^2] NHS England moved in March 2026 to rein this in, publishing a new payment framework and asking ICBs to agree "Indicative Activity Plans" with each independent provider, fixing how many assessments they deliver, the maximum wait patients should face, and how much money is available to pay for it.[^2] The West Yorkshire policy, and others like it, appear to be the sharp end of that exercise — a ceiling on activity translated directly into a floor on waiting time.

There is also a demand story underneath the cost story. Diagnosis rates for both conditions have climbed sharply this century: among boys aged 7 to 18 in England, recorded autism prevalence rose from roughly 0.2% in 2000 to about 4.7% in 2025, and ADHD prevalence from 0.4% to 5.1% over the same period, according to figures compiled in reporting on the policy — a more than tenfold increase in each case.[^2] Whether that reflects genuinely rising incidence, wider awareness, changed diagnostic thresholds, or some mixture of the three is disputed among clinicians, and this piece does not attempt to adjudicate it. What is not disputed is that NHS commissioning budgets were not built to absorb demand growth of that scale.

The political proximity is hard to miss, if arguably coincidental. West Yorkshire ICB's area includes Pontefract, Castleford and Knottingley — the parliamentary constituency of Yvette Cooper, the health secretary in Andy Burnham's government.[^1] Neither the ICB nor the Department of Health and Social Care has suggested the timing carries any particular significance, and there is no evidence the policy was directed from Whitehall rather than decided locally in response to the board's own finances.

"A locked door with a date on it"

Patient groups have not been measured in their response. Henry Shelford, chief executive of ADHD UK, called a two-year minimum wait "heartless and dangerous," adding: "It is institutionalised cruelty… This is the health service denying people care they need, and care that is available, and slamming the door shut on people who need an assessment of their symptoms to give them a diagnosis and unlock essential care that helps people hold down jobs, stay in education and stay out of crisis services."[^1][^3] A spokesperson for the campaign group Finally Seen put it more tersely: "A two-year minimum wait is not a waiting list. It is a locked door with a date on it."[^2] Mel Merritt of the National Autistic Society warned that restricting access "denies people support because a diagnosis is needed to get help" — pointing to the practical problem that in England, many school accommodations, workplace adjustments and specialist services are gated behind a formal diagnosis, not just a suspected one.[^2]

Rebecca Gray, representing the NHS Alliance body of independent providers, offered a blunter diagnosis of the system itself, describing the current arrangement as "unsustainable" and one that "delivers a bad deal for patients."[^2] That is a notable admission from within the sector being asked to absorb the rationing.

What comes next

The nearest catalyst is a government-commissioned review of adult ADHD and autism services led by Professor Peter Fonagy, reportedly running to some 700 pages, whose publication has slipped from its original schedule to early October.[^2] That report is expected to address exactly the questions this policy raises by default — how assessments should be funded, priced and prioritised nationally, rather than left to 42 ICBs making incompatible local judgements. Until it lands, the working answer in West Yorkshire and elsewhere is that patients wait, and the NHS no longer pretends otherwise.
