Shell sells Rhode Island gas plant stake to Constellation for $715m

A combined-cycle natural gas power plant, the same generation type as the Rhode Island State Energy Center at the center of the deal. — Peoplepoweredbyenergy / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Shell sold its 609MW Rhode Island gas plant to Constellation for $715m, trading operational power assets for trading-desk focus as utilities chase AI-driven demand.

Shell has agreed to sell its stake in RISEC Holdings, the owner of the Rhode Island State Energy Center, to Constellation Energy for $715 million — a deal that hands the country's largest owner of nuclear plants a 609-megawatt gas-fired workhorse in one of New England's tightest power markets.

The facility, a combined-cycle plant in Johnston, Rhode Island, has been feeding electricity into the ISO New England grid since 2002. Shell only came to own it in 2025, buying out Carlyle's majority stake as part of a broader push into North American power trading. Now it's flipping the asset within roughly a year.

"This transaction enables Shell to realize significant value on an accelerated timeline" while the company pursues other opportunities, said Andrew Smith, Shell's president of trading and supply.

That quick turnaround is the tell. Shell isn't retreating from RISEC because the plant underperformed — it's retreating because operating regional gas generation was never really the point. The oil major has said for more than a year that it wants a leaner power business built around trading and supply rather than owning and running plants. In a separate, simultaneous transaction, Shell is acquiring 100% of Hunlock Creek Generating in Pennsylvania, a 169-megawatt combined-cycle and peaking facility that strengthens its position in the PJM Interconnection — the kind of trading-adjacent footprint Shell wants to keep, even as it sheds an operating asset like RISEC.

Why Constellation wants it

For Constellation, the calculus runs the other way. Chief executive Joe Dominguez said the plant "perfectly complements Constellation's extraordinarily successful customer business in New England" and pointed to its favorable position on both the electric grid and the gas pipeline network feeding it. The company expects the deal to be immediately accretive to operating earnings, with returns above its 10% unlevered threshold, and says it won't disturb the $5 billion share buyback program already authorized through the end of 2027.

The price tag works out to roughly $580 million net of an estimated $135 million in first-year tax benefits — a detail Constellation flagged prominently, suggesting the deal was structured as much for its balance-sheet efficiency as for the megawatts themselves. Closing is expected in the first quarter of 2027, pending regulatory sign-off.

The bigger pattern

The transaction lands inside a wave. Power and utilities M&A hit $216 billion across the six months to May 2026, up 173% from the same period last year, according to industry deal trackers — a surge driven largely by data-center operators' hunger for dispatchable capacity that doesn't depend on the wind blowing or the sun shining. Renewables-led deals, the dominant flavor of utility M&A a few years ago, have given way to a scramble for gas and nuclear assets that can run around the clock.

Shell's exit and Constellation's entry sketch out the two sides of that scramble. Legacy oil majors that picked up power assets opportunistically are trimming operational exposure to focus upstream and on trading margins. Pure-play utilities and generators, meanwhile, are paying up for exactly the kind of steady, gas-fired baseload that data centers need and that new construction can't deliver fast enough — multiyear turbine lead times and a tight domestic gas market have made existing plants more valuable than the queue for new ones.

The next marker will be regulatory approval, expected ahead of the Q1 2027 close. Watch for whether state or federal reviewers press on market concentration in New England, where Constellation is already a dominant supplier — and for whether Shell's Hunlock Creek purchase signals it intends to keep some PJM-facing generation even as it walks away from operating assets elsewhere.
