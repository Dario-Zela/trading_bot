STANDING WATCH · GILTS · By M. Halloway · 2026-09-25 · 4 min read

BoE's gilt overhaul beds in as 10-year yields ease to 5.20%, still near crisis levels

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, which set out its revised gilt-unwind plan on 17 September. — George Rex / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
The BoE will hold £120bn of long gilts permanently and slow QE unwind to 2034, easing long-end pressure as 10-year yields dip to 5.20% — still near 2007 highs.

The Bank of England has, in effect, admitted that shrinking its balance sheet on autopilot was becoming a liability rather than a tidy piece of housekeeping. On 17 September it rewrote the rulebook for winding down the £488bn stockpile of gilts left over from more than a decade of quantitative easing — and the headline change is what it will no longer do: sell long-dated bonds into a market that has been struggling to absorb them.

"Today we provided clarity over the future of our quantitative tightening policy," said Governor Andrew Bailey, adding that the Monetary Policy Committee — which voted 9-0 for the plan — would "withhold a substantial part of the stock of gilts held for monetary policy purposes" while unwinding the remainder over the next eight years.

What changes, and what doesn't

The mechanics matter more than the headline number. Of the £488bn portfolio, £120bn — bonds maturing in 2049 or later — will be kept permanently, matched against the future growth of banknotes in circulation rather than sold. A further £222bn maturing by 2034 will simply be allowed to run off as bonds come due. That leaves £146bn of medium-to-long paper earmarked for active sales, at a pace of roughly £20bn a year rather than the £70bn annual rate the Bank had been running as recently as last September. Combined with maturities, the total annual unwind falls to about £46bn, and all sales are paused until April while the Bank consults the Debt Management Office on selling gilts directly to the Treasury rather than at public auction.

The practical effect is that the long end of the curve — 30-year and ultra-long gilts, the segment most exposed to pension and insurance demand that has been thinning for years — no longer faces a steady drip of Bank supply on top of the government's own issuance. The Bank's own estimate is that QT has added roughly 0.25 percentage points to gilt yields; some independent analysts put the effect on 30-year paper as high as 0.75 points. Removing forced seller behaviour at the long end doesn't eliminate that overhang, but it stops adding to it.

The number that framed it

The timing is not incidental. Ten-year gilt yields hit 5.295% on 10 September, their highest since 2007, as a global bond selloff collided with oil pushing through $100 a barrel. By Thursday they had eased to around 5.20–5.23%, a two-week low, as the QT overhaul and a partial retreat in oil gave the market room to breathe. That is relief, not recovery — yields remain roughly where they were during the 2022 mini-Budget crisis, and well above anything seen in the pre-2022 era of ultra-low rates.

Equities did not read the gilt move as unambiguously good news. The FTSE 100 closed at 10,679.99 on Thursday, down 0.24% on the session, as Brent crude jumped back to $107.25 a barrel from $102.74 the day before. That is the tension running through the market right now: lower long-end gilt supply is disinflationary for the discount rate on risk assets, but rising oil is a live inflation input that could yet force the MPC's hand on the short end even as it eases pressure on the long end.

The read-through

The substantive signal is less about gilts specifically than about how far a central bank will bend its own tightening programme when financial stability is at stake. The Bank spent 2022 through 2025 treating QT as a slow, steady, largely mechanical process, insulated from market conditions. This overhaul concedes that a sustained run of weak long-dated auctions and a still-jittery pension and insurance sector made that stance unsustainable — an implicit acknowledgment that balance-sheet reduction and market stability can conflict, and that stability wins.

That has a bearing well beyond the UK. The Federal Reserve and the ECB are both further along in shrinking their own portfolios, and both face similar questions about how much duration risk the market can absorb without central bank buying underneath it. The Bank of England has just shown one way to answer that question — hold the long end, sell the middle, and let time do the rest. Watch the DMO consultation due to conclude before the April restart, and whether Thursday's oil spike proves durable enough to complicate the MPC's next rate decision.
