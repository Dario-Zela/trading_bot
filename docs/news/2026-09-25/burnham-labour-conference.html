BEYOND THE TAPE · UK POLITICS · By L. Bordeaux · 2026-09-25 · 5 min read

Burnham vows to 'draw a line' under Brexit decade at first conference as PM, faces Gaza revolt as Reform slips in polls

Prime Minister Andy Burnham, who opens his first Labour conference as PM in Liverpool this weekend. — Alecsandra Dragoi / No 10 Downing Street (Open Government Licence v3.0)
In one sentence
A YouGov MRP poll shows Reform UK's seat projection collapsing by 188 in a year as Burnham opens his first Labour conference facing a left-wing Gaza revolt.

Liverpool this weekend will look, for the first time, like Andy Burnham's party rather than Keir Starmer's. The Labour conference that opens on Sunday at the BT Convention Centre is the new prime minister's first as leader, arriving barely ten weeks after he took the keys to Downing Street — and arriving, more usefully for him, on the back of a poll that has quietly rewritten the story of British politics.

The poll that moved the goalposts

A YouGov MRP model published on 24 September projects Labour as the largest party in a hypothetical snap election, taking 241 seats — well short of a majority, but comfortably ahead of the Conservatives on 130 and Reform UK on 123, according to Bloomberg's reporting on the data. No party would reach the 326 needed to govern alone; this is a hung-parliament picture, not a Labour landslide.

What makes it newsworthy is the direction of travel. The same MRP exercise a year earlier had Nigel Farage's Reform on course for 311 seats, just 15 shy of a majority. That projection has now fallen by 188 seats in twelve months — one of the sharper reversals YouGov's model has recorded. Reform's slide shows up in the weekly tracker too: in the 20–21 September voting-intention poll, Reform sat at 21%, its lowest reading since YouGov restarted the series in January 2025, with Labour and the Conservatives both close behind. Part of the squeeze is coming from Reform's own right flank — Rupert Lowe's breakaway Restore Britain, founded after he split from Farage, is now pulling 3–4% in the same tracker, fragmenting the vote Farage had been consolidating.

For a currency and gilt market that spent much of the past year pricing a real chance of a Farage government, this is a meaningful repricing exercise, even if nobody expects an election to be called imminently. Political risk premia are sticky things — they get built in gradually and unwound the same way — but a poll swing of this size, confirmed rather than one-off, gives investors a reason to mark down the tail risk of a disorderly change of government.

Burnham's pitch: a line under the decade

Burnham arrives in Liverpool wanting to bank that shift. Briefing ahead of the conference, he has framed his opening speech around the idea of "drawing a line" under what he calls the turbulent decade since the Brexit referendum — a period that has swallowed five Conservative prime ministers, a mauled Labour government under Keir Starmer, and now his own accidental elevation after Starmer's resignation in June. Burnham's argument, as trailed, is that political stability is now the precondition for a genuine change of direction, not a nice-to-have: only once the churn stops, he has suggested, can the government set out the "new direction" he wants to build toward in the autumn Budget and a promised ten-year plan for Britain.

It's a pitch aimed as much at markets and swing voters as at his own party — stability first, ambition second. The favourable poll numbers give him cover to make that case; the previous year's Reform surge was itself partly a product of voters concluding that nothing in Westminster was stable at all.

The Gaza fault line

The conference floor may prove less cooperative than the polling. Labour's left is planning to press Burnham on Gaza, with Stop the War, the Palestine Solidarity Campaign, Momentum and allied groups organising a march to the conference venue to demand recognition of genocide in Gaza and a full arms embargo on Israel. It is the sharpest visible fracture in the party since Burnham took over, and it lands squarely inside conference week rather than on the fringes of it.

Burnham's own position has shifted since he became prime minister — under Foreign Secretary David Miliband, the government has banned goods from illegal West Bank settlements and raised objections to Israel's E1 settlement tender — but he has stopped short of the genocide designation the left is demanding. Asked about the prospect of a conference floor vote against government policy, he was characteristically unworried in public: "I think the vast majority of people in Liverpool will vote for the change that [Miliband] has made in relation to the UK's position on the illegal settlements, the concerns we raised about the E1 tender process, the timing of it." Whether that confidence survives contact with the conference floor is one of the week's genuine unknowns — a defeat, even a symbolic one, would undercut the unity message Burnham is trying to sell alongside the poll numbers.

What ties it together

The two threads point in the same direction for anyone pricing UK political risk. The poll says the electoral threat that has hung over sterling and gilts for the past year — a Farage government — has receded, at least for now. The conference says the coalition delivering that outcome is not without its own stress points, Gaza chief among them, but none currently look large enough to threaten Burnham's majority in the Commons, which remains what actually matters for legislative continuity between now and the next scheduled election in 2029.

The more precise question for markets is less "who wins" and more "when does anyone vote again." Burnham has given no indication he intends to call an early election, and a comfortable-if-short-of-majority MRP projection is not an obvious incentive to gamble on one. The next data points worth watching are the Budget in October, where the ten-year plan gets its first fiscal test, and whether Thursday's conference vote on Gaza policy passes as comfortably as Burnham predicts.
