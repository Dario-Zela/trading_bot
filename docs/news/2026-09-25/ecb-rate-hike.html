ECB raises rates to 2.65%/2.50%, cites Middle East inflation pressure

ECB President Christine Lagarde, who called the September rate hike a "no-brainer" while warning inflation will stay elevated for an extended period. — Philippe Buissin / © European Union, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The ECB raised rates to 2.65%/2.50% and warned inflation will stay well above target for an extended period, citing Middle East-driven energy and shipping costs.

The European Central Bank raised its key interest rates on September 10th for the second time this year, lifting the main refinancing rate to 2.65% and the deposit facility rate — the one that matters most for market pricing — to 2.50%. The quarter-point move was widely expected. What rattled bond markets was the language accompanying it: policymakers said inflation would stay "well above target for an extended period," and pinned the blame squarely on a conflict thousands of miles from Frankfurt.

"The conflict in the Middle East continues to generate inflation pressures," the Governing Council said in its statement, a sentence that marks a shift in how the ECB is framing its own job. This is not a central bank fighting an overheating economy. It is a central bank trying to contain a price shock imported from a war zone, using a toolkit that was never built for that purpose.

A war the ECB cannot end

Euro area headline inflation hit 3.3% in August, up from 2.9% in July, driven overwhelmingly by energy. Prices at the pump and in gas bills have been climbing since the United States and Israel's confrontation with Iran escalated into sustained disruption of the Strait of Hormuz, the chokepoint through which roughly a fifth of the world's oil passes. Brent crude has traded above $100 a barrel through the summer; Dutch TTF gas, Europe's benchmark, is up roughly 190% since the start of the year.

Shipping costs have moved in tandem, and not gently. War-risk insurance premiums for tankers transiting Hormuz have risen from around 0.25% of a vessel's hull value before the conflict to between 7.5% and 10% now — meaning a $100 million tanker can face a war-risk bill north of $7 million for a single voyage that used to cost a few hundred thousand dollars to insure. The Baltic Exchange reported this month that hauling oil from the Persian Gulf to China has, for the first time, broken the $1 million-a-day mark. None of that is a monetary phenomenon, and none of it responds to a quarter-point rate move.

ECB President Christine Lagarde was blunt about the mismatch at her press conference, reportedly calling the hike a "no-brainer" while acknowledging the limits of what the bank can actually fix. The target isn't the oil price itself — Frankfurt has no lever for that — but the risk that an energy shock hardens into something stickier: wage demands, repricing across the economy, inflation expectations that stop believing the 2% target is real. "Wages do not show a material response to the energy shock at this stage," Lagarde said, in comments relayed from the conference, adding that the bank intends to keep it that way.

The forecasts moved with the rate

The ECB's staff projections, updated alongside the decision, now put headline inflation at 3.0% for 2026, easing to 2.5% in 2027 and 2.1% in 2028 — each figure revised higher than the bank's previous round of forecasts. Core inflation, stripped of energy and food, actually ticked down to 2.4% in August and services inflation eased to 3.0%, which is the detail keeping this from being a full-blown 1970s-style wage-price spiral scenario, at least for now. The gap between core and headline is the whole story: this is an energy shock sitting on top of an otherwise cooling economy, not a demand-driven boom.

That combination is awkward for a central bank. Growth forecasts were nudged up even as the rate path tightened, and the Governing Council was careful to frame its stance as "data-dependent and meeting-by-meeting," explicitly declining to "pre-commit to a particular rate path." Lagarde gave no forward guidance on the next move, which several analysts read as hawkish restraint rather than genuine uncertainty — a bank keeping its options open because it expects to need them.

Markets are pricing more to come

Bond investors did not wait for confirmation. Germany's two-year yield jumped as much as 12 basis points to 3.19% on the day, its highest level in nearly three years; the 10-year Bund yield rose to its highest since 2011, and France's 30-year yield touched levels last seen in 2003. Swaps markets are now pricing three more quarter-point hikes by the middle of 2027, with better-than-even odds of another move as soon as next month. Pantheon Macroeconomics put a number on it: a deposit rate near 3.0% by February, built on hikes in December and February.

Stocks and the euro both fell on the announcement, an unusual pairing for a rate hike — normally a stronger policy rate lifts a currency and dents equities on cost-of-capital grounds, but rarely both legs move against the standard playbook at once. That is a market reading the ECB's message correctly: this is not a central bank signalling confidence, it is one signalling that a geopolitical risk beyond its control has become the dominant input into its reaction function.

September's move was the second hike of the year, following a rise to 2.25% in June and a deliberate pause in July while the bank waited to see whether the Iran conflict's price effects would fade or persist. They persisted. The next data point that matters is the October inflation print, alongside whatever the Strait of Hormuz situation looks like by then — a phased reopening remains under discussion between Washington and Tehran, but no deal has been signed. Until one is, the ECB has effectively told markets to treat the conflict, not the domestic economy, as the variable driving policy.
