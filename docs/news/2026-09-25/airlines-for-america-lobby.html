Airlines for America opposes diesel export ban and Chinese carrier flight expansion

An Air China Boeing 777, the aircraft type flown on the disputed Beijing-New York and Beijing-Washington routes. — Konstantin von Wedelstaedt / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Airlines for America is fighting a proposed diesel export ban and extra Air China flights at once, a sign neither fight is urgent on its own.

Airlines for America, the trade group representing American, Delta, United and most of the rest of the US carrier fleet, picked two fights in Washington this week that have nothing to do with each other beyond both landing on the desk of its new president, Chris Sununu.

The first: a formal objection to a proposed ban on US diesel exports. The second: a protest against Air China running extra flights to New York and Washington that A4A says should never have counted as scheduled service in the first place. Neither is a crisis. Together, they read like a lobby group covering its bases rather than marshaling for a single decisive battle.

The diesel angle

On its face, an airline group weighing in on diesel policy looks like a stretch — commercial jets burn jet fuel (kerosene), not diesel. But the argument holds up better once you follow the refinery math. Trump has floated banning diesel exports to bring down prices for truckers and farmers, reasoning that keeping Gulf Coast diesel at home would ease the domestic squeeze. The catch, according to critics across the energy industry, is that refineries don't produce diesel, gasoline and jet fuel on separate lines. A glut of unsellable diesel would push refiners to cut overall throughput, and that means less jet fuel too.

Sununu made the airlines' case bluntly: "The chain reaction of issues that it causes — the prices don't go down," he said, adding that A4A has been in contact with the Departments of Energy and Interior. He's not alone. The US Chamber of Commerce, Business Roundtable, National Association of Manufacturers and the American Fuel & Petrochemical Manufacturers all signed a joint letter this week warning the ban would tighten supplies and raise costs — the trade group calling it a case where fewer exports simply mean less US production, not cheaper fuel at home.

The Air China dispute

The second front is a narrower, more procedural fight. Air China notified the Department of Transportation on September 14 that it planned two additional flights — Beijing to JFK, which flew September 19, and Beijing to Washington Dulles, scheduled for September 25 — timed around the run-up to a Trump-Xi meeting. A4A filed a formal objection on September 17, arguing the flights were being dressed up as one-off "event specific" operations when they actually expand Air China's scheduled capacity under the US-China aviation agreement. "Air China should not be permitted to use an extraordinary, event specific operation to expand scheduled capacity," the group told regulators.

Underneath the procedural language sits a competitive gripe US carriers have raised for years: Chinese airlines can still route through Russian airspace on some US-China legs, cutting flight time and fuel burn, while US carriers have been locked out since 2022 and must fly longer, costlier paths around it. That asymmetry, not this particular pair of flights, is the real irritant — the Beijing-JFK and Beijing-Dulles sectors are just the latest flashpoint in a dispute over market access that predates this administration.

Why it reads as routine

Neither position amounts to a five-alarm threat to airline margins. The diesel ban is still a proposal, not policy, and it's drawing opposition from petrochemical, trucking and manufacturing lobbies well beyond aviation — A4A is a co-signer, not the tip of the spear. The Air China dispute, meanwhile, is a recurring skirmish over a handful of flights, not a wholesale reopening of US-China capacity. That A4A is fighting both simultaneously, rather than concentrating its lobbying on one, suggests neither is urgent enough on its own to demand full attention — a lobby group hedging on two fronts because it doesn't have to choose.

The next checkpoints: whether the Trump administration formalizes the diesel export ban rather than leaving it as a talking point, and whether the Department of Transportation rules on Air China's request before the broader US-China aviation talks — still shadowed by the unresolved Russian-airspace imbalance — resume.
