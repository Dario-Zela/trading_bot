Ukrainians in UK fear homelessness as host-scheme funding cuts bite

10 Downing Street illuminated in the colours of the Ukrainian flag, February 2022, at the start of the war that prompted the Homes for Ukraine scheme. — UK Prime Minister's Office / Wikimedia Commons (Open Government Licence v3.0)
In one sentence
The UK is cutting Homes for Ukraine host payments by 70% from January 2027, and refugees like 73-year-old Tetiana fear their hosting arrangements — and housing — won't survive the change.

Tetiana is 73. She has lived with the same host family in the UK for more than two years, ever since fleeing Ukraine under a scheme that promised, at least implicitly, that Britain would stand behind its hosts for as long as it took. Now she is doing the arithmetic that thousands of other Ukrainian families in Britain are doing this week, and she does not like the answer.

"I feel that this financial change could impact on my host's ability to host me," she said. "If the hosting comes to an end, I don't know where I will go."

The change is a government decision, confirmed in a written statement to Parliament on 15 September, to cut the monthly "thank you" payment made to British households hosting Ukrainian guests from £350 to £100 — a reduction of more than 70 percent — starting 1 January 2027. Local authorities, meanwhile, will see the per-arrival tariff that funds their administration of the scheme fall from £5,900 to £3,300. The Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government says the changes are meant to "secure the scheme's long-term future while maintaining support for sponsors, guests and councils." Hosts and refugees describe something closer to a foundation being kicked out from under a programme that only recently marked its fourth anniversary.

A scheme built on goodwill, running low on subsidy

Homes for Ukraine launched in March 2022, days after Russia's invasion, as the centrepiece of Britain's response to the largest forced displacement in Europe since the Second World War. Unlike most refugee resettlement programmes, it did not rely primarily on state-run accommodation. It asked ordinary households to open spare rooms to strangers, in exchange for a monthly stipend intended to cover the household's added costs. Roughly 181,000 of the 234,000 Ukrainians who have sought sanctuary in the UK since 2022 have come through the scheme, backed to date by more than £1.81 billion in government funding.

The model always carried a structural risk: it depended on sustaining goodwill and cash in equal measure over years, not months, in a country not obviously equipped to do either. That risk is now surfacing. By the end of April 2026, councils had recorded 12,860 Ukrainian households as homeless, and two-thirds of those — more than 8,000 — became homeless specifically because a hosting arrangement broke down or ended, according to figures cited in the House of Commons Library. Officials themselves caution the real total is probably higher, since not every council reports consistently.

The payment cut lands directly on that fault line. Under the current rules, once a hosting arrangement collapses and no alternative housing can be found, the local authority's statutory homelessness duty kicks in — meaning the same councils facing a smaller tariff are also the ones legally obliged to pick up the pieces when hosting fails.

Hosts describe a lifeline being pulled

For many sponsors, the £350 payment was never framed as a wage — hosting was pitched, and largely taken up, as an act of solidarity rather than a commercial arrangement. But four years on, for some households it has become something closer to essential income.

"I was dismayed to learn that thank you payments are reducing to just £100," one host in Greenwich, who has sponsored a Ukrainian guest since May 2022, said. "I lost my job within months of becoming a sponsor and these payments have become an absolute lifeline even though I did not volunteer for this scheme for the money. I genuinely worry about the consequences of this change for Ukrainians in the UK."

Nataliya Rumyantseva, a host, framed the concern less in terms of household budgets than in what the payment structure was actually protecting against. "When refugees arrive here from Ukraine, they are shellshocked and need time to adapt," she said. "If hosting changes to become a more commercial rental arrangement, I think that's cruel."

A host in Cardiff currently sponsoring a Ukrainian student put it more bluntly: "The situation feels like a cheap shot from the government. We opened our home in good faith."

The government's own guidance acknowledges the tension it is trying to manage. Funding, ministers say, must "remain at a sustainable level" while still supporting "rematching and homelessness prevention activity" — official language for the process of finding a new host when an existing arrangement fails. The £100 rate is not new invention; it is closer to a floor the scheme has occasionally dipped to during past reviews, restored to £350 mainly in response to cost-of-living pressure. What is new is applying it permanently, to a refugee population whose members are, on average, further from returning home than they were at any point since 2022, given the continued fighting in Ukraine.

A test case for how refugee promises age

The UK is not alone in discovering that a refugee scheme designed at speed, in the emotional aftermath of an invasion, is harder to sustain once the invasion becomes a permanent backdrop rather than breaking news. Germany, Poland and other major host countries have each gone through their own rounds of trimming support for Ukrainian arrivals as budgets tightened and public attention shifted elsewhere. What distinguishes the British case is how directly the funding model ties household-level payments to the prevention of literal homelessness — there is no large state reception-centre system to fall back on if a host family calls it quits.

That design choice made Homes for Ukraine cheap and fast to stand up in 2022. It also means the scheme has no slack: when the subsidy that keeps hosts hosting shrinks, the next stop for a displaced family is not a waiting list, but a council homelessness office. Whether that office has the housing stock to respond — in a country already gripped by an acute shortage of affordable homes — is a separate and largely unresolved question.

The £100 rate takes effect on 1 January 2027, giving councils and charities roughly three months to prepare for what the rematching and prevention data since April already suggests: a meaningful share of hosting arrangements will not survive the change intact. The next test will be how many local authorities publish updated homelessness figures before that deadline, and whether Westminster treats a rise in Ukrainian homelessness claims as an argument to revisit the cut or as confirmation the scheme is simply being wound down by other means.
