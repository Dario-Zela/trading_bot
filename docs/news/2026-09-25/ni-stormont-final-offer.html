UK tells Stormont parties £1.5bn is 'final offer' as budget deadlock persists

Parliament Buildings at Stormont, seat of the Northern Ireland Assembly, where the Executive remains without an approved budget for 2026/27. — Dom0803 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
The UK government told Stormont's parties its £1.5bn, three-year funding offer — unchanged since 10 September — is final, with no more Treasury money coming.

There will be no more money. That was the message Sir Chris Bryant, the Northern Ireland Secretary, delivered to Stormont's party leaders on Thursday afternoon, closing off what little room remained for negotiation over a budget package that has now sat unchanged for two weeks.

The offer — a shade over £1.5 billion in additional funding spread across three years — was first put to the parties in writing on 10 September. Despite lobbying from an Executive that has been operating without an approved budget since the financial year began in April, the Treasury has not moved the figure since.

What's on the table

The package breaks down as £450 million in resource funding for 2026/27, falling to £400 million in each of the following two years. On top of that sits £50 million a year for public-service transformation — money the government wants Stormont departments to match pound-for-pound — plus £50 million in capital funding for social housing in the final two years of the deal. The Executive would also keep half of whatever it recovers through welfare fraud and error crackdowns.

It is, notably, an improved figure from where talks began: an initial approach earlier in September had put roughly £1.1 billion over three years to the parties, which Executive ministers judged insufficient. The increase to £1.5 billion was the government's answer. Bryant's message this week was that it is also the government's final one.

"It represents more than £1.5 billion extra funding at a time when nearly all other government departments are being required to make significant savings," Bryant told the party leaders. "I cannot emphasise enough how tight the financial situation is and the pressure on the public finances."

He urged them to "get around the table and work out with us how we can put together a balanced budget as a matter of urgency based on these figures," and is understood to be expecting responses within the coming week.

A structural squeeze, not a negotiating tactic

Stormont's departments have been running on continuity funding — able to spend up to 95% of the previous year's allocations — since April, unable to pass a proper budget without knowing how much Westminster support is coming. Executive ministers have argued that years of underfunding relative to need leave Northern Ireland unable to balance its books on devolved revenue alone.

Bryant's flat refusal to add a penny more reflects the wider fiscal position facing Whitehall under Chancellor pressure to hold departmental spending down across the board, not any particular hardening toward Belfast. But it does narrow the Executive's options: accept the figures and build a budget around them, or continue into a funding vacuum that risks direct Treasury intervention if the parties cannot agree among themselves.

The Democratic Unionist Party has signalled that a cash injection alone will not resolve the pressures on Northern Ireland's public services, while Sinn Féin has pointed to what it calls a long history of British underfunding as the deeper cause of the impasse. Neither has rejected the £1.5 billion outright.

Party leaders are expected to give the Northern Ireland Office their responses within days. If no budget emerges, the choice facing Westminster narrows sharply: impose one through civil servants, or watch the devolved institutions drift further into paralysis.
