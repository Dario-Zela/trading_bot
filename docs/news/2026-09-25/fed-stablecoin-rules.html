Fed opens comment period on stablecoin reserve and capital rules under GENIUS Act

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C., headquarters of the Federal Reserve Board of Governors. — AgnosticPreachersKid / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
The Fed opened a 60-day comment period on GENIUS Act rules requiring full reserves and tiered capital charges for the stablecoins its supervised banks issue.

The Federal Reserve Board opened a 60-day public comment window on Thursday for two proposed rules that would, for the first time, set binding reserve and capital standards for the stablecoins issued by the banks it supervises — the central bank's first concrete step toward implementing the GENIUS Act, the stablecoin law President Trump signed in July 2025.

The first proposal requires Board-supervised payment stablecoin issuers to back their tokens fully with short-term Treasury bills and other high-quality liquid assets, on a strict one-for-one basis. Layered on top is a standardized capital charge for credit and operational risk: 2% of an issuer's first $20 billion in stablecoins outstanding, 1.5% on the next $30 billion, and 1% on anything above $50 billion — a sliding scale that eases as scale increases. A companion proposal sets out a tailored application process for banks that want to issue stablecoins, plus formal procedures for appeals and hearings when the Fed turns an applicant down.

Why now

The GENIUS Act gave regulators an 18-month runway from enactment — pointing to a January 2027 deadline — or 120 days after final rules are issued, whichever comes first. The Fed's proposals, released alongside a related FDIC framework for state and insured-depository issuers earlier this year, are the mechanism by which that statute actually bites: without reserve and capital rules on the books, banks have no clear path to issuing stablecoins under Fed supervision at all.

The reserve requirement mirrors what market leaders already claim to do voluntarily. The capital charge is new. It effectively prices the operational risk of running stablecoin infrastructure — custody failures, smart-contract bugs, redemption runs — the same way the Fed already prices credit risk into bank capital ratios.

The concentration question

The stablecoin market the rules would govern is already lopsided. Tether's USDT and Circle's USDC together account for roughly 83% of a stablecoin market worth around $310–320 billion, with USDT alone near 59% share. A capital regime scaled to reward size — cheaper per-dollar capital costs as issuance climbs past $50 billion — does little to invite challengers into that arrangement, and industry critics argue the compliance overhead of GENIUS Act supervision (AML programs, Bank Secrecy Act reporting, ongoing audits) will be easier for large, well-capitalized banks to absorb than for smaller fintechs or crypto-native issuers trying to get a Fed-supervised charter off the ground.

That tension has surfaced inside the Fed itself. Governor Michael Barr flagged concerns that the GENIUS Act's "significant or systemic" threshold for triggering stricter oversight leaves the Board without a clear basis for verifying that an issuer's compliance programs actually hold up. Vice Chair for Supervision Michelle Bowman has pushed the opposite instinct — arguing for capital and diversification standards tough enough to keep large banks and crypto-native issuers on a level footing, rather than rules so light they invite a race to the bottom on reserve quality.

What's next

Comments are due 60 days after the proposals are published in the Federal Register, putting a final rule plausibly on the calendar for early-to-mid 2027 — tight against the GENIUS Act's own January 2027 effective date. Banks weighing whether to seek Fed approval to issue stablecoins, and the fintechs hoping to compete with Tether and Circle for institutional flow, will be watching whether the final capital tiers narrow or widen the gap between what a $60 billion issuer pays per dollar and what a $2 billion challenger does.
