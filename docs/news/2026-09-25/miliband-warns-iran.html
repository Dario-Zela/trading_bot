Miliband warns Iran's minister UK will not tolerate 'hostile activity' on British soil

Ed Miliband, who became UK Foreign Secretary in July 2026, warned Iran's foreign minister against hostile activity on British soil. — Shaun Curry / DESNZ, UK Government (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
Ed Miliband warned Iran's foreign minister the UK won't tolerate hostile activity on British soil, but announced no sanctions or concrete response.

Britain's foreign secretary looked across a table at his Iranian counterpart in New York on Thursday and delivered a warning dressed up as diplomacy. Ed Miliband told Iran's foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, that the UK "will not tolerate intimidation, threats or hostile activity on UK soil" — a formulation vague enough to cover everything from cyber-espionage to arson, and specific about none of it.

The meeting, held on the sidelines of the UN General Assembly, was the first substantive face-to-face between the two governments since Miliband moved from the energy brief to the Foreign Office in July, when Prime Minister Andy Burnham reshuffled his cabinet. It is a role in which Miliband, who is Jewish, has made confronting Iran-linked threats to Britain's Jewish community an early priority.

What's actually behind the warning

The Foreign Office readout points to two concrete threads, even if the public statement doesn't name them. The first is the arson attack in March on four ambulances belonging to Hatzola, a Jewish volunteer emergency service, set alight in a synagogue car park in Golders Green, north London. British prosecutors have since charged three men, one with dual British-Pakistani nationality, and counter-terrorism police are examining a claim of responsibility — not yet verified — from a group calling itself Harakat Ashab al-Yamin al-Islamia, posted to Telegram alongside surveillance footage of the ambulances' location.

The second is cyber. Earlier this month Britain's National Cyber Security Centre joined the FBI and Dutch intelligence in publicly attributing a spyware strain called CHOSEN BRICK to Iran's Ministry of Intelligence and Security. The malware, deployed through fake rapport-building conversations on WhatsApp and Telegram, harvests emails, messages, screenshots and microphone audio from journalists, dissidents and activists — with stolen material sometimes surfacing on pro-Iranian leak sites. Neither incident was mentioned by name in Thursday's public statement, which is itself part of the story: London is content to let the threat stay abstract in public while pursuing it concretely through prosecutors and cyber-attribution channels.

The rest of the agenda

Miliband also raised the case of Craig and Lindsay Foreman, the East Sussex couple detained since January 2025 while travelling through Iran by motorcycle and now held in Tehran's Evin prison, repeating Britain's call for their release. And he pressed Araghchi on the Strait of Hormuz, where shipping disruption tied to the wider Iran conflict continues to weigh on insurance costs and freight routing months after fighting first flared. Miliband urged "de-escalation and diplomacy instead of prolonging the war." Araghchi's side offered no public rebuttal or concession on any of it.

Why the vagueness matters

What the meeting did not produce is any signal of consequence. There was no announcement of sanctions, no expulsion of diplomats, no move against the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps' presence in Britain — options that have circulated in Westminster for months and remain unused. A foreign secretary telling a hostile government to behave is not, on its own, a policy shift; it's the standard grammar of diplomatic irritation, delivered at a forum built for exactly this kind of exchange. For anyone trying to price UK-Iran relations, the meeting confirms that London is watching closely and building a paper trail — useful if sanctions or prosecutions follow — but it does not by itself move the needle on Hormuz risk, UK-Iran trade exposure, or the safety calculus for Iran-linked assets in Britain.

The more telling test comes next: whether the Golders Green prosecution surfaces an evidenced Iranian state link at trial, and whether the CHOSEN BRICK attribution is followed by sanctions designations rather than just a joint advisory. Until one of those lands, Thursday's warning is a marker laid down, not a policy enacted.
