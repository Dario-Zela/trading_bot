France says EU 'Made in Europe' law should not extend to the UK

French Industry Minister Sébastien Martin, who told fellow EU ministers that Britain's departure from the bloc means it should not share in 'Made in Europe' procurement protections. — European Union / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Paris told the EU that Britain should not get preferential access under the bloc's planned 'Made in Europe' procurement law, deepening a UK-EU trade fault line.

France has told fellow EU governments that Britain should not be covered by the bloc's planned "Made in Europe" procurement rules, hardening a dividing line that has become one of the thorniest issues in the run-up to November's UK-EU reset summit.

The policy at stake is the Industrial Accelerator Act, presented by the European Commission in March and designed to steer public money — subsidies, infrastructure contracts, government purchasing — toward manufacturers based in the bloc. The goal, officials say, is to lift EU manufacturing back to 20% of GDP by 2035, up from 14.3% last year, and to blunt what Brussels calls unfair state-subsidised competition from China in sectors such as steel, batteries, electric vehicles and net-zero technology.

At a meeting of EU industry ministers in Brussels last week, France pushed for a narrow reading of "European" that would exclude non-EU suppliers by default, starting with the UK. "European taxpayers' money should support European workers and European factories," French Industry Minister Sébastien Martin told the gathering. On Britain specifically, he was blunter still: the country "made a choice a few years ago to leave the European Union. That doesn't mean that we don't discuss things or that we don't trade" — but, in his view, that doesn't buy preferential access either.

France's Europe minister, Benjamin Haddad, made the underlying logic explicit: "At the end of the day, if you are not part of the internal market and if you are not contributing to the financial capacity of the internal market, you don't get the same kind of protection."

A split among the 27

France is not alone — Greece and Spain have also argued against extending preferential treatment too far beyond the EU27 — but the bloc is far from united. Germany, Luxembourg and Sweden favour a more open approach that would keep close trading partners like the UK inside the tent. Sweden's Ebba Busch put it more plainly: "we always miss the Brits." A separate bloc of nine member states, including Czechia, Ireland, Finland and Sweden, has warned that overly tight local-content rules risk damaging competition, prices and quality across the single market.

Under the current draft text, the UK would actually qualify for equivalent treatment by virtue of its trade and cooperation agreement with the EU — but the Commission has reserved discretion to strike partners from the list if they don't offer reciprocal market access, and officials involved in the talks expect the final list of protected countries to be "considerably shorter" than it is now. Most of the law's substantive rules would not bite until 2029, but EU officials are aiming to have it adopted before 2027, meaning the lobbying now under way will likely decide Britain's fate under the law.

Why Downing Street is worried

For Prime Minister Andy Burnham, resolving the question has become one of his top priorities in the EU relationship. Pressing Brussels last week, he argued for what he called a "trusted partner" status: "Let's work towards a trusted partner approach that doesn't cause a problem where there wasn't a problem and where we have common cause."

The stakes are concrete. UK-EU trade in cars and components alone is worth roughly €80bn a year, and supply chains run in both directions — components made in Britain go into cars assembled on the continent, and vice versa. The Society of Motor Manufacturers and Traders has warned that exclusion would put the UK automotive industry "in jeopardy" while also damaging EU carmakers that depend on British-made parts.

What's next

The Industrial Accelerator Act is still working through Parliament, with MEPs Christophe Grudler, Pierre Jouvet and Anna Cavazzini steering the relevant committees, and adoption targeted before 2027. Downing Street will use November's UK-EU summit to press its case for "trusted partner" status directly with EU leaders; whether France's narrower definition or Germany's more open one prevails will determine whether this becomes a genuine new barrier for UK exporters or a resolved footnote in the post-Brexit relationship. Either way, the fact that the argument is happening at all — a decade after the UK left the bloc — is itself a sign of how unsettled that relationship remains.
