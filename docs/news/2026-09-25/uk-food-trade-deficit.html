UK food and drink trade deficit hits £21.1bn, largest since 2000

The ferry terminal at Dover's Eastern Docks, the main crossing point for UK-EU food and drink trade now subject to post-Brexit border checks. — DeFacto / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
UK food and drink exports fell 11.7% in H1 2026 as Brexit border costs and an Iran-war-driven Middle East slump pushed the trade deficit to £21.1bn, the widest since 2000.

Britain's food and drink industry is buying far more from the world than it is selling to it, and the gap is now the widest it has been since the turn of the century. New figures from the Food & Drink Federation put the sector's trade deficit at £21.1bn for 2026, a number that has been climbing for years but has lately started climbing faster — pulled in different directions by a hardening EU border and a Middle East export market that has largely stopped buying.

The numbers behind the gap

Export volumes for the first half of 2026 came to roughly four billion kilograms, according to the FDF's Trade Snapshot, based on HMRC customs data — a fall of 11.7% on the same period last year and the third-lowest half-year figure since 2000. The federation notes, pointedly, that the only weaker stretches on record were the depths of the Covid-19 pandemic and the aftermath of the 2001 foot-and-mouth outbreak, when export markets were closed by disease control rather than economics. In value terms exports held up a little better — £12.4bn, down 3.4% year-on-year — but that gap between the volume and value declines points to a sector selling less abroad and getting paid more per unit for what does move, a sign of costlier logistics being passed through rather than genuine pricing power.

Imports went the other way. At 19.1 billion kilograms, up 3% on the year, they were the second-highest on record. Put the two trends together and the deficit — imports minus exports — reached £21.1bn, according to the FDF, eclipsing anything seen this century.

"Our food and drink trade deficit is growing and is now the largest it's been in over 25 years," said Karen Betts, the federation's chief executive and a former UK trade diplomat who ran the Scotch Whisky Association through the first years of Brexit disruption. Tom Bradshaw, president of the National Farmers' Union, framed the stakes more starkly: "Food security is national security."

Two shocks, not one cycle

What distinguishes this deficit from the UK's periodic trade wobbles is that it is not a single, reversible shock but two structural ones arriving together.

The first is the EU border. Ireland, still the UK's largest single food and drink export market, saw shipments fall 4.9% by value; France, the second-largest, fell 4.6%. Overall EU export value was down 0.9%. None of these are collapse-scale numbers on their own, but the FDF's language is telling — it describes the pattern as "ongoing border friction," not a one-off disruption. Five and a half years after full post-Brexit customs controls began phasing in, the additional paperwork, health certification and consignment-level checks required for animal and plant products continue to add cost and delay that a growing share of smaller exporters appear to be deciding is not worth absorbing.

The second shock is geographically distant but has hit harder in percentage terms. Non-EU export value fell 6.9%, driven largely by the war in Iran and its knock-on effects across Gulf shipping and trade routes. Exports to the United Arab Emirates, a significant regional hub for UK food and drink re-exports, dropped 23.4%. A separate and unrelated pressure — the 10% US tariff imposed on UK goods — knocked exports to America down 16.5%, adding a third drag that has nothing to do with Brexit or the Gulf at all.

Why the deficit may not simply correct itself

Trade deficits usually widen and narrow with exchange rates and demand cycles — a weaker pound makes exports more competitive, a recovering trading partner buys more, and the gap closes on its own over a few quarters. The FDF's data suggests this deficit is different in kind. Regulatory divergence from the EU is a policy choice with no near-term reversal in sight, and the Iran conflict's effect on Gulf trade routes is tied to a war whose endpoint remains unresolved, even as US and Iranian officials continue to discuss a phased reopening of Hormuz shipping lanes. Neither driver responds to the kind of monetary or currency adjustment that typically narrows a trade gap.

For sterling, a persistently wide deficit in a staple sector adds to an already crowded list of structural headwinds — a large fiscal deficit, sticky services inflation and a slowing economy — none of which point toward near-term currency strength. For the food industry itself, the FDF's own framing is that this is now a competitiveness problem as much as a cyclical one: British producers losing share in markets they have supplied for decades, replaced not necessarily by cheaper rivals but by the accumulated cost of distance from both of the UK's most important trading relationships.

The next FDF trade snapshot, covering the third quarter, will show whether the UAE and US declines are stabilising or deepening — and whether any progress on Hormuz shipping security is enough to show up in the numbers before year-end.
