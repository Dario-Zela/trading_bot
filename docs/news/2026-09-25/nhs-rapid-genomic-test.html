NHS pilots rapid genomic test cutting brain tumour diagnosis from eight weeks to two hours

Queen's Medical Centre, Nottingham — where surgeons trialled real-time genomic diagnosis of brain tumours during operations. — Alan Murray-Rust / Geograph, CC BY-SA 2.0
In one sentence
An NHS pilot cut brain tumour diagnosis from up to eight weeks to two hours using portable gene sequencing — but the 100% accuracy figure comes from just 50 cases at one hospital.

Steve Palmer was still on the operating table at Queen's Medical Centre in Nottingham when his surgeon got the answer. Twenty minutes after a fragment of his tumour went into a machine not much bigger than a shoebox, the genomic signature came back: glioblastoma, grade 4. Under the old pathway, that answer would have taken six to eight weeks — biopsy, fixation, staining, a pathologist's eye, then a battery of separate molecular tests sent off to specialist labs. Palmer, 55, had it while he was still under anaesthetic.

"Getting that quick diagnosis removed weeks of anxiety," he said afterwards. "It wasn't the result I wanted to hear, but it means I can get on with the next phase."[^1]

His case is the leading edge of an NHS England pilot now running across five specialist centres — Nottingham University Hospitals, University Hospitals Birmingham, Great Ormond Street Hospital, King's College Hospital and Newcastle Hospitals — with expansion planned to Bristol, Oxford, Leeds and Manchester.[^1] The technology, developed by the University of Nottingham and Nottingham University Hospitals NHS Trust, pairs a portable Oxford Nanopore sequencer with locally built classification software to read a tumour's genetic and epigenetic fingerprint in the time it takes to finish an operation, rather than the weeks it takes to finish a lab queue.

How a shoebox replaces six weeks of lab work

Conventional brain tumour diagnosis is a relay race across departments. A sample goes to histopathology for microscopy, then typically to four or five separate molecular tests — looking for specific mutations, methylation patterns, chromosomal changes — each run in a different lab, often at a different hospital, with results arriving piecemeal over weeks.

Nanopore sequencing collapses that into one pass. DNA strands are threaded through microscopic pores in a membrane; as each strand passes through, it disrupts an electrical current in a pattern specific to its sequence and methylation state, which software translates into a readout. Because the device is portable and the analysis runs on standard computing hardware, the whole loop — sample preparation, sequencing, classification — happens inside the hospital rather than being couriered elsewhere.

"The diagnosis of brain tumours relies on molecular and genetic analysis, but it can take up to six to eight weeks," said Stuart Smith, the consultant neurosurgeon who operated on Palmer. "Now, with this new technology, we can do more for patients because we can get answers so much more quickly, which will have a much bigger influence on clinical decision-making, in as little as two hours."[^2]

Dr Simon Paine, a consultant neuropathologist at Nottingham University Hospitals who has spent a career reading tumour samples under a microscope, put the contrast more starkly: "I'm crystal ball gazing, and I can do it reasonably well on a good day, but nowhere near with the clarity" of genomic classification.[^1] The underlying research — published on medRxiv as the ROBIN assay, combining real-time intraoperative methylome classification with next-day comprehensive profiling — reported that across 50 brain tumour surgeries at Nottingham, the method returned a usable classification within two hours in every case, with fuller molecular profiling completed within 24 hours.[^3] Professor Matt Loose, the University of Nottingham biologist who helped build the classification pipeline, put the running cost at roughly £450 per patient, and said it could fall further at scale because it replaces four or five separate tests with one.[^2]

NHS England's clinical leadership has framed the pilot as a systemic fix, not just a Nottingham curiosity. "For people with suspected brain tumours, getting the right diagnosis quickly can feel like a race," said Professor Frankie Swords, NHS medical director for cancer. Professor Dame Sue Hill, NHS England's chief scientific officer, said rapid genomic testing "could dramatically shorten the wait for answers" for a disease that remains the leading cancer killer of people under 40 in the UK, with roughly 12,000 new primary brain tumour diagnoses in England each year and five-year survival for brain cancer specifically — 12.9% — far below the roughly 56% average across all cancers.[^1][^4]

What "100% accuracy" actually means here

The headline figure — 100% diagnostic accuracy in the Nottingham cohort — deserves a footnote of its own. It describes concordance across 50 surgical cases at a single, highly practised centre: the team that built the classifier testing it on their own patients, with an experienced neuropathology service available to adjudicate discordant results. That is a meaningful proof of concept, not an independent validation. Separate peer-reviewed work on nanopore methylation classifiers — including a MethyLYZR-based approach benchmarked elsewhere — has reported accuracy closer to 94.5% within 15 minutes, using far sparser data.[^5] Sample sizes across this literature are still small by clinical-trial standards, and none of the published figures yet reflect performance averaged across five NHS trusts with varying case mix, equipment, and staff experience — which is precisely what the current pilot exists to test.

That distinction matters because the harder problem in UK brain tumour genomics has never really been the sequencing chemistry — it has been getting any molecular testing to every patient, consistently. A 2023 study surveying UK neuro-oncology centres found that not one had sufficient capacity to offer whole genome sequencing to all its neurological tumour patients in a timely manner through NHS-accredited pathways.[^6] The bottlenecks were mundane and structural: inadequate tumour tissue, delays in sample transport, and — most persistently — too few clinical scientists to interpret the results that come out the other end. Access to basic tests such as methylation arrays already varies by region, with a small number of overloaded reference labs creating backlogs even before a faster front-end test enters the picture.

The gap between a pilot and a national service

A sequencer that returns an answer in two hours is only as useful as the pathway around it. Bringing intraoperative genomic classification to five centres means training neuropathologists and lab staff to run and interpret an unfamiliar assay, embedding bioinformatics support inside surgical workflows, and building quality-assurance processes robust enough to satisfy UK accreditation standards — all before the NHS can extend it to the dozens of trusts that see brain tumour patients but are not part of the pilot. NHS England has not published a costed timeline for national rollout, and the £450-per-test estimate, while promising, is a research-lab figure rather than an audited NHS tariff.

What the pilot has already changed is the shape of a single operation. For patients like Palmer, a genomic answer delivered mid-surgery lets a neurosurgeon decide in real time whether to pursue an aggressive resection of a curable mass or a more conservative approach around an aggressive, infiltrative tumour — a decision that, under the old timeline, had to be made on a working guess and revisited weeks later once the lab results arrived.

The next test of the technology's staying power will be whether the four expansion sites — Bristol, Oxford, Leeds and Manchester — can reproduce Nottingham's numbers with different staff, different case mix and different lab infrastructure, and whether NHS England is willing to publish that comparison rather than the single-centre figures the pilot has generated so far.
