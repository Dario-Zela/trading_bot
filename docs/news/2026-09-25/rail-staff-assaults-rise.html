BEYOND THE TAPE · RAIL · By L. Bordeaux · 2026-09-25 · 3 min read

Reported assaults on Britain's rail staff rise by more than a third

A British Transport Police station at Paisley Gilmour Street railway station. — Thomas Nugent / Geograph, CC BY-SA 2.0
In one sentence
Assaults on Britain's rail passengers jumped 36% and on staff 12% in the past year, but no cost or operational data has been published to size the impact on operators.

The platform announcement most rail passengers dread these days isn't about delays. It's the one that asks everyone to remain calm while police deal with an incident further up the train. New figures from the Office of Rail and Road suggest that scene is becoming more common, not less.

Between April 2025 and March 2026, Britain's mainline railway recorded 13,464 reported assaults against passengers and members of the public — up 36% on the year before, and the highest total since the ORR's data series began in 2004. Harassment complaints rose 52%, common assaults 30%. Assaults specifically against rail staff — the guards, ticket inspectors and station teams who deal with the public all day — rose a more modest but still striking 12%, to 11,289 incidents. Of those, 2,487 were physical; the rest verbal abuse and threats. Add it up across the network and Great Britain's railways logged close to 25,000 reported assaults in a single year.

Two-thirds report violence

The RMT union, which represents guards and station staff, says the ORR numbers understate the problem. Its own survey found two-thirds of members had experienced some form of violence at work in the past year. General secretary Eddie Dempsey called the figures "horrendous" and said they came despite the union's Action Against Assaults campaign, launched last year specifically to drive the numbers down. His demands are familiar ones: an end to lone working on trains and stations, tougher legal protections for staff who are assaulted on the job, and bringing outsourced security and enforcement staff in-house. He also pointed to cuts in British Transport Police funding, which he said have left the force unable to maintain a proper policing presence across the network.

Richard Hines, the ORR's chief inspector of railways, was more measured but no less concerned: "No-one on the railway should be subjected to violence, threats or abuse." The regulator is pushing operators to map where incidents cluster, tighten controls at those locations, make it easier for staff to report abuse, and back up workers who do.

What's missing from the picture

What the ORR's release does not contain is any costing. There is no estimate of lost staff hours, insurance claims, sick leave or recruitment strain tied to the rise — the kind of figures that would let an investor weigh whether this is a line-item risk for operators like Avanti West Coast, LNER or the newly renationalised Great British Railways, or simply a grim social trend playing out on public transport. Lone working reductions and expanded body-worn camera rollouts, both under discussion, would carry real costs, but none have been quantified publicly.

That gap is likely to persist until the next reporting cycle. The ORR publishes these statistics annually, meaning the first read on whether 2026's staffing and policing debates have moved the numbers won't arrive until autumn 2027. Until then, the more immediate marker to watch is whether Great British Railways, in its first full year running services, attaches any operational spending to staff safety in its upcoming business plan.
