Four civilians killed in Pakistani strikes in Afghanistan, Taliban says

Zabihullah Mujahid, the Taliban government's chief spokesman, whose statements are the primary source for Thursday's civilian casualty claim — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The Taliban says Pakistani strikes killed four Afghan civilians, the latest single-source claim in a rivalry that turned to open war in February.

Afghanistan's Taliban government says four civilians were killed on Thursday when Pakistani jets struck ten locations across three Afghan provinces — the latest exchange in a rivalry between the two neighbours that has moved well past the occasional border skirmish and into something closer to open, if intermittent, war.

The strikes hit sites in Kandahar, Paktia and Khost, according to Zabihullah Mujahid, the Taliban administration's chief spokesman, who said Pakistani aircraft had targeted what Islamabad described as drone storage and launch facilities. A resident of Kandahar city told reporters he heard a jet overhead followed by a large explosion. Pakistan's information minister, Attaullah Tarar, called the operation "deliberate, precise, calibrated, and proportionate," saying it came in response to a drone incursion from Afghan territory a day earlier that Pakistani air defences said they detected and neutralised before it reached its target.

The Taliban administration denies that any drones were launched from its soil and rejects Islamabad's broader claim that it shelters the Pakistani Taliban, known as the TTP, whose cross-border attacks have killed dozens of Pakistani security personnel this year. Kabul summoned Pakistan's chargé d'affaires and lodged a formal protest over what it called a violation of Afghan airspace. None of the casualty figures — from either side — have been independently verified; the BBC's reporting, like most outlets covering the strikes, relies on Taliban statements rather than witnesses on the ground or Pakistani confirmation of civilian deaths.

A conflict past the point of isolated incidents

What makes Thursday's claim harder to read as a one-off is the calendar behind it. Pakistan and Afghanistan fought their worst clashes in years in February, after Pakistan's defence minister, Khawaja Asif, declared the two countries in "open war" following retaliatory Taliban attacks on Pakistani border posts. Only three days before this week's strikes, on September 21, the United Nations said a separate round of Pakistani airstrikes in Paktika, Paktya and Kunar provinces had killed at least 28 civilians — a toll roughly seven times higher than Thursday's, and one that drew a rare independent count rather than a single-source claim. Pakistan, for its part, said an earlier round of strikes this week killed 28 people it described as militants, not civilians, underscoring how thoroughly the two governments' narratives diverge.

The proximate trigger for the latest round was an attack on a police facility in northwestern Pakistan that killed at least 21 people, which Islamabad blames on TTP fighters operating from Afghan sanctuary. Afghanistan disputes the charge and frames Pakistan's air campaign as aggression against a sovereign, if unrecognised, border — the colonial-era Durand Line that Kabul has never formally accepted.

What to watch

China, Turkey, Qatar and Saudi Arabia have all offered to mediate, but no ceasefire framework has held since February's escalation. With Kabul vowing an "appropriate response" to Thursday's strikes and Islamabad showing no sign of pausing its campaign against suspected TTP infrastructure, the immediate marker to watch is whether Afghan forces retaliate against Pakistani border posts, as they did in February — the move that turned isolated strikes into the sustained conflict now defining the relationship.
