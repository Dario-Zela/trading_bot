Pope Leo XIV begins first France visit with Notre-Dame vespers, huge crowds expected in Paris

Notre-Dame Cathedral in Paris, where Pope Leo XIV presided over solemn vespers on 25 September. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Pope Leo XIV opened his first France visit with Notre-Dame vespers and an 80,000-strong youth vigil, the first papal Paris visit since 2008.

Pope Leo XIV opened his first visit to France on Thursday evening with solemn vespers inside Notre-Dame Cathedral, the first time in eighteen years that a pope has set foot in Paris — and the first papal service in the cathedral since its reopening in December 2024, following the 2019 fire that nearly destroyed it.

The pontiff presided over the ceremony at 5:15pm alongside French bishops, priests, deacons and seminarians, before an evening that was as much a logistical operation as a religious one. Notre-Dame was closed to the public from Wednesday through Friday to accommodate the service, and central Paris rerouted traffic around a popemobile procession that carried Leo XIV between Notre-Dame-des-Champs and Place Saint-Michel.

A packed opening day

Earlier on Friday, Leo XIV met President Emmanuel Macron at the Élysée Palace and addressed UNESCO headquarters on artificial intelligence, education and peace — a rare instance of a pope speaking directly to the Paris-based UN cultural agency. The day closed with a vigil for young people at the Stade de France, where organisers expected around 80,000 attendees aged 17 to 35, filling the stadium that has hosted World Cup finals and rock concerts rather than papal Masses.

The four-day itinerary, which continues to Lourdes and Metz, is Leo XIV's first as pontiff to France and only the second papal trip to the country this century, after Benedict XVI's 2008 visit that drew roughly 250,000 people to an open-air Mass at Les Invalides. Organisers are bracing for larger numbers this time: an open-air Mass planned for Saturday at Place de la Concorde has drawn comparisons to that scale, with some estimates putting potential attendance across the weekend in the hundreds of thousands.

What's actually at stake

Beneath the pageantry sits one genuine point of tension. On Sunday in Lourdes, Leo XIV is due to meet privately with seven survivors of clergy sexual abuse — a group selected by French bishops rather than by victims' advocacy organisations, a choice that has already drawn criticism from campaigners who argue the church is managing the encounter rather than confronting it. France's independent reparations authority has logged nearly 2,000 abuse claims over the past five years, and how the Vatican handles that meeting will likely outlast any memory of Friday's crowds.

For now, though, the visit is unfolding largely as scripted: a cathedral service, a stadium vigil, a head-of-state meeting, all tightly choreographed and short on surprises. The next test of that script comes Saturday at Place de la Concorde, and then Sunday in Lourdes, where the closed-door meeting with survivors is the one item on the itinerary that isn't fully under the Vatican's control.
