IRAN · TANKER WAR · By C. Tanaka · 2026-09-07 · 6 min read

US strikes three Iranian tankers as 'tanker war' escalates, Brent nears $97

A US Navy carrier strike group in Gulf waters; carrier groups have been the backbone of Washington's Hormuz posture since the February air war on Iran. — Lt. Juan D. Guerra / US Navy, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
US forces struck three Iranian tankers under a new 'tanker-for-tanker' policy after Iranian missiles targeted US warships, sending Brent crude to $97.

The tanker war that has stalked the Persian Gulf for months turned openly kinetic over the weekend. On Saturday, US Central Command said its forces had struck three Iranian crude carriers — permanently disabling two, the M/T Downy off Kharg Island and the M/T Stark 1 near Jask, and destroying a third, the unladen M/T Kylo, in the Gulf of Oman. The strikes came hours after Iran's Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) fired ballistic missiles at a US aircraft carrier and a guided-missile destroyer patrolling near the Strait of Hormuz. CENTCOM said both warships evaded the attack; no American casualties were reported.

What makes this different from the strikes, seizures and mine-laying that have punctuated the Gulf since February is the policy behind it. The Trump administration has now formalised a "tanker-for-tanker" doctrine: an Iranian attack on shipping or on US forces in the strait will draw a direct US strike on an Iranian tanker in return. It is a shift from deterrence-by-presence — carrier groups sailing the Gulf as a warning — to deterrence-by-retaliation, with commercial vessels as the currency of exchange.

From air war to sea war

Context matters here, because the tanker strikes are not the opening move of a new crisis but the latest phase of one that has run for more than six months. On 28 February, the United States and Israel launched a joint air campaign against Iran aimed at its nuclear and missile programmes; the strikes killed Iran's supreme leader, Ali Khamenei. Tehran's response was to turn the Strait of Hormuz — the channel through which roughly 20% of the world's traded petroleum and a similar share of liquefied natural gas passes — into a contested zone, boarding merchant ships and laying mines. By some estimates, crude-carrying traffic through the strait fell more than 95% at the campaign's peak. A Pakistan-brokered ceasefire and a June memorandum briefly cooled the fighting, but both sides have accused each other of violations ever since, and the maritime front has now reignited into direct ship-on-ship and ship-on-warship attacks.

Iran's retaliation for the weekend strikes was immediate. The IRGC said it hit three oil tankers and three US-linked vessels transiting the strait, and Tehran's top security official, Mohsen Rezaee, said Iran would soon declare a "restricted" or "exclusion" zone running from the US Navy's blockade line, through the strait and into the Persian Gulf — warning that any vessel entering it would be added to Iran's sanctions list. Iran's parliament speaker and chief negotiator, Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, went further, declaring that the era of "proportionate responses" was over: future retaliation, he said, would be "faster, heavier and more painful." He also acknowledged, unusually, the domestic cost of the standoff — inflation, unemployment and currency volatility mounting as Iran's oil trade remains strangled.

Crude follows the missiles

Oil markets have been pricing the risk in real time. Brent crude touched $97.39 a barrel on Sunday, up more than 11% over the past month and some 47% higher than a year ago — a climb that began in earnest after the February air war but has steepened sharply with each round of tanker strikes. Traders reached for comparisons to April, when Brent briefly topped $102 as the US assembled a three-carrier blockade force in the Gulf, and to the near-50% monthly gain logged earlier in the conflict. The pattern in the futures curve suggests something more specific than a generic risk premium: markets are not treating this as a shock that fades once the news cycle turns, but as a structural repricing of Gulf transit risk that persists for as long as tankers are being struck. Vice President JD Vance has added to that pricing pressure by saying Washington will not resume talks with Tehran until Iran stops attacking shipping in the strait — removing, for now, the off-ramp traders might otherwise price into the curve.

The mechanics of that repricing run through insurance as much as through the futures market. War-risk premiums for hull and cargo cover in the Gulf have followed the conflict's intensity all year, and a reversion to direct strikes on named, identifiable vessels — rather than mines or boardings, which are harder to attribute — tends to push those premiums up further because underwriters can now point to a specific, repeatable US and Iranian tactic rather than an ambient risk. Shipowners routing around the strait, where that is even possible, add days and fuel costs that eventually surface in freight rates and, downstream, at the pump.

The gap between the war room and the podium

The most jarring note came not from the Gulf but from the White House. President Trump, asked about the conflict on Friday, called it "small potatoes" for the United States and claimed "we took over Venezuela and we have essentially taken over Iran" — remarks made as his administration works to keep the war from becoming a midterm liability. Pressed on the toll — 18 American service members have died since February — an irate Trump compared the campaign to the multi-year US engagements in Vietnam and Afghanistan, an odd rebuttal from a president simultaneously insisting the conflict is minor and nearly won.

That framing sits awkwardly against the facts on the water: Iranian missiles fired at a US carrier group, three tankers struck by each side within 24 hours, and a foreign parliament speaker publicly discarding restraint. Iran's economy is visibly straining under the standoff, which gives Washington a case that pressure is working. But an adversary that says its next response will be "faster, heavier and more painful" is not one signalling retreat, and a policy built on tit-for-tat strikes on named ships is, definitionally, escalatory — each exchange raises the number of tankers that both sides are willing to put at risk.

What happens next

The near-term signal to watch is whether Iran actually implements the exclusion zone Rezaee described; a declared zone that foreign-flagged tankers treat as real would do more to choke Gulf traffic than the strikes themselves, since insurers and shipowners tend to act on declared no-go areas rather than wait to see if they are enforced. Beyond that, the tanker-for-tanker policy will be tested by its own logic: it only holds as a deterrent if Washington keeps striking every time Iran does, which means the next dated data point is not a scheduled announcement but whichever side moves first. Brent's path from here will likely track that exchange more closely than any OPEC meeting or US inventory report.
