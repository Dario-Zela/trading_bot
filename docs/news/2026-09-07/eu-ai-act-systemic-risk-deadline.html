Frontier AI labs face first EU AI Act systemic-risk deadline

The Berlaymont building in Brussels, headquarters of the European Commission, which houses the AI Office overseeing the September 15 deadline — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Frontier AI labs above the EU's 10^25 FLOPs threshold must submit systemic-risk evaluations to Brussels by September 15 — the AI Act's first real enforcement test.

For the past year, the European Union's AI Act has mostly been a paperwork exercise — codes of practice to sign, templates to fill in, dialogues with Brussels officials that carried no real teeth. That changes on September 15. By that date, every company whose AI model was trained using more than 10^25 floating-point operations — a threshold that currently catches somewhere between a dozen and fifteen systems worldwide, including OpenAI's frontier models, Google's Gemini line, Anthropic's Claude and Meta's Llama 3 405B — must hand the EU's AI Office a formal, written evaluation of the risks their model poses to society.

It is a narrow provision on paper. In practice, it is the first moment the AI Act stops being a compliance checklist and starts being a regulator asking frontier labs to show their homework, with fines attached if they don't.

What Brussels is actually asking for

The obligation flows from Article 55 of the Act, which applies specifically to "general-purpose AI models with systemic risk" — the Act's term for models powerful enough that their failure modes could ripple beyond their immediate users. The 10^25 FLOPs figure is a rough proxy for that power: it is roughly the compute budget used to train GPT-4-class systems, and the Commission treats crossing it as an automatic presumption of systemic risk, rebuttable only if a provider can show otherwise.

Companies above the line must submit documentation covering three things, according to guidance from the AI Office: their red-teaming methodology (how they stress-test models for dangerous capabilities before release), disclosures of the energy consumed in training, and compliance with a standardised copyright-training-data summary template the Commission published in July. Providers must also have already notified Brussels within two weeks of crossing the compute threshold — a separate, quieter obligation most large labs have apparently already met through what the Commission calls "technical compliance dialogues."

This is the follow-through on a broader shift that began on August 2, when the Commission's AI Office formally started enforcing the Act's transparency rules — chatbots disclosing they're not human, AI-generated content carrying machine-readable marks, deepfakes labelled as such. Those rules apply to a much wider swathe of the industry. September 15 narrows the aperture back down to the handful of companies building the most capable systems on the planet.

A voluntary code, and one conspicuous holdout

Most of the industry has, at least nominally, opted into the framework already. Amazon, Anthropic, Google, IBM, Microsoft, Mistral AI, OpenAI and Cohere are among more than twenty companies that signed the EU's General-Purpose AI Code of Practice when the Commission published its final version in July 2025 — a voluntary instrument that, in theory, gives signatories an easier path to demonstrating compliance with Article 55 rather than negotiating bespoke arrangements with regulators. Even xAI signed on, though only to the code's safety-and-security chapter.

Meta did not. The company's global affairs chief, Joel Kaplan, called the code an example of regulatory overreach that introduced "legal uncertainties" beyond what the AI Act itself requires and warned it could "stunt" frontier model development in Europe. That leaves Meta's Llama models — some of which, at 405 billion parameters, sit comfortably above the systemic-risk compute threshold — subject to the same September 15 deadline, but without the compliance scaffolding the code was designed to provide. Meta will instead have to satisfy the AI Office directly, without a pre-agreed template to point to.

What happens if a lab simply doesn't comply

This is where the deadline becomes genuinely interesting, because nobody has watched Brussels test it yet. Article 101 of the Act gives the Commission power to fine a GPAI provider up to €15 million or 3% of its global annual turnover, whichever is larger, for infringements including supplying incomplete or misleading information, or refusing to make a model available for the AI Office's own technical evaluation. Beyond fines, the Office can demand risk-mitigation measures or, in the most severe cases, restrict or withdraw a model from the EU market entirely.

But a maximum fine is not a track record, and there is no precedent yet for how aggressively the AI Office will move against a company that submits a thin evaluation, misses the deadline outright, or — as seems likelier for Meta — challenges the process in European courts before a fine is ever levied. For an agency that only gained full enforcement powers on August 2, six weeks is a short runway to establish credibility. A soft first response would signal to every other regulated industry watching this rollout that the Act's biggest teeth are more decorative than the €15 million figure suggests; a genuinely punitive one would mark the EU as the first jurisdiction willing to discipline frontier AI development on its own terms, well ahead of the US or UK.

The submissions themselves won't be made public on September 15 — the AI Office reviews them privately, and any resulting enforcement action would likely surface only in the weeks that follow. The nearer-term marker to watch is whether Meta, or any other provider, misses the deadline outright, and how quickly Brussels says so.
