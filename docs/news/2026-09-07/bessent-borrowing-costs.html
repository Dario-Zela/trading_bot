BEYOND THE TAPE · US FISCAL · By L. Bordeaux · 2026-09-07 · 4 min read

Treasury Secretary Bessent confronts climbing US borrowing costs

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, whose department has doubled long-bond buybacks in a bid to cap rising US borrowing costs. — US Department of the Treasury (official portrait) / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Bessent's Treasury has begun buying back long bonds to cap yields near 5.3%, an admission that America's $1-trillion-a-year interest bill is now a live political problem.

There is a particular tell in Washington when a fiscal problem has stopped being deniable: officials stop insisting it doesn't exist and start explaining, at length, why they have it handled. Scott Bessent crossed that line this summer. The Treasury secretary who once waved away the $40 trillion debt milestone — "there's nothing magic about the $40 trillion number," he told CNBC on August 20th, adding that "we can grow our way out of that" — has spent the past three weeks doing something considerably more urgent than shrugging: intervening directly in the bond market to keep America's borrowing costs from climbing further.

The numbers explain the shift in posture. Net interest on the federal debt ran to $963 billion over the first ten months of fiscal 2026, up 14% from $846 billion over the same stretch a year earlier — a run rate of roughly $3.2 billion a day, and comfortably past $1 trillion on an annualised basis. That is now larger than the defence budget. It is the fastest-growing line item in Washington, and it grows automatically, with no vote required, every time the bond market decides Treasury debt deserves a higher yield.

From denial to damage control

On August 19th, Treasury announced it would at least double the size of its buybacks of longer-dated bonds — from a $2 billion cap to "at least" $4 billion per operation — running from September 9th through November 4th, concentrated in 10-to-30-year paper. Bessent framed it as a liquidity fix rather than a rate call: "we believe that there are many underlying factors in turn that the market is not looking at, and we are going to make a market... in these," he said, pointing to what he described as a persistent buyers' strike in the 30-year sector since late June. Officials have since floated tapping the roughly $1 trillion Treasury General Account to fund further purchases.

It worked for about a day. The 30-year yield fell from 5.26% to 5.18% and the 10-year slid from 4.68% to 4.63% on the announcement — then gave the move back within 24 hours as the broader sell-off resumed. By September 1st, the 30-year was back at 5.27%, roughly where it stood before Bessent intervened, and the 10-year had climbed more than 10 basis points since mid-August to around 4.8%. Bessent's own response to the reversal was studied nonchalance — "what happens over a month doesn't matter," he told reporters — the kind of line a Treasury secretary reaches for when the market has just demonstrated that it does.

The Fed problem underneath the bond problem

The buyback push has drawn a second, more delicate objection: it edges Treasury onto ground that traditionally belongs to the Federal Reserve. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh has made a point of favouring rates set by the open market rather than official intervention, and critics — including inside the central bank — warn that suppressing long-end yields by fiat risks fuelling the inflation the Fed is trying to contain, while quietly shifting more of the government's borrowing into short-dated bills that need refinancing sooner. That tension is compounding rather than easing: a blowout jobs report has firmed up expectations that the Fed's next move is a hike, not a cut, which pulls against everything Bessent is trying to do to the long end from the other side of the curve.

Not the only finance ministry with this problem

Bessent is hardly alone. Britain's Rachel Reeves-successor-in-waiting equivalent — chancellor-in-all-but-name pressures aside, it is Chancellor John Healey who has watched 10-year gilt yields hit their highest since June 2008, near 5.27%, squeezing his fiscal headroom from an estimated £23.6 billion at the Spring Statement down to roughly £13 billion ahead of next month's budget. The read-across is uncomfortable for Washington: when a G7 government's borrowing costs climb faster than its growth rate, the arithmetic of "grow our way out of it" gets harder every quarter, not easier.

Bessent still has room to manoeuvre — he has pointed to an OMB-led spending review under Russell Vought and a fraud task force he claims could recover "several hundred billion dollars," with a broader fiscal package promised imminently. Whether that lands before the next heavy slate of long-bond auctions, or whether the buyback strategy needs another escalation to hold the line, is the thing to watch. The bond market, unlike the podium, does not take a Treasury secretary's word for it.
