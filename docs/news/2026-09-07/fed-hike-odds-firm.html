Fed rate-hike odds firm as Wall Street digests blowout jobs report

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C., headquarters of the central bank weighing a September rate hike. — AgnosticPreachersKid / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
August payrolls beat forecasts by triple, pushing Fed hike odds to 58%, but next week's CPI — not jobs — will decide the September rate call.

Payrolls rose by 162,000 in August, nearly three times the 56,000 economists had penciled in, while the unemployment rate held at 4.1%. On a normal week, a labor market report that resilient would settle the question of what the Federal Reserve does next. Instead it has simply hardened the two camps already arguing over it.

Bond traders moved first and moved hard. The 10-year Treasury yield rose to roughly 4.79% on Friday, its highest level in months, while the 2-year note — most sensitive to Fed policy expectations — climbed to its highest since January 2025. CME FedWatch pricing for a quarter-point hike at the September 15–16 meeting jumped to 58.4%, up from 49.4% the day before. Stocks did the opposite: the S&P 500 slipped 0.38% to 7,718.60, the Dow fell 271.86 points (0.51%) to 53,414.25, and the Nasdaq eased 0.29%. None of those equity moves qualifies as a selloff — they sit comfortably inside a routine trading day — but the fact that a blowout jobs report couldn't lift stocks says something about where the market's attention actually is.

A jobs report that argues with itself

The details reinforced the headline number rather than undercutting it. June and July payrolls were revised up by a combined 55,000, meaning the labor market has been running hotter than reported for three straight months, not just one. Bars and restaurants led the hiring, a sign of steady consumer spending; information-sector jobs shrank, which several economists tied to AI-driven cost-cutting rather than a broader slowdown. Wage growth, notably, continued to lag inflation — a detail that keeps this from reading as a straightforward overheating story.

That nuance matters because the Fed is no longer speaking with one voice. Fed Governor Christopher Waller said this week that his vote hinges on the inflation data still to come, and that continued disinflation would incline him toward holding rates steady. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh, sworn in on May 22 after a bruising 54–45 Senate confirmation, has taken the opposite tone: at Jackson Hole he said recent tame inflation prints "do not tell me that underlying trends have meaningfully improved," language markets read as leaning toward a hike.

Rates, not payrolls, are driving stocks

That split explains why equities didn't celebrate a strong jobs number the way they normally would. With a rate decision genuinely contested — FedWatch odds swung from roughly 45% to nearly 60% and back over the space of a week — every incoming data point is being read primarily for what it means for the September meeting, not for what it means for the economy on its own terms. A resilient labor market ordinarily reads as good news for equities; when the Fed's reaction function is this uncertain, it instead reads as a reason yields might stay elevated for longer, which is what actually moved stocks Friday.

What decides it

By the Fed's own account, the jobs report was never going to be the deciding input. Waller said as much directly: the August and July consumer and producer price indexes, due before the meeting, are what will determine his vote. The CPI print lands September 11, four days before the FOMC convenes. If it shows inflation cooling further, Waller's camp gains ground and the hold case strengthens despite Friday's hiring surprise. If it doesn't, Warsh's reading — that disinflation has stalled — gets reinforced by a labor market that gives the Fed room to stay restrictive without fear of triggering a downturn. Either way, the jobs report didn't resolve anything; it raised the stakes on the number that will.
