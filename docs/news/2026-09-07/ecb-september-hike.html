ECB on track for September hike as energy keeps inflation elevated

The European Central Bank's headquarters in Frankfurt, where the Governing Council meets Thursday to decide on rates. — Daniel Vorndran (DXR) / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0
In one sentence
Markets price a near-certain ECB hike to 2.50% on Thursday, but Lagarde's own guidance suggests rates alone won't tame energy-driven inflation before 2027.

Barring a surprise, the European Central Bank raises interest rates on Thursday for the second time this year — not because the eurozone economy is running hot, but because the Middle East is still on fire.

Money markets are pricing a 25 basis-point hike to the deposit rate at close to 99% ahead of the September 10 meeting, according to LSEG data, which would take the rate to 2.50% from 2.25%. That certainty is itself the story: a hike this well-flagged carries almost no event risk for EUR positioning on the day. The more consequential signal is what ECB President Christine Lagarde has been saying about the two years after it — that inflation will stay "well above target" into the first half of 2027, even with borrowing costs higher than at any point since the bank's last tightening cycle ended.

An energy shock, not a demand problem

Euro-area headline inflation hit 3.3% in August, up from 2.9% in July and the highest since September 2024. The ECB's own economists — Kristina Barauskaitė Griškevičienė and Claus Brand — have traced the mechanism precisely: adverse energy supply factors accounted for roughly 90% of the rise in energy inflation between January and May this year. That is a strikingly narrow attribution for a central bank whose main tool, the price of money, works chiefly by cooling demand.

The proximate cause sits roughly 3,500 kilometres from Frankfurt. The tanker war between the United States and Iran, which reignited over the collapse of a June memorandum meant to reopen the Strait of Hormuz to commercial shipping, has kept Brent crude grinding higher — trading near $97 a barrel after US strikes on three Iranian tankers drew retaliatory attacks on vessels linked to Washington. Iran's foreign ministry has said flatly that talks are off the table while the US naval presence in the strait continues. None of that is something a quarter-point rate move touches.

ECB staff have been careful to distinguish the current episode from 2021–22, when supply and demand shocks arrived together and fiscal stimulus amplified both. This time, they argue, demand and fiscal effects play only a "minor" role — which is precisely why the bank has opted for a gradual response rather than the forceful hiking cycle that followed the pandemic-era inflation surge. The deposit rate rose from 2% to 2.25% in June; Thursday's expected move would be the second step of that more measured climb, not a return to 2022-style urgency.

The bind: hike into weakness, or don't hike at all

This is the position Lagarde has been trying to describe without quite admitting it outright: a central bank raising rates while telling markets, in the same breath, that it doesn't expect those rate rises to bring inflation to heel on any near-term timetable. She has said that "the longer energy prices stay high, the more likely they are to drive up broader inflation through indirect and second-round effects" — the pass-through into transport costs, into manufacturing input prices, into wage bargaining, that turns an energy shock into something stickier. The ECB's own projections put headline inflation returning to target only in the second half of 2027, averaging 2.3% across the year as a whole.

That creates an uncomfortable asymmetry for a hiking decision. Euro-area growth has not been strong enough to justify tightening on demand grounds; the case for Thursday's move rests almost entirely on preventing an energy shock from becoming embedded in expectations. Some economists — a minority against the market's near-unanimous pricing — argue the ECB should hold rather than hike into a shock it cannot address at the source, on the view that a supply-side inflation spike is better weathered than fought. The ECB's evident preference for gradualism over the 2022 playbook suggests the Governing Council shares some of that unease, even as it hikes anyway.

Market pricing also shows an 80% probability attached to the September move specifically, alongside a roughly 40% chance of a further increase by December — implying traders think Thursday is not necessarily the last word if the Strait of Hormuz standoff drags on.

What it means for positioning

For a hike this thoroughly priced, the euro's reaction on the day is likely to hinge less on the decision itself than on the tone of Lagarde's press conference — specifically, whether she leans into the December option or signals the ECB wants to pause and assess pass-through effects first. A hawkish hold-the-door-open message would be euro-supportive; anything that reads as resignation to a prolonged above-target period, absent a peace deal in the Gulf, risks unsettling both the currency and eurozone rate-sensitive equities, given growth has already been the weaker side of this trade-off.

The next real catalyst is less the meeting itself than the Strait of Hormuz. Any durable de-escalation between Washington and Tehran would do more to bring eurozone inflation back toward target than a full percentage point of ECB tightening. Absent that, Thursday's hike looks less like a solution than an acknowledgment — a central bank raising the price of money largely to signal it is still in the room, while the actual inflation problem is decided by tanker movements it cannot control.
