AfD's historic Saxony-Anhalt win nears power for first time since WW2

Ulrich Siegmund, the AfD's lead candidate in Saxony-Anhalt, campaigning ahead of the vote that delivered the party's strongest result since its founding. — Roy Zuo / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
AfD won a record 43.8% in Saxony-Anhalt, edging past Merz's CDU for the first time, but fell three seats short of power as rivals held the coalition firewall.

The far-right Alternative for Germany won more votes than any other party in Sunday's Saxony-Anhalt state election, taking 43.8 percent — its strongest result anywhere since the party was founded in 2013, and enough to make it the largest single force in a German regional parliament for the first time since the Second World War. It will not, however, be forming a government.

That contradiction — a landslide that changes little in the short run but a great deal in the long run — is the story of German politics in September 2026.

A 24-point swing

The scale of the result is best understood against what it replaced. In the 2021 Saxony-Anhalt election, the AfD took 20.8 percent. On Sunday it more than doubled that, a swing of roughly 23 percentage points that exit polls from ARD and ZDF had signalled hours earlier, when they put the party at 44.1 to 44.5 percent. Turnout was unusually high, at 77.8 percent, suggesting the result reflects genuine mobilisation rather than a low-turnout fluke.

The collapse on the other side was just as sharp. Chancellor Friedrich Merz's Christian Democrats, who had governed the state for 24 consecutive years, fell to 17.2 percent — down almost 20 points from 2021 and, for the first time in any major German election, trailing the AfD by a wide margin. The Social Democrats took 9.3 percent, the Greens 8.9, the Left Party 8.6, and the upstart Sahra Wagenknecht Alliance, BSW, scraped in at 5.3. The pro-business FDP fell out of the state parliament entirely.

Ulrich Siegmund, the AfD's 35-year-old lead candidate in Saxony-Anhalt, called it a moment voters had chosen deliberately. "We have written history," he told supporters, adding that the result showed "things cannot carry on like this." He was also happy to credit an unlikely campaigner: "a very good campaigner for us in this election campaign was Friedrich Merz," he told public broadcaster ARD — a jab at a chancellor whose government has struggled to revive growth in Europe's largest economy. Alice Weidel, the AfD's national co-leader, described the outcome simply as "sensational."

Three seats short of the wall

What the AfD did not win was power. Saxony-Anhalt's parliament has 83 seats, and a governing majority requires 42. The AfD's vote share translated into 39 — enough to dominate every debate in Magdeburg, not enough to govern alone, and every other party in the chamber has ruled out joining it in coalition. Franziska Hoppermann, the CDU's national general secretary, restated the party's position bluntly: the AfD is a "right-wing extremist party" that "wants to leave the euro" and has cultivated ties with Russia. Cooperation, she said, is not on the table.

That leaves the arithmetic of exclusion looking uncomfortable. Between them, the CDU, SPD, Greens, Left Party and BSW hold 44 seats — just two more than the majority threshold, and only if all five agree to sit in the same government. The CDU has separately ruled out working with the Left Party at a federal level, which would force an unprecedented five-party coalition, a shaky minority government leaning on ad hoc votes, or some combination of defections and abstentions to get a budget through. Sven Schulze, the CDU state premier who took over from the retiring Reiner Haseloff earlier this year, initially hedged when asked whether the party's long-standing "firewall" — the Brandmauer against any dealings with the AfD — would hold, before confirming he did not intend to cooperate with the far right. The state's incumbent government, a CDU-SPD-FDP arrangement nicknamed the "Germany coalition" for its black-red-yellow colours, no longer commands enough seats to reconvene in that form.

Merz's problem, and the eurozone's

For Merz, the result lands as a direct rebuke less than a year into his chancellorship. He made reducing the AfD's support an explicit goal when he took office; instead it has roughly doubled in the polls nationally, now sitting close to 30 percent. Merz gave no public statement on election night, leaving CDU headquarters after 10pm, and party colleagues in the east are reported to be keeping their distance from a leader whose economic agenda has yet to show results voters can feel.

The practical policy effect of Sunday's vote is limited — Saxony-Anhalt is a state of just over two million people, and firewalls elsewhere in Germany remain, for now, intact. But the pattern is what matters for anyone pricing German and eurozone risk: a governing coalition in Berlin that must build ever-wider, more fractious alliances to keep the AfD out is a coalition with less room to manoeuvre on fiscal discipline, EU treaty questions, or NATO burden-sharing, all areas where Germany's assent is usually assumed rather than negotiated. A country accustomed to boringly stable government is edging toward the kind of multi-party bargaining that has periodically paralysed Italy, Belgium and the Netherlands — with the difference that Germany is the currency bloc's anchor economy.

The next test comes fast. Berlin and Mecklenburg-Vorpommern both vote on 20 September, just two weeks away, with the AfD polling around 36 percent in Mecklenburg-Vorpommern against an incumbent Social Democrat government. A second strong showing there would harden the sense that Saxony-Anhalt was not an aberration but a floor.
