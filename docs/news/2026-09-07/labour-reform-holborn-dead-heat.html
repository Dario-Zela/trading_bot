Labour and Reform locked in dead heat as Holborn by-election turns tense

Keir Starmer's resignation from the Commons on 1 September, two months after leaving No 10, triggered the Holborn and St Pancras by-election. — Official portrait, UK Government / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Starmer's resignation as MP has opened a Holborn by-election that looks like Labour vs Reform nationally but is really Labour vs Green locally, with a racism row over Reform's Zia Yusuf overshadowing both.

Keir Starmer left Downing Street in June. On 1 September, he left Parliament too — resigning as MP for Holborn and St Pancras, the constituency he had represented since 2015, and taking up the ceremonial post of Steward of the Chiltern Hundreds that formally vacates a Commons seat. The result is a by-election in one of Labour's safest London strongholds, arriving at the exact moment national polling shows Labour and Reform UK running dead level — and it has already produced a racism row that has consumed more attention than the campaign itself.

Why a former PM quit his own seat

Starmer's departure from No 10 was not, in the end, of his own choosing in the way he might have liked. He announced his resignation as prime minister on 22 June, after a bruising stretch of poor local election results in May and mounting discontent inside his own party. Andy Burnham, then mayor of Greater Manchester, won the subsequent Labour leadership contest and was sworn in as prime minister on 20 July — Britain's seventh in ten years.

That left Starmer as a backbencher for barely six weeks before he decided to leave the Commons altogether. In his resignation statement he said it was "the right time" to "pass the baton to a successor" and turn instead to "international affairs including defence, security, trade and technology in a fast-changing world," alongside continuing his campaign against violence against women and girls. Former prime ministers staying on as backbenchers is common practice in British politics; quitting within two months of leaving office is not. The move reads less as scandal than as a pragmatic exit — but it has handed Reform and the Greens a rare opening in a seat Labour has not seriously had to defend in a generation.

A three-cornered fight that isn't quite what it looks like

Starmer's majority in 2024 was commanding on paper: 18,884 votes, or 48.9% of the vote, a lead of 11,572 over the runner-up. But that runner-up was not Reform — it was the independent, pro-Gaza candidate Andrew Feinstein, who took 18.9%. The Green Party's David Stansell ran third with 10.4%. Reform did not seriously register.

That history complicates the neat "Labour vs Reform" framing the row over the by-election has attracted. Labour's selection meeting is due on 9 September, with Camden Council leader Sagal Abdi-Wali the frontrunner against former Westminster council leader Adam Hug and ex-Camden councillor Rishi Madlani. The Green Party leader, Zack Polanski, announced on 3 September that he would stand personally — a significant gamble that turns the contest into a referendum on his own six-month-old leadership. His campaign, which claimed to have knocked on nearly 1,500 doors in its first three days, is aiming squarely at the seat's young, student-heavy electorate: almost half of Holborn and St Pancras residents are aged 18 to 34, a demographic the New Statesman's polling model shows shifting toward him.

Reform UK, by contrast, polled just 8.5% across the seat's wards in May's Camden local elections and has not been treated as a serious contender by forecasters — Britain Predicts currently gives Labour a five-point lead in the seat itself. Analysts note Labour's local lead would likely narrow from nine points to around three if the Greens had fielded candidates in every ward in May, which points to a genuine Labour-Green fight rather than a Labour-Reform one. That Reform has nonetheless dominated the early coverage says more about the party's national momentum, and its willingness to pick a fight, than about its prospects on the ground in Camden.

The row that overtook the campaign

The fight arrived via Zia Yusuf, Reform's spokesperson for home affairs, who posted on X that "the front runner being foisted on the people of Holborn by Labour to replace the former Prime Minister Keir Starmer in our Parliament was born in Somalia." Abdi-Wali, who fled Somalia as a child refugee in the 1990s, has lived in Camden for more than three decades, sat as a councillor since 2022, and became council leader in May.

The backlash was immediate and crossed party lines. Business Secretary Jonathan Reynolds told Sky News it was "pretty straightforward racism, isn't it?", noting Abdi-Wali had "spent decades in this country" and made a "huge contribution to her local community." Nadine Dorries, the former Conservative minister who defected to Reform, called the post "nasty," while Laila Cunningham — Reform's own candidate for London mayor and the daughter of Egyptian immigrants — said she didn't understand the point Yusuf was making: "that woman is British… she's the leader of Camden council." Yusuf did not withdraw the post.

Why Labour can't simply shrug this off

The episode lands awkwardly for Labour because the party's national position is far shakier than a safe-seat by-election ought to suggest. YouGov's Westminster tracker for 30–31 August put Labour and Reform tied at 23% apiece, with the Conservatives on 20%, the Greens on 13% and the Liberal Democrats on 12% — a genuinely fragmented field with no obvious route to a majority for anyone.

Burnham himself remains personally popular: 37% approve of his handling of the job against 21% who disapprove, a net rating of +16 that YouGov calls the best for any prime minister since Boris Johnson's early pandemic honeymoon. But that popularity has not transferred to his government, where approval sits at just 22% against 55% disapproval — up from 17% and down from 61% respectively since he took office, but still a wide gap. YouGov's own analysts have puzzled over why a well-liked leader is failing to move a stubbornly unpopular administration; the honest answer is that voters appear to be separating the man from the record.

That gap is what makes Holborn and St Pancras worth watching beyond its local arithmetic. A safe seat becoming competitive — even if the competition turns out to be with the Greens rather than Reform — is a symptom of a governing party that cannot yet convert a popular leader into durable support. Reform's move to inject itself into the contest, however clumsily, is a bet that grievance politics travels even into constituencies where it has no organic base. Whether that bet pays off, or simply hands the seat's substantial student and minority vote to the Greens, will be one of the first tests of Burnham's premiership beyond the opinion polls. The Labour selection concludes on 9 September, the Greens confirm their nomination the following day, and a polling day has yet to be set — but the shape of the fight is now set regardless.
