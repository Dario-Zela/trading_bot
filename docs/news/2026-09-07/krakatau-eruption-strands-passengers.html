Indonesian volcano eruption strands 170,000 air passengers

Satellite imagery shows an ash plume rising from Anak Krakatau in the Sunda Strait. — Copernicus Sentinel-2 / European Union
In one sentence
Anak Krakatau's ash cloud shut eight Indonesian airports, grounding 1,500+ flights and stranding 170,000 passengers on routes to Singapore, Sydney and Doha.

Anak Krakatau, the young volcano that rose from the caldera left by history's most famous eruption, sent ash to 50,000 feet over the weekend and shut down one of Southeast Asia's busiest air corridors. Eight Indonesian airports — including both of Jakarta's, Soekarno-Hatta and Halim Perdanakusuma — suspended operations, grounding more than 1,500 flights and stranding an estimated 170,000 passengers.

The volcano, sitting in the Sunda Strait between Java and Sumatra, produced two distinct eruptions early Sunday: a 30-second burst at 3:53am local time that sent ash to 20,000 feet, followed roughly three hours later by a 16-second eruption that doubled the plume's height to 50,000 feet. That second cloud is the one that mattered — high enough to sit squarely in cruising altitude for long-haul jets, and dense enough to strip turbine blades and clog fuel systems on contact. Indonesia's Geological Agency has held the volcano at Alert Level III, the second-highest of four tiers, since early July, and officials say they cannot rule out a larger eruption. A 3-kilometre exclusion zone remains in place around the crater.

The toll on carriers

Soekarno-Hatta, Indonesia's principal international gateway, alone absorbed roughly 900 to 960 cancelled or delayed movements. Qatar Airways scrapped four Doha–Jakarta rotations; Qantas pulled flights on its Jakarta route; Singapore Airlines, Malaysia Airlines, AirAsia and the Lion Air Group all cancelled services, with ripple effects reaching connections to Sydney. At least four hubs, Soekarno-Hatta among them, extended their shutdowns through Monday evening local time. Lukman F. Laisa of Indonesia's transport ministry framed the closures plainly: "Safety remains our top priority in every operational decision" — an unglamorous line, but the correct one, given what ash ingestion does to a jet engine mid-flight.

The direct cost falls on airline balance sheets in the form of rebooking, crew rotations pushed out of position, and aircraft stranded on the wrong side of the network — the same mechanics that turned a 2010 Icelandic ash cloud into a multi-day scramble for European carriers, only compressed here into a corridor that handles a much denser mix of intra-Asia and long-haul traffic. No shipping closures have been reported; ash disperses at altitudes well above sea lanes, and the Sunda Strait itself remains open to the container traffic that transits it toward Singapore. But the episode is a reminder that the strait does double duty, as both a critical shipping lane and the flight path linking Jakarta to Singapore, the Gulf and Australia, and that Indonesia's aviation network has limited redundancy when its two busiest airports go down together.

What to watch

The binding constraint now is geology, not logistics. Indonesia's Geological Agency says it cannot forecast whether Sunday's eruption was a peak or a prelude — a caveat with some historical weight, given that the volcano's 1883 parent eruption killed more than 36,000 people and this cone has been rebuilding the caldera ever since. Reopening timelines hinge on ash output falling and staying down for several consecutive days, not a single quiet morning. Until then, expect rolling extensions at Soekarno-Hatta and the seven other airports, continued rerouting on Gulf and Australia-bound long-haul, and a slow clear of the passenger backlog that will likely stretch into the following week even once ash clears — rebooking congestion typically outlasts the weather event that caused it.
