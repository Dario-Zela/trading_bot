BEYOND THE TAPE · SCOTLAND · By L. Bordeaux · 2026-09-07 · 4 min read

Swinney unveils biggest NHS shake-up since devolution

First Minister John Swinney, who announced the NHS board merger as part of his 2026-31 Programme for Government. — Scottish Government / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Swinney will merge Scotland's 14 NHS boards into two by 2031 to hit a 26-week wait target, but won't say how many jobs will go.

Scotland's National Health Service is about to lose most of its middle management, at least on the org chart. John Swinney used his Programme for Government address on 1 September to announce that the country's 14 territorial health boards — the regional bodies that have run hospitals and clinics since long before devolution — will be collapsed into two: one for the west of Scotland, one for the east. Separate arrangements are still being worked out for the island health boards in Orkney, Shetland and the Western Isles.

"The most significant structural reform of our NHS in the era of devolution."

That was how Swinney framed it to MSPs, and on scale he is not exaggerating. The current 14-board map has been the basic architecture of Scottish healthcare since the 1970s. Tearing it down for two mega-boards is the kind of change usually reserved for a system in crisis — which, on waiting times at least, this one is.

The pitch: shorter waits, same postcode

The headline commitment is that by the end of 2031, no patient in Scotland will wait more than 26 weeks for treatment. That target is meant to be underpinned by a "National Flow Plan" and what the government describes as targeted investment — including a previously trailed £10 billion of NHS capital spending over the coming decade, with a new Monklands hospital pencilled in to break ground by 2028.

Swinney has also been at pains to insist patients will not have to travel further to get treated, even as their local board disappears into a regional one. Whether that promise survives contact with a consolidated back office remains to be seen — folding 14 boards into two is precisely the kind of exercise that tends to centralise specialist services onto fewer sites, whatever ministers say about local access on launch day.

The gap: nobody will say how many jobs go

The plan's weak joint is cost and headcount. Pressed three times in Holyrood on how many NHS jobs might disappear as boards merge, Swinney said he "can't put a specific figure" on it — while declining to rule redundancies out altogether. No accompanying cost-benefit analysis has been published either, so there is no public estimate of what the reorganisation itself will cost to execute, set against the savings it is supposed to generate.

That vagueness has not gone unnoticed. Unison Scotland's head of health, Matt McLaughlin, accused the government of "hiding behind reform" and said the plan was "about cutting jobs and millions of pounds from the health budget." Opposition parties were similarly unimpressed: Scottish Labour's Jackie Baillie said it was hard to tell whether Swinney was "being evasive or simply has no idea what he is going to do," while the Scottish Conservatives' Miles Briggs warned that a restructuring exercise was being dressed up as reform while communities risk losing local services.

The scepticism has history behind it. This is not the SNP's first go at fixing waiting times — a legal guarantee of 12-week treatment dates back to 2011, and Swinney's own pledge to eliminate year-long waits by March 2026 was not met. Public Health Scotland data from June showed 42.5% of children waiting more than 12 weeks just for an outpatient appointment, which is the backdrop against which a 26-week promise for 2031 has to be judged.

What it means, and what to watch

For readers outside Scotland, this is a domestic policy story with a long fuse: the wait-time target sits five years out, well past the point where any single quarter's NHS Scotland performance data would move a market. The closer thing to watch is execution risk within Scotland itself — whether the government publishes a cost and workforce breakdown before legislation is drafted, and whether Unison and other health unions escalate from statements to industrial action if redundancy numbers do emerge. The island-board question, still unresolved, is the next concrete decision point; expect more detail when the legislation underpinning the two strategic boards is introduced to Holyrood.
