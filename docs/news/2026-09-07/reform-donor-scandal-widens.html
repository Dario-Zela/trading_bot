Reform UK donor scandal widens after undercover recordings of aides

Nigel Farage, whose party faces its second foreign-funding scandal since July. — Laurie Noble / UK Parliament, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Two senior Reform UK aides quit and rivals reported the party to police after recordings showed them arranging hidden foreign funding for £32,500 of polling, which Farage calls 'entrapment'.

Nigel Farage spent Thursday's Reform UK conference opening celebrating the party's poll lead. By Friday morning he was explaining, in front of a bank of cameras, why two of his most senior aides had just resigned over allegations that the party tried to disguise foreign money as domestic funding for opinion polls.

The allegations come from an undercover investigation broadcast by Channel 4 News on 3 September, built on roughly a year of covert filming by Verbatim, a reporting outfit founded by journalists from the Centre for Climate Reporting. Undercover operatives posed as a wealthy American donor's associates, cultivating relationships with Reform's inner circle over months. What they recorded now sits at the centre of a widening dispute over whether Britain's newest major party broke election-finance law — or was set up.

What the recordings show

At the centre of the footage are two men: James Orr, Reform's head of policy, and Dan Jukes, a longtime aide to Farage. The two are recorded discussing three opinion polls, costing £32,500 in total, that were published in outlets including The Times without disclosing that a US company had covered the bill — money Reform figures believed, per the recordings, originated from a foreign donor rather than a domestic one, as UK law requires for anything of value given to a political party.

"It's clandestine... hugely important."

That is how Jukes is heard describing the polling arrangement. Orr, separately, is recorded saying Farage "doesn't know, won't know" about how the polls were funded, and that it was something the leader would "rather not know." Elsewhere in the footage, Jukes is filmed suggesting ways an American donor could route money into the party while avoiding the rules that bar foreign contributions — advice offered, unknowingly, to an undercover reporter.

A pattern, not an isolated lapse

The scandal lands two months after a separate controversy in July, when Reform faced scrutiny over undeclared donations linked to the crypto entrepreneur Christopher Harborne, reported at roughly £5 million, and to George Cottrell, a former Farage aide with a fraud conviction. That earlier episode drew the attention of the National Crime Agency, the Metropolitan Police and the Parliamentary Standards Commissioner. Taken together, the two affairs point to a party whose fundraising apparatus has repeatedly struggled to keep pace with the scrutiny that comes with genuine electoral contention — Reform now regularly leads national polling and is locked with Labour in a closely watched Holborn by-election.

Orr and Jukes stood down on Friday morning "pending the outcome" of an internal Reform inquiry, according to the party. Lee Anderson, Reform's chairman, told Sky News there was "no chance" of either man returning. Farage was blunter about his own feelings: "They have been removed this morning. I'm not happy about it," he said, describing both men as contractors rather than core staff — Jukes, he noted, had "worked with me for many years, but never involved in fundraising."

Entrapment defence meets a skeptical referee

Farage's central argument is that the entire operation was a set-up. "It's a clear case of entrapment, and they got a couple of our contractors to say things that perhaps should not have been said," he told reporters, calling it a "massive sting operation" that "must have cost them hundreds of thousands of pounds, all foreign funded, by the way." On the substance, he was categorical: "No illegal transaction has occurred," citing a barrister's opinion and what he called "plenty of precedent."

That framing has not persuaded the officials whose job it is to police party funding. Bob Posner, a former chief executive of the Electoral Commission, rejected the entrapment framing outright: "What I saw was people willing to break the law," he told LBC, adding that the footage pointed toward "serious criminal offences." The Liberal Democrats' Lisa Smart and Labour chair Bridget Phillipson have both filed formal reports with the Metropolitan Police and the Electoral Commission; the Green Party has separately called for an investigation.

The distinction matters legally as well as politically. Entrapment can complicate a prosecution's use of evidence in some circumstances, but it is not, on its own, a defence to the underlying conduct — if Reform officials did knowingly arrange for a foreign-linked entity to fund domestic political activity, the recordings of them discussing it would still be relevant evidence of intent, regardless of who was on the other end of the conversation.

What comes next

The Electoral Commission and the Metropolitan Police must now decide whether to open formal investigations — a process that in comparable UK cases has taken months rather than weeks. Reform's own internal inquiry, promised by Anderson's office, has no public deadline. In the meantime, the party heads into the Holborn by-election needing to show voters that a funding structure built quickly, to match a poll surge nobody entirely expected, can survive the scrutiny that surge has now attracted.
