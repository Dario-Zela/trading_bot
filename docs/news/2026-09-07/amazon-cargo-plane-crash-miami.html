Amazon-branded cargo plane overshoots runway in Miami, five dead

An Amazon Prime Air Boeing 767-300 freighter, the same model involved in Sunday's Miami runway overrun. — formulanone / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
An Amazon Prime Air Boeing 767 overran the runway landing in Miami and hit parked vehicles, killing five; the NTSB has dispatched a team led by Jennifer Homendy.

A Boeing 767 flying under the Amazon Prime Air brand overran the runway while landing at Miami International Airport on Sunday afternoon, plowed through a perimeter fence and struck a lot full of parked vehicles. Five people were killed and five more injured, three critically, according to airport and federal officials.

The aircraft, flying as 21 Air Flight 7598, was arriving from Luis Muñoz Marín International Airport in San Juan, Puerto Rico, when it overshot Runway 30 shortly before 2 p.m. local time, according to the Federal Aviation Administration. It came to rest in a field beyond the airport perimeter after hitting ground vehicles; several reports describe a fire breaking out on impact. The National Transportation Safety Board said it is sending an investigative team led by chair Jennifer Homendy, who is expected to brief reporters in Miami.

The 767-300 was 32 years old, built for passenger service in the early 1990s and later converted to a freighter — a common lifecycle for aircraft flying overnight cargo routes, where operators favor older, cheaper hulls over new-build jets. It was not flown by Amazon itself. Like most of the roughly 100 aircraft in the Prime Air network, it was operated on Amazon's behalf by a contract carrier, in this case 21 Air, a Miami-based cargo airline that also flies for DHL.

"We're heartbroken to learn that five people lost their lives in today's incident at Miami International Airport," Amazon said in a statement, adding that it was cooperating fully with investigators. The airport briefly closed operations before reopening arrivals and departures later Sunday.

A structural weak point

Amazon does not hold its own air-carrier certificate for cargo operations. Instead it leases aircraft and buys capacity from a roster of third-party operators — 21 Air, Atlas Air, ATSG and others — while marketing the service under the Prime Air name. That arrangement has let Amazon scale a network of roughly 100 aircraft in under a decade without building an in-house airline from scratch, but it also means safety oversight sits one layer removed: the FAA certifies and inspects the contract carriers, not Amazon's logistics arm directly.

Sunday's crash is not the first fatal accident tied to that network. In 2019, an Atlas Air 767 flying an Amazon cargo route crashed into Trinity Bay near Houston, killing all three people aboard; the NTSB later attributed that crash to pilot spatial disorientation rather than mechanical failure. A pattern of two fatal 767 losses in seven years, both involving contract carriers flying for Amazon, is likely to sharpen the NTSB's questions about training standards and oversight consistency across the patchwork of operators Amazon relies on.

What's at stake for Amazon

Prime Air is central to Amazon's pitch that it can move packages, and increasingly cargo for other shippers, faster than rivals. An accident with fatalities gives the FAA grounds to order additional inspections or restrict specific aircraft types or operators while the investigation proceeds — a step that, even short of a grounding, can ripple through delivery schedules during the run-up to the holiday shipping season. Insurers covering Amazon's contract carriers will also be watching the NTSB's eventual finding on cause, since a repeat pattern of accidents tends to move premiums for the entire fleet, not just the aircraft involved.

The NTSB investigation into a runway overrun typically examines braking performance, runway conditions, aircraft weight and approach speed before turning to crew actions — a process that took roughly 18 months in the Atlas Air case. Homendy's Miami briefing, expected in the coming days, should give the first indication of which of those threads investigators are pulling on first.
