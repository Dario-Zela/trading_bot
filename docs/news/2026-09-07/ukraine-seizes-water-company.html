Ukraine moves to seize water company tied to billionaire family

Mikhail Fridman, the sanctioned Alfa Group founder whose 49% stake in IDS Ukraine prompted the 2022 freeze that now threatens to engulf the company's other shareholders. — Anton Nossik / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
Ukraine is seeking to nationalise the water company behind Morshynska, reaching beyond a sanctioned Russian oligarch's stake into a British family's separate shareholding.

Ukraine's asset-recovery agency is moving to take permanent state control of the country's largest bottled-water producer — and this time the target isn't a Russian oligarch's own shareholding, but a stake belonging to the British-based family of a Georgian billionaire who has been dead since 2008.

IDS Ukraine, maker of the Morshynska and Myrhorodska brands that between them command something close to universal recognition among Ukrainian shoppers, has been under state-appointed management since its corporate holdings were frozen in 2022. The freeze was triggered by a 49% stake held by Russian investors led by Mikhail Fridman, the Alfa Group founder sanctioned by Ukraine, the US, the EU and the UK. But the Ministry of Justice's push, now before Kyiv's High Anti-Corruption Court, seeks nationalisation of the whole group — including the 34% held by relatives of Badri Patarkatsishvili, the late Georgian tycoon whose estate passed to his wife and daughters in the UK, one of whom, Liana Patarkatsishvili, is a London theatre producer with no sanctions history of her own.

A stake once removed

The Patarkatsishvili family's position, relayed through a spokesperson, is straightforward: an asset should not be taken from shareholders who are not themselves sanctioned. Badri Patarkatsishvili, who fled to Britain in 2007 and died the following year at 52, built his fortune amid the scramble for Soviet-era assets alongside business partners including Boris Berezovsky. His 34% in IDS passed to his widow Inna and daughters Liana and Iya — none of them on any sanctions list, and none, the family argues, deserving of confiscation for the sins of a co-shareholder.

Ukraine's counter-argument, made by Yaroslava Maksymenko, who heads the Asset Recovery and Management Agency (ARMA), is that claims of unlawful conduct by the agency amount to "misinformation," and that the structure of IDS Ukraine — Fridman's Alfa-linked entities holding just under half, family and Georgian-government stakes making up the rest — has functioned for years as a vehicle that lets a sanctioned oligarch's money sit inside an ostensibly clean company. IDS Ukraine itself denies money-laundering allegations and says operations have continued uninterrupted since the freeze took effect.

Why now

The case has taken on added urgency this year. ARMA opened a tender in August to appoint an independent manager for the roughly $165m IDS group — its water brands, alongside Alaska and Aqua Life, control something close to 40% of Ukraine's bottled-water market — only for its own servers to be hit by a cyberattack just days before the bidding deadline. Ukrainian officials have pointed to APT28, the GRU-linked hacking unit, and the Security Service of Ukraine and the National Anti-Corruption Bureau are both now investigating. ARMA says the competition will proceed regardless.

The Ministry of Justice's underlying lawsuit, filed with the High Anti-Corruption Court in late 2024, names not just Fridman but his Alfa Group co-founders Petr Aven and Andrei Kosogov, seeking full nationalisation of the holding structure. A ruling could send the assets to Ukraine's State Property Fund, ending ARMA's management role — and, if it extends to the family stake, would mark the clearest instance yet of Kyiv reaching past a sanctioned individual's direct holdings into the wider ownership chain around them.

The Patarkatsishvilis have signalled they will take the fight to international arbitration if Ukrainian courts do not grant relief. What happens next hinges less on politics than on a narrower legal question the anti-corruption court has yet to answer: whether proximity to a sanctioned shareholder is itself grounds for confiscation, or whether Ukraine's asset-recovery net has simply been cast wider than the law was written to allow.
