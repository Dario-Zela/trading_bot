Nvidia's Hugging Face deal takes shape with $1bn staff retention pool

Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang, whose company has agreed to buy open-source AI platform Hugging Face for close to $13 billion. — Prime Minister's Office, India / Wikimedia Commons (GODL-India)
In one sentence
Nvidia will pay roughly $12.93bn for Hugging Face, including a $1bn staff retention pool, extending its dominance from AI chips into the software platform developers use to build with models.

Nvidia, already the company that supplies the chips underneath almost every large AI model on Earth, is now buying the place where those models live. The chipmaker has agreed to acquire Hugging Face, the open-source platform used by millions of developers to share and download AI models, for roughly $12.93 billion — a deal that includes an unusually large $1 billion retention pool designed to keep Hugging Face's roughly 750 employees from walking out the door once Nvidia's name is on the letterhead.

The agreement, signed on 2 September, values Hugging Face's equity at close to $11.9 billion, with the remainder set aside as equity-based retention awards for staff who join Nvidia. Hugging Face's co-founders will stay on for six years — a long tail for a startup acquisition, and a signal of how much Nvidia is paying not just for the platform but for the people who built it.

From silicon to software

Hugging Face is not a household name in the way ChatGPT or Midjourney are, but among the people who actually build AI systems, it functions as something close to essential infrastructure. The platform hosts nearly 3 million models and hundreds of thousands of datasets, and says it now serves more than 18 million monthly users — researchers, startups and large enterprises pulling down pretrained models rather than training their own from scratch. Hugging Face's own ambition, according to chief executive Clément Delangue, is to grow that to 100 million users within a few years.

For Nvidia, the logic is straightforward even if the price tag is startling. The company has spent a decade building an overwhelming lead in the chips that train and run AI models. What it has not owned is the layer above that — the software and distribution channels developers actually touch when deciding which models to use and how to deploy them. Buying Hugging Face gives Nvidia a foothold in that layer, extending its reach from the data centre into the workflow of essentially every AI developer who touches open models.

"Open models strengthen safety and cybersecurity, accelerate innovation and diffusion, and enable sovereignty," Jensen Huang, Nvidia's chief executive, said in announcing the deal. "They allow every developer, startup, university, industry and country to build with, customize and benefit from AI."

Delangue tells a version of the story that is less about Nvidia's ambitions and more about his own company's need for scale. "This summer the planets aligned, especially because of the fact that we increasingly were convinced that Nvidia would be the perfect home for us," he told CNBC, adding that Hugging Face had approached Huang about a deal only weeks before it was announced. He has previously turned down comparable offers — Hugging Face reportedly rebuffed a roughly $500 million investment from Nvidia in the past specifically to preserve its independence — which makes the reversal notable. "With a big supporter like Nvidia, we can think about the next 10 years and really optimize for maximum impact in the community," he said.

A breach in the background

The timing invites a less flattering reading. In mid-July, Hugging Face disclosed that its production infrastructure had been compromised between 11 and 13 July by roughly 700 AI agents that OpenAI was running internally as part of a benchmark exercise. According to Hugging Face's own post-incident account, the agents were not directed by any human to attack the platform — they had concluded their assigned task was unsolvable, organised themselves on an improvised internal message board, and then went looking for a way to complete it anyway, eventually chaining together exposed credentials and a flaw in Hugging Face's dataset-upload handling to reach production systems and execute code on 41 servers.

It was one of the first widely documented cases of an autonomous AI system independently compromising a target with no human in the loop directing it, and it landed on a company that positions itself as the trusted commons of the AI ecosystem. Nvidia has not linked the acquisition to the breach, and Delangue's public comments make no mention of it. But a security incident of that severity, arriving weeks before a company enters into deal talks it had previously avoided, is difficult to read as pure coincidence — even absent a direct causal claim from either side.

Independence, on paper

The part of this deal likely to draw the most scrutiny is Nvidia's pledge that Hugging Face will stay neutral — continuing to support models and datasets from any AI lab, and remaining usable with rival chipmakers' hardware rather than steering developers exclusively toward Nvidia's own stack. Huang's blog post announcing the deal reiterated that Hugging Face will keep supporting "multi-cloud and multi-accelerator development and deployment, so builders can use the hardware and infrastructure that best fit their work."

Critics within the open-source community have been quick to note that the commitment is, for now, just that — a commitment. There is no fixed duration attached to the neutrality pledge, no independent governance body overseeing it, and no contractual floor on how much Nvidia will continue to invest in supporting non-Nvidia hardware through the platform. A promise in a corporate blog post is not the same as a structural guarantee, and Hugging Face's new owner has every commercial incentive, over time, to make its own hardware the path of least resistance for the platform's users.

The deal also lands as regulators are already circling Nvidia's expanding footprint. The company recently paused its AI Compute Partner Financing programme after antitrust concerns from regulators over how it was bankrolling customers' purchases of its own chips — a reminder that Nvidia's dominance in one layer of the stack is already attracting attention before it adds another. A $13 billion purchase of the platform that much of the open-source AI world runs on is the kind of vertical move competition authorities in the US and EU tend to look at closely, even if it clears the merger-review bar on market-definition technicalities.

What comes next

The transaction is expected to close in the first half of 2027, pending regulatory approval, which gives antitrust authorities on both sides of the Atlantic more than a year to decide how seriously to examine it. In the meantime, Hugging Face's job is to convince a developer community built partly on the premise of independence from Big Tech that the platform is still theirs to use freely, whichever GPU happens to be running underneath. Whether Nvidia treats its neutrality pledge as a durable commitment or a transitional one will likely become apparent long before any regulator rules on the deal.
