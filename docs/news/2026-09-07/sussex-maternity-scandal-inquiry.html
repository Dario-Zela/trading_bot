Bereaved parents give evidence in Sussex maternity scandal review

Donna Ockenden, the midwife and maternity-safety reviewer now chairing the independent inquiry into University Hospitals Sussex. — Monika Niziol / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Bereaved families began giving evidence to Donna Ockenden's independent review into Sussex maternity deaths, with no findings or reforms yet due.

Susan Cacciacarro remembers the exact sensation of her daughter going still. "My belly just didn't feel right. I didn't feel any movements," she has said of the days before Chiara was delivered stillborn at nearly 37 weeks in 2021, at Worthing Hospital. This week she was among the bereaved parents giving evidence to the independent review into maternity and neonatal care at University Hospitals Sussex NHS Foundation Trust — a process that formally launched in Brighton on 6 September, a day after families met the review's chair, Donna Ockenden, in person for the first time.

"I felt robbed when we lost Chiara," Cacciacarro said. "I'm hoping this review will provide answers... We definitely don't want other families to go through this."

Ockenden, the midwife who led the landmark Shrewsbury and Telford inquiry to its final report in March 2022 and now also chairs reviews at Nottingham and Leeds hospital trusts, was appointed to the Sussex role by Health Secretary Wes Streeting on 15 April 2026. Her brief is expansive: more than 1,000 cases dating back to 2018, at a trust where a joint BBC and New Statesman investigation, published in February, found that at least 55 baby deaths between 2019 and 2023 might have been avoided with better care.

That investigation examined 227 internal hospital reviews and found 55 cases graded C or D — the trust's own classification for care that "may" or was "likely" to have affected the outcome. Reporters also identified missed opportunities in nine stillbirths in 2021–22, and raised concerns about a unit culture that favoured unassisted vaginal delivery even when risk signs — reduced fetal movement, monitoring lapses, poor communication between staff — went unheeded.

Families' testimony, not yet a verdict

Among those expected to give evidence are Eileen McCarthy and Noel German, whose son Walter died nine days after his birth at the Royal Sussex County Hospital in 2021. They are two of more than 60 bereaved families organised under the campaign group Truth for Our Babies, which pushed for more than a year for an independent inquiry before Streeting agreed to one. "We will proactively reach out to ensure that the voices of seldom-heard families are heard through this review," Ockenden said at the launch.

University Hospitals Sussex has not disputed the scale of the problem. Chief executive Dr Andy Heeps said the trust "welcomes" the review and is "committed to fully supporting the process," alongside changes already under way in the maternity unit. Those changes have not stopped the trust becoming the costliest in England for maternity negligence: it paid out £103.8 million in claims between 2021 and 2025, including £34.3 million in 2024/25 alone — the highest single-year total of any English trust. Sussex is also one of 14 trusts swept into the national maternity investigation Streeting announced in September 2025, after Nottingham, East Kent and Shrewsbury and Telford each produced their own reckonings in the years before.

What happens next is evidentiary rather than legislative. Ockenden's review has no fixed reporting date, and unlike the Shrewsbury inquiry — which took roughly six years from commissioning to final report — this one is still taking statements. Families and clinicians will continue to give evidence over the coming months, with the number of cases under review likely to grow as more parents come forward. No regulatory or funding decisions hinge on a specific date yet; the next marker to watch is whether Ockenden's team publishes interim findings, as she did partway through the Shrewsbury process, once the evidence-gathering phase closes.
