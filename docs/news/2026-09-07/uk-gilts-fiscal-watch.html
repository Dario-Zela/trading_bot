Gilt yields hold near 18-year highs as Healey insists economy is 'turning a corner'

Chancellor John Healey, who faces a shrinking fiscal headroom as gilt yields climb ahead of his 28 October Budget. — Lauren Hurley / Crown Copyright (Open Government Licence), via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
30-year gilts at 5.79% and 10-year near 5.15% show markets pricing UK fiscal risk beyond what BoE rate-hike bets alone justify, with a DMO auction due Thursday.

British government debt is being priced as though the public finances, not just inflation, are the problem. The 30-year gilt yield sat at 5.79% this week and the 10-year hovered near 5.15% — territory last visited in 2008, before the financial crisis had properly finished doing its damage. Chancellor John Healey, five weeks from his first Budget, says the economy is "turning a corner." The bond market has yet to agree.

An 18-year climb, and not a gentle one

Gilt yields spent early September pushing to fresh multi-year highs, with the 10-year touching 5.268% on 2 September — its highest since June 2008 — before easing modestly to around 5.15% by the end of the week as traders digested US jobs data. The 30-year yield, more sensitive to long-run fiscal worries than to the Bank of England's next move, has held near 5.79%, a level that puts Britain's borrowing costs among the highest in the G7.

What makes the move notable is the driver. Markets are now fully pricing a Bank of England rate hike by year-end, with a further increase pencilled in for March 2027 — Chief Economist Huw Pill has argued that tightening now reduces the risk of having to act more aggressively later, with inflation stoked by the fallout from the Iran war and the oil-price spike that has followed it. But rate expectations alone don't explain why 30-year paper — largely insulated from near-term policy moves — is trading at levels not seen since the late 1990s. That gap is the market's own verdict on fiscal sustainability, delivered independently of anything the Bank does.

Healey's headroom problem

Pantheon Macroeconomics puts a number on the damage: the rise in gilt yields has cut Healey's fiscal headroom to roughly £13bn, down from £23.6bn at the Spring Statement. To restore that cushion, economists reckon he needs to find upward of £11bn a year in tax rises or spending cuts — a considerably tougher ask than the one he inherited when Andy Burnham appointed him chancellor in July.

Healey's public response has been to separate the politics of growth from the arithmetic of the gilt market. "Fiscal credibility is indivisible from good growth," he has told audiences, arguing the two are not in tension despite what the numbers on a screen might suggest. In a speech setting out plans to devolve more economic power to regional leaders, he described Britain as "turning a corner," with businesses and communities "ready to seize the opportunities of new technologies" even against what a Treasury source called a "bleak global backdrop." It is a deliberately un-triumphant pitch — growth as something achievable through discipline, not a talking point independent of it — and one squarely aimed at reassuring a market that so far isn't buying it.

The test comes Thursday

The more immediate pressure point is supply. The Debt Management Office has a gilt auction scheduled for Thursday, and while the DMO has already trimmed its long-dated issuance plans — long conventional gilt sales are down to around £23bn, roughly 9% of this fiscal year's total, reflecting weaker structural demand from pension funds — any sign of soft bidding at current yields would sharpen the narrative that investors are demanding a higher premium to hold UK debt at all maturities. A clean auction would suggest the sell-off is a rates story that can normalise; a weak one would harden the fiscal-sustainability reading.

What it means beyond Whitehall

For the Bank, elevated long-end yields tighten financial conditions on their own, which narrows the room to manoeuvre on the year-end hike Pill has flagged — pushing too hard risks compounding a debt-servicing problem the Bank doesn't control. For sterling, the split between a hawkish central bank and a nervous gilt market is an unusual combination that traders will be watching for signs of currency stress rather than the more familiar rates-driven strength. And for Healey, the 28 October Budget now has to do two jobs at once: convince voters the corner really is being turned, and convince a market currently charging Britain 2008-crisis prices for the privilege of finding out. Thursday's auction, and whatever the 10-year does in response, will be the first read on which audience is winning.
