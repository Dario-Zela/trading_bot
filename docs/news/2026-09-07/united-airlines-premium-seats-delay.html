United sits on unused premium seats as delivery delays bite

A United Airlines Boeing 737 MAX; the larger MAX 10 variant central to United's premium transcontinental plans remains undelivered after a six-year delay. — Konstantin von Wedelstaedt / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
United is storing hundreds of lie-flat business-class seats built for a Boeing 737 MAX 10 that was due in 2020 and now isn't expected until summer 2027.

United Airlines has hundreds of business-class seats it cannot install anywhere. They were built for the Boeing 737 MAX 10, a jet the airline ordered in 2020 and still does not have.

In one sentence: United is sitting on hundreds of lie-flat premium seats built for a Boeing jet whose delivery has slipped from 2020 to summer 2027, forcing costly workarounds on its most profitable routes.

The MAX 10 is Boeing's largest 737 variant, and United is its biggest customer with 167 firm orders. It was meant to anchor a specific niche: premium transcontinental service between the West Coast and New York-area hubs, routes where corporate travelers pay a heavy premium for a lie-flat seat on what is otherwise a short-haul aircraft. United ordered the cabins to match. It just never got the plane.

Six years and counting

Boeing has now completed final certification flight testing on the MAX 10, logging 976 test flights and more than 2,000 flight hours before the last run on July 28. That clears a major hurdle, but certification itself — the FAA's sign-off on the paperwork behind those flights — is still pending, with Boeing targeting approval this autumn and United now expecting its first jets in the summer of 2027. Up to 20 could arrive by the end of that year. The original in-service target was 2020.

CEO Scott Kirby put the seat problem bluntly on CNBC: "We got a bunch of lie-flat seats that we don't know what to do with. They don't fit on other airplanes." That is the crux of it — premium seating is engineered to a specific airframe's floor pitch, power routing and door spacing, so a business-class suite built for a 737 cannot simply be bolted into an Airbus or a widebody. United ordered the hardware years ahead of the jet; the jet did not show up.

The workaround has its own price tag

Rather than let premium transcontinental capacity sit idle, United redirected the strategy toward its Airbus fleet, retrofitting 20 A321neo jets — branded "Coastliner" — with 20 Polaris lie-flat suites and 12 Premium Plus seats apiece. Roughly 50 are planned, with 40 expected in service by early 2028. It is a functional fix, but not a cheap one: the airline is now paying twice for premium capacity on the same routes — once for seats that remain in storage, and again for cabins retrofitted onto aircraft the MAX 10 was supposed to make unnecessary.

The timing is awkward. Delta has committed roughly $1 billion to expanding its Premium Select cabin and unifying premium configurations across its widebody fleet, while American Airlines has been widening its own premium-economy push to capture leisure travelers trading up. Across the industry, carriers are converting more of each cabin to premium seating because those seats generate disproportionate revenue per square foot — precisely the fight United is trying to stay in in with an idle aircraft order and stored inventory of hardware it cannot yet use.

What happens if the seats never fly

United has not said what it will do if the MAX 10 slips again, and Boeing's certification programs have a documented history of doing exactly that — the MAX 10 has already missed its original date by more than six years. A further delay would harden a decision United has so far treated as temporary: whether to keep paying storage costs on cabins built for a plane that hasn't arrived, or write off the mismatch and redesign the seats for aircraft it actually operates. Either choice concedes that the MAX 10's role in United's fleet plan was, at minimum, badly mistimed. The next marker to watch is the FAA's expected certification decision this autumn — the last major checkpoint before United either gets its jet or, once again, a new delivery date.
