Takeda's narcolepsy drug oveporexton hits all endpoints in Phase III

In one sentence
Takeda's oveporexton won FDA approval in August as Orzeyful, but DEA scheduling, pricing near $142,000 a year, and entrenched rivals will decide its market share.

Takeda's experimental narcolepsy pill has already cleared the hurdle its own headline suggests it's still clearing. Two Phase III trials completed last year showed the drug, oveporexton, produced statistically significant improvements over placebo across every measure of narcolepsy type 1 that regulators care about — daytime sleepiness, sudden muscle collapse, nighttime sleep quality. Since then, Takeda has taken that data to the U.S. Food and Drug Administration and, on August 6, won full approval. The drug now has a brand name, Orzeyful, and a place in FDA history as the first medicine designed to act on the biological cause of narcolepsy type 1 (NT1) rather than treat its symptoms one at a time.

For Takeda's shareholders, the easy part — trial wins, priority review, approval — is now behind the company. What isn't yet settled is whether Orzeyful takes meaningful share from a narcolepsy market that three other companies have spent a decade building, and how large a bill patients and insurers will be asked to pay.

What the trials showed

Narcolepsy type 1 is caused by the near-total loss of orexin-producing neurons in the hypothalamus; patients typically retain only 5–15% of the signalling that keeps the brain anchored in wakefulness, per researchers surveyed in Nature's review of the field. Oveporexton, known in development as TAK-861, is an oral agonist for the orexin 2 receptor. Rather than dampening histamine or dopamine pathways to force wakefulness by a side door, as older narcolepsy drugs do, it is designed to substitute directly for the signal patients have lost.

Two placebo-controlled trials, FirstLight (168 participants) and RadiantLight (105 participants), tested that idea across 19 countries, Takeda said when it announced topline results in July 2025. Both studies hit every primary and secondary endpoint at week 12, with p-values below 0.001 at every dose tested — gains on the Maintenance of Wakefulness Test, the Epworth Sleepiness Scale, and weekly cataplexy rate, with many patients reaching what the company called "near-normal" ranges. Data presented at the SLEEP 2026 conference in June added a cognitive dimension: roughly 70% of patients on oveporexton reported no significant cognitive difficulties, against about 15% on placebo. Takeda has reported no serious treatment-related adverse events; the most common side effects were insomnia and urinary urgency, and more than 95% of trial completers opted into a long-term extension study.

That clean safety record matters more here than it might elsewhere in Takeda's pipeline. The company's two earlier attempts at an orexin agonist both failed: TAK-994 was scrapped after signs of liver toxicity, and a second candidate, danavorexton, stalled on slow enrollment. Oveporexton is the company's third — and so far only successful — shot at a drug class it has pursued since at least the mid-2010s.

Approved, but not yet for sale

FDA approval does not mean Orzeyful is available. Because the drug acts on a receptor system tied to sleep-wake regulation, it still needs a scheduling decision from the Drug Enforcement Administration under the Controlled Substances Act before Takeda can ship it commercially. The company has said it expects that ruling within roughly 90 days of the August approval, which points to a plausible commercial launch around November. Pricing has not been disclosed, but analysts tracking the filing are modeling a gross annual price near $142,000 per patient, benchmarked against Jazz Pharmaceuticals' Xywav, with some arguing Orzeyful's distinct mechanism could justify a premium over that figure.

That benchmark points to the real question for traders. Narcolepsy is not an empty field waiting for a first mover. Jazz's sodium-oxybate franchise — Xywav and the older Xyrem — generated more than $2 billion in the past year; Harmony Biosciences' Wakix, a histamine-pathway drug approved in 2019, is tracking toward $1.0–1.04 billion in 2026 revenue; Alkermes' Lumryz, folded in with this year's Avadel acquisition, is expected to bring in roughly $335 million; and Axsome's Sunosi trails at about $125 million. Every one of those drugs carries years of prescriber familiarity and insurance formulary placement that a newly approved entrant has to dislodge one appointment at a time.

Takeda has pegged Orzeyful's own peak-sales potential at $2–3 billion a year, against an estimated 120,000 diagnosed NT1 patients in the U.S. Jefferies analyst Stephen Barker has floated a similar $3 billion ceiling. Both figures assume a pace of switching from established therapies, and a degree of insurer cooperation on prior authorization, that no clinical trial can actually demonstrate.

What's next

The next hard catalyst is the DEA's scheduling decision, expected around November, which will fix both the real launch date and, once Takeda discloses a list price, the first genuine test of how Orzeyful is positioned against Xywav and Wakix. After that, the figures worth watching are formulary wins and new-prescription counts through the first two quarters of commercial availability — not the trial results, which are now more than a year old, but what happens once doctors and payers decide where oveporexton actually fits.
