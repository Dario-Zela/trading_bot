Iran deal optimism drives oil and gas lower, lifts luxury and emerging-market assets, splits UK defence sector

The Strait of Hormuz separating Iran from the Musandam Peninsula — closed to commercial shipping since 4 March 2026 — NASA/MODIS — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A US-Iran draft accord to reopen Hormuz within 30 days sent Brent down 3.8% to $86.98 and sparked a sharp rotation into luxury stocks and EM assets — but no deal has been signed yet.

Oil fell more than 3% on Friday, European natural gas followed, luxury stocks across the continent snapped back sharply, and the UK's two dominant defence names went in opposite directions — all because Iranian state media published the text of a draft accord it said Tehran and Washington had largely agreed.

Iran restores Hormuz shipping within 30 days; Brent crude drops 3.8% to $86.98 as markets begin to price out the blockade premium — but the deal has not been signed.

The chokepoint price

The Strait of Hormuz has been closed to commercial shipping since March 4, when Iranian forces declared the waterway off-limits and began attacking vessels attempting to transit it. The strait carries roughly 20% of the world's seaborne oil and liquefied natural gas, and its closure installed a durable geopolitical risk premium in crude benchmarks through the spring.

That premium began to deflate in earnest on Friday morning, after Iranian state media published what it described as a 14-point draft memorandum of understanding. Under the reported terms, Tehran would restore Hormuz shipping to pre-war levels within 30 days in exchange for a US military withdrawal from the Gulf and the lifting of its naval blockade. Brent crude futures fell 3.78% to close at $86.98 a barrel — an eight-week low. West Texas Intermediate shed 3.85% to $84.33. The announcement timing, just ahead of the G7 summit that opens in Evian on June 15, was not accidental: both sides appear to be managing expectations toward a formal signing around or during the gathering.

European natural gas tracked oil lower. The Dutch TTF front-month contract — the benchmark that most directly feeds into euro-area energy inflation — fell toward €46.20 per megawatt hour, extending a slide that first emerged in late May when early deal signals began to circulate. The direction of travel matters as much as the level: sustained TTF weakness feeds into central bank models for medium-term price pressures, and analysts at several European houses noted on Friday that a deal closing the Hormuz file would materially soften the inflation assumptions underlying rate-cut projections for the second half of the year.

On supply arithmetic, Iran historically produced between 3.5 and 4 million barrels per day at full capacity. Analysts estimate that a sanctions-relief path could add 500,000 to 1 million barrels per day of newly accessible supply to world markets over the following twelve months. The more immediate effect is the anticipated release of an estimated 100 million barrels of crude currently sitting in tankers stranded in the Gulf since the blockade began — a figure markets began pricing Friday as a near-term ceiling on any oil rebound.

From risk premium to risk appetite

Lower energy costs compress inflation expectations. Lower inflation expectations ease the discount rate across risk assets. The rotation was visible and rapid.

European luxury stocks, which had taken heavy losses since late February when Gulf stores shuttered and Middle East tourism collapsed, reversed sharply. LVMH and Kering each gained around 5%; Richemont, with denser exposure to high-end Gulf jewellery retail, added 3.4%. The region accounts for roughly 6% of total global luxury revenues by most estimates — modest in aggregate, but it was growing faster than China or the United States before the conflict began. LVMH chairman Bernard Arnault said in April that a resolution would produce a sharp reversal; Friday looked like the opening chapter of that trade. Whether the full thesis holds depends on how quickly Iranian consumer spending recovers once sanctions ease, which no one can model with confidence yet.

Emerging-market assets also firmed. The MSCI Emerging Markets index had already recovered most of its war-related losses through May and early June, but Gulf sovereign credit spreads compressed further on Friday and Turkish assets outperformed — a pattern consistent with a specific regional risk-premium unwind rather than a broad macro bid. A Reuters report that the UAE had released a first tranche of roughly $3 billion in previously frozen Iranian assets — as part of the broader sanctions-relief architecture — briefly amplified the move, before the UAE's Foreign Affairs Ministry issued a denial. The contradiction itself told a story: the architecture is live, contested, and moving faster than official channels can manage.

Defence: choosing platforms

A geopolitical risk repricing is not costless across all sectors. For the UK's defence names, Friday produced a visible split.

Rolls-Royce Holdings gained 4.8%. Investors appear to have concluded that reduced Middle East conflict intensity leaves the group's civilian aerospace recovery and small modular reactor programme intact, and that any regional peace dividend could accelerate certain procurement programmes elsewhere. BAE Systems, by contrast, edged up only around 1%. The divergence maps to a specific debate: GCC defence buyers are expected to reassess force-structure priorities in a post-blockade environment, and the platform categories under most scrutiny — air-defence systems procured during the acute conflict phase — overlap significantly with BAE's order pipeline. Naval and undersea capability faces a different calculus, and investors appear to be sorting at the platform level rather than treating UK defence as a monolithic block.

The same rotation logic suppressed US healthcare and biotech names late in the session. Defensive sectors ceded ground to energy and consumer cyclicals as the session's risk-on tone consolidated.

What to watch

The market is long a deal that has not been formally executed. Trump administration officials put the probability of a signing at around 80% before or during the Evian G7. Iran's foreign minister has urged caution about treating any published draft text as final; Tehran's hardline establishment has said nothing publicly to endorse the reported terms; and Israel has signalled opposition throughout the week.

The 30-day Hormuz reopening clock starts from a signature that has not yet arrived. If the G7 summit ends without a formal agreement — or if Iran's domestic politics slow ratification — the energy premium that unwound on Friday will partially rebuild, and the luxury and EM trades that followed it will reverse with comparable speed. Traders who went long cyclicals and short defensives on Friday are, in effect, holding a position on diplomatic execution. The Evian communiqué, expected around June 17, is the first hard test of whether that bet is correctly sized.
