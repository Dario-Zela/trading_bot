China signals review of rare earth export curbs amid US trade tensions

Mountain Pass rare earth mine in California — one of the few significant non-Chinese rare earth operations in the Western hemisphere — Ken Lund / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
China offered to review rare earth export curbs in US trade talks, but controls stay live — and Europe's battery and defence chains remain acutely exposed.

Beijing has signalled it is willing to review its controls on rare earth exports as part of ongoing trade negotiations with Washington — a diplomatic gesture that falls well short of structural concession but is enough to move commodity markets and expose just how exposed European and UK supply chains remain to Chinese mineral policy.

China's Ministry of Commerce, following talks earlier this month, said it would "jointly study solutions" on rare earth shortages and cooperate with the US on "reasonable" concerns. The language is careful by design. China's April 2025 licensing regime — covering seven heavy rare earths including dysprosium, terbium, yttrium and scandium — remains fully in force. A second tranche of October 2025 controls, with extraterritorial provisions applying to any product containing more than 0.1 percent Chinese-origin content, is suspended until November 10, 2026, a deadline that now functions as the market's most watched regulatory clock.

China's leverage, by the numbers

The arithmetic of dependency explains why Washington is at the table at all. China controls roughly 70 percent of global rare earth mining, 90 percent of separation and processing, and 93 percent of permanent magnet manufacturing. For heavy rare earths — the subset critical to the high-performance motors in EVs and the guidance systems of precision weapons — the monopoly is near-total. Outside China, the key question for dysprosium is no longer price but availability. Dysprosium is trading up roughly 105 percent year-to-date; terbium oxide commands five times its Chinese domestic price in ex-China spot markets.

The April 2025 controls demonstrated the instrument's potency quickly. Magnet exports fell by three-quarters within months of the controls taking effect, and several European carmakers were forced to halt production lines. Yttrium shipments to the US dropped from 333 metric tonnes in the pre-controls period to 17 metric tonnes in the eight months after.

Europe's structural exposure

For the UK and EU, the diplomatic manoeuvre between Beijing and Washington is a reminder that critical mineral policy is being shaped by a bilateral relationship they are not party to. Europe sources an estimated 98 percent of its rare-earth magnet demand from Chinese suppliers. More than 80 percent of large European firms sit no more than three supply-chain intermediaries from a Chinese rare-earth producer, according to European Central Bank analysis.

When China tightened export licences in 2025, European automotive and defence manufacturers felt the squeeze before any political response was possible. EU recycling initiatives — targeting 20 percent of annual permanent magnet requirements from recovered material by 2030 — are a credible long-run hedge, but industrial timelines stretch into the next decade. Rebuilding independent rare earth separation capacity is a 20-to-30-year project by most assessments.

For market participants tracking commodity exposure, the November 2026 deadline carries more weight than any diplomatic communiqué. If China lets the suspension of Wave 2 controls lapse without renewal, extraterritorial licensing requirements would trigger across global supply chains simultaneously. That is the date to watch — not the next round of trade talks.
