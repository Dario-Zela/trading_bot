Air India seeks to defer hundreds of Airbus and Boeing deliveries, straining both manufacturers

An Air India Boeing 787-8 Dreamliner — the type at the centre of both the carrier's 2023 order ambitions and its June 2025 fatal accident at Ahmedabad. — Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA 2.0
In one sentence
Air India is seeking to defer up to 500 of its ~600 ordered jets, pressuring Airbus's production ramp-up and Boeing's cash-flow recovery narrative.

Air India has approached Airbus and Boeing seeking to defer delivery of as many as 500 of the roughly 600 aircraft it has on order, according to people familiar with the matter cited by Bloomberg — a move that would push billions of dollars in handover payments well into the 2030s and scramble the production arithmetic at both manufacturers.

The negotiations, which emerged on June 12, cover jets from a combined backlog assembled between 2023 and 2025. Air India, in a statement, called the claims "highly speculative" and said it remained committed to fleet modernisation and its long-term transformation strategy. Airbus and Boeing did not respond to requests for comment. But the numbers in play are large enough that neither manufacturer can treat the conversation as routine scheduling.

Tata pulls the handbrake

The pressure for deferrals originates with Tata Group, which took control of Air India in January 2022 and holds a 74.9% stake. The group spent 2023 and early 2024 executing one of commercial aviation's most ambitious expansion plays — the 470-jet order signed in February 2023 was, at the time, the largest in industry history, spanning 210 A320neo-family aircraft, 40 Airbus A350s, 190 Boeing 737 Max jets, 20 787 Dreamliners and 10 777X widebodies. A follow-on 100-plane order with Airbus came in December 2024, bringing total committed exposure to roughly 600 frames.

What changed was the accumulation of bad luck and bad economics at strikingly similar speed. Air India has run up losses exceeding ₹55,000 crore (approximately $5.8 billion) since the Tata acquisition; the annual burn runs at roughly $3 billion. The financial year ending March 2026 looks set to be the worst yet, with projected losses of nearly ₹28,000 crore.

A series of operational blows compounded the structural problem. Pakistan closed its airspace to Indian carriers — a closure that has now endured for well over a year — adding hours to North Atlantic routes and ceding competitive ground to European and Gulf carriers unaffected by the restriction. Air India Flight 171, a Boeing 787, crashed on takeoff from Ahmedabad in June 2025, killing 241 of the 242 on board and 19 on the ground; investigators have yet to publish a final report. The airline subsequently suspended long-haul services during the most acute phase of the Iran conflict, further haemorrhaging yield and market share — the Delhi–New York route, once 17 hours, now stretches close to 22 hours via Rome, while American Airlines still flies the same city pair in roughly 16.

With deliveries concentrated in 2027 and 2028 — and airlines typically paying roughly 80% of the purchase price on handover — deferral is the most immediate lever for liquidity management. Tata's instruction to its airline subsidiary is unambiguous: the growth chapter is paused.

Two manufacturers, one headache

Airbus and Boeing receive the news in very different positions, but neither position is comfortable.

Airbus is midway through one of the most complex production ramp-ups in its history. The target is 75 A320-family aircraft per month by 2027 — up from a rate already strained by Pratt & Whitney engine shortfalls and fuselage supply delays. In the first quarter of 2026 the company managed just 114 deliveries across all programmes, a 16% decline year-on-year and the weakest quarterly performance since 2009. Against that backdrop, its full-year target of around 870 deliveries depends on every major customer absorbing aircraft roughly on schedule. Air India's deferral requests arrive when Airbus is counting frames most carefully.

Airbus chief executive Guillaume Faury said recently that he was not seeing demand for outright cancellations — a distinction worth holding. Deferrals are not cancellations; they keep aircraft on the books, slot payments alive, and the backlog headline intact. But they disrupt the cash flow arithmetic. An aircraft deferred two years is revenue not recognised when the production ramp most needs it.

Boeing's situation is structurally different. The 737 Max programme is accelerating — the FAA authorised Boeing to lift output to 47 aircraft per month this spring — and the company is targeting positive free cash flow in the second half of 2026, reversing years of cash burn. Its commercial backlog has swelled to a record $695 billion, boosted by a 200-plane deal with Chinese carriers. Against that scale, deferrals from one Tier-1 carrier are manageable in isolation. The risk is narrative: Boeing's recovery pitch rests on the credibility of its order book. If Air India's move encourages similar conversations elsewhere — and analysts will be watching Tier-2 carriers across Asia and the Middle East — the backlog's apparent solidity becomes harder to assert in investor calls.

What the market should watch

The critical question for investors is whether Air India's request is idiosyncratic — a carrier facing unusually severe financial and operational pressures — or an early signal of a broader capacity correction. The post-pandemic order wave was built on assumptions of sustained traffic growth; three years of supply-chain chaos, elevated interest rates and geopolitical disruption have eroded airline margins faster than either side modelled in 2023.

Watch Airbus (EADSY) delivery guidance for any revision to the 870 target at its next update, and Boeing (BA) free cash flow trajectory in its second-quarter report, due in late July. Any language about deferrals spreading across the customer base — or, conversely, confirmation that Air India negotiations have been quietly resolved — will move both stocks. An Iran deal approaching signature and a potential Hormuz reopening could ease some of the route economics that are squeezing Indian carriers, but that relief, if it comes, would take quarters to flow through to Air India's order commitment calculus.
