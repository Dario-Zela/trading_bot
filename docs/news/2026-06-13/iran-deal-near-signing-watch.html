Iran nuclear deal days from signing as Hormuz blockade set to lift; Israel signals opposition

The Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly one-fifth of global oil supply flows — closed since March 2026 after Iran blocked passage following US and Israeli strikes. — NASA/MODIS via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The US and Iran are days from signing a deal that would end the Hormuz blockade and commit Tehran to abandoning nuclear weapons, though Israel opposes the terms and a 60-day technical negotiation follows any signing.

An agreement between the United States and Iran that would end a three-month war, reopen the Strait of Hormuz, and commit Tehran to abandoning nuclear weapons development is closer to being signed than at any point since hostilities began in late February. Senior US officials said Friday they were "80-85%" confident a deal would be formalised within days, and Pakistan's Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif confirmed that "a final, agreed upon text" had been reached, with his government "working closely with both sides to finalise the next steps."

President Trump had already told reporters he expected to sign in Europe over the weekend — one of roughly 40 times since March that he has announced a deal was imminent. Iran's foreign ministry cautioned that "nothing has been finalised" and that "the Americans kept changing their positions," but Foreign Minister Abbas Araqchi separately signalled that a digital signing could occur "in the next few days" — a more concrete formulation than Tehran has offered before.

What the deal would involve

The framework that has emerged from mediation led by Pakistan, Qatar and Turkey is a memorandum of understanding rather than a full treaty. Its central planks: Iran would commit to a 15-to-20-year freeze on uranium enrichment and the dismantling of enrichment infrastructure; existing stockpiles — Iran holds close to 1,000 pounds of uranium enriched to 60%, enough material for roughly ten weapons according to international inspectors — would be downblended to fuel-grade concentrations and converted into reactor fuel in a process Oman's foreign minister described as "irreversible." In exchange, the US would lift its naval blockade and oil sanctions, the Strait of Hormuz would reopen within 30 days, and roughly $24 billion in frozen Iranian assets would be released.

That last point matters for markets as much as the nuclear headline. Oil prices have risen approximately 40% since Iran closed the strait in early March; Brent fell more than 5% and US crude more than 8% in a single week in May on the first credible signs of a breakthrough. A confirmed signing would trigger further sharp moves in energy markets, compress Middle East risk premiums that have supported defence equities, and — given the Hormuz route's centrality to Gulf crude flows — reconfigure correlations that algorithmic strategies have been running against stagflation and supply-shock scenarios for months.

Israel's divergence

The deal's most conspicuous absence is Israel, which is not a signatory and has made clear it regards the current terms as insufficient. Netanyahu's office told Trump it could accept an agreement only if it included the physical removal of enriched material from Iranian territory, the full dismantling of enrichment infrastructure, limits on ballistic missile production, and an end to Iran's funding of regional proxies — conditions that go beyond what Tehran has offered.

Araqchi, for his part, said publicly that Israel was "actively working against" a nuclear agreement. Washington's response was calibrated: senior officials said they were confident Israeli objections would be overcome before any signing, without specifying how. The divergence is real, not rhetorical. Israel has a direct security interest in ensuring Iran's nuclear latency is eliminated rather than merely paused, and Netanyahu's government has shown no sign of endorsing a memorandum that defers the hardest verification questions to a subsequent 60-day technical negotiation.

Whether US assurances to Jerusalem are sufficient — or whether Israel could take independent action to derail talks — is the thread to watch in the days before any ceremony.

What comes next

Even a signed memorandum would open, not close, the substantive negotiations. A 60-day technical window follows, during which inspectors, sanctions lawyers and weapons experts would attempt to translate broad commitments into verifiable schedules and enforcement mechanisms. The history of Iran nuclear diplomacy — from the 2015 JCPOA through the 2025 European talks that collapsed in February — suggests that gap between framework and finalised agreement is where deals tend to unravel.

The specific catalyst to monitor: whether a signing ceremony materialises before Sunday night European time, as Trump has suggested. If it does not, the question shifts to whether Iran's "final deliberations" are genuine or tactical, and whether Trump's patience — already strained by weeks of false starts — will hold. The oil market, which has priced in partial probability of a deal for several weeks, will move on the signing itself; the size of that move will depend on how much of the Hormuz reopening timeline is confirmed in the text.
