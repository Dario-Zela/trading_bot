Rate-cut hopes trimmed globally as US inflation lifts gilt yields and narrows UK fiscal headroom

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, facing its narrowest window for rate cuts in the current cycle — Geograph / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
US consumer prices hit a three-year high of 4.2%, pushing UK gilt yields toward 5% and squeezing the OBR's £23.6bn fiscal headroom as BoE rate cuts are fully priced out.

For most of the first quarter, investors had allowed themselves a cautious optimism. US headline inflation held at 2.4% in January and February. The Federal Reserve's easing cycle seemed intact. Rate cuts were pencilled into calendars from Washington to Threadneedle Street. Then came the Iran war, the Strait of Hormuz, and a sequence of monthly CPI prints that have rewritten the global monetary policy calendar, one data point at a time.

Tuesday's Bureau of Labor Statistics report — May CPI at 4.2%, its highest since April 2023 — did not technically surprise. Both the 0.5% monthly gain and the 4.2% annual rate arrived on consensus. But in a market calibrating direction of travel as much as magnitude, the absence of a downside surprise is itself a verdict: inflation is not misbehaving, it is performing exactly as an energy shock, compounding month by month, is supposed to. The trajectory is unambiguous: 2.4% in February, 3.3% in March, 3.8% in April, 4.2% in May. Energy drove more than 60% of the monthly increase — gasoline alone up 40.5% year-on-year, and 7% in May — the legacy of sustained disruption to Hormuz shipping lanes. CME FedWatch now prices better than a 70% probability of at least one Fed rate hike before year-end.

A shock that crosses the Atlantic

US 10-year Treasury yields have settled around 4.55%, and the carry-over into UK gilts has been direct. Ten-year gilt yields, which spent much of the spring trading between 4.25% and 4.5%, have risen to approximately 4.94% — close to their highest since late May — as capital pricing recalibrates across developed markets. The mechanism is well-worn: higher US yields draw capital toward dollar assets, pressuring other major sovereign bond markets to reprice upward to compete. In gilts, that global gravity is compounded by a domestic fiscal position that leaves the UK more exposed than most.

For the Bank of England, the arithmetic is uncomfortable. Its current Bank Rate sits at 3.75%, the product of four cuts delivered through 2025. As recently as February, the MPC voted 5-4 to hold, with four members still favouring further easing. That calculus has shifted sharply since. By early June, market probability of a cut at the June 18 meeting stood at approximately 4%, down from the 86% probability attached to what once looked like a plausible March move. Some positioning has gone further, pricing the possibility of rate hikes — a complete reversal from where the BoE sat six months ago.

Andrew Bailey's committee faces a structural constraint familiar to central banks caught in energy-price crossfire: with gilt yields already elevated by global spillovers, any signal of renewed easing risks steepening the curve further, weakening sterling, and embedding the inflationary pressures the Bank is attempting to moderate. The room for manoeuvre has narrowed considerably.

The fiscal vice tightens

Gilt yields do not merely constrain monetary policy — they carry a direct and measurable cost to the Treasury. The OBR's March spring statement projected fiscal headroom of £23.6bn against the balanced current budget rule by 2029/30, a figure produced when 10-year gilt yields were in the 4.25–4.5% range. At around 4.94%, yields now sit approximately 0.4 to 0.5 percentage points above that baseline.

The OBR has been explicit about the sensitivity: the government's fiscal headroom would be effectively eliminated by a 0.6-percentage-point rise in the BoE base rate. The rule of thumb on gilt yields is similarly sobering — every 100 basis points of sustained increase costs the government approximately £10bn annually in debt service by 2030. Full-year 2025/26 borrowing came in at £132bn, the fourth-highest April-to-March figure since 1993. March borrowing alone arrived £2.1bn above the OBR forecast. The £23.6bn headline headroom figure is beginning to look thinner against those inputs.

The Treasury has not issued formal guidance in response to the recent yield movements. Stephen Millard of the National Institute of Economic and Social Research was less circumspect following the spring statement: "The Chancellor had an opportunity to commit to the importance of reducing the debt-to-GDP ratio. This opportunity was missed." NIESR separately projected that if net migration falls to zero from 2030, the fiscal deficit would worsen by roughly 1% of GDP by 2040 — a reminder that the structural risks run considerably deeper than any single month's gilt movement.

In the mortgage market, the repricing is already tangible. Swap rates — the instruments that underpin fixed-rate mortgage pricing — have risen in lockstep with gilt yields. Approximately 1.8 million fixed-rate deals are due for renewal in 2026.

The global rate cycle in reverse

The BoE's predicament is shared. The ECB, which entered 2026 with further easing broadly anticipated, delivered a 25-basis-point hike at its June meeting — its first increase since 2023 — after revising euro-area inflation forecasts upward to 3.0% for this year, from 2.6%. The driving force is identical: energy-price contagion from the Middle East conflict, which has pushed oil toward $80 per barrel and more than doubled European wholesale gas prices since the start of the year.

Japan presents the starkest divergence. The yen has fallen to multi-month lows as US yields widen the spread against Japanese equivalents, leaving the Bank of Japan pinned in a policy bind of its own. The dollar's renewed strength — product of a Fed on hold with hikes now partially priced — compounds the difficulty for any central bank navigating the same geopolitical energy shock while meeting a domestic price-stability mandate.

What to watch

Two rate decisions in quick succession will test current assumptions. The Federal Reserve meets on 17 June; markets price a 97% probability of a hold, but Chair Jerome Powell's forward guidance will be closely read for any signal on the pace of any eventual resumption of easing. The Bank of England follows on 18 June, with its MPC broadly expected to hold at 3.75% — though the composition of the vote and any shift in language around the pace of future cuts will be scrutinised against elevated gilt yields and a government fiscal position carrying materially less room than it held in March.

The underlying variable is oil. The Iran nuclear talks, reported to be in their final stages, carry the potential to reopen Hormuz and begin unwinding the energy premium embedded in global CPI. Until that happens — or until US core inflation shows a convincing deceleration independent of energy — the window for rate cuts, at Threadneedle Street, Frankfurt, or Washington, remains firmly shut.
