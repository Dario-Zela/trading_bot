BP seeks co-investors for Gulf of Mexico deepwater project amid ongoing capital constraints

BP's Mad Dog spar platform in the Gulf of Mexico — one of the company's operated production hubs in the same deepwater Paleogene play as Kaskida and Tiber. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
BP is marketing stakes of up to 50% in Kaskida and Tiber-Guadalupe to reduce $25bn in net debt while keeping operatorship of its flagship Gulf deepwater projects.

BP has begun marketing minority stakes in its two flagship Gulf of Mexico deepwater projects — Kaskida and Tiber-Guadalupe — in a farm-down process that underlines how stretched the British major's balance sheet remains even as global energy prices sit at elevated levels.

According to people familiar with the matter, BP could sell as much as 50% in each project, with both assets currently 100% BP-owned. Deliberations are at an early stage and a formal auction process could kick off later this year. The company declined to comment.

Two $5bn bets, one capital problem

Both projects sit in the Paleogene geological formation roughly 250 miles southwest of New Orleans — a high-pressure, technically demanding play where BP claims to have identified the equivalent of 10 billion barrels of discovered resources. Kaskida, in 5,860 feet of water, received US regulatory approval in March and is slated to begin producing around 80,000 barrels of oil per day by 2029. Tiber-Guadalupe, a separate hub, reached final investment decision last September and targets first oil in 2030 at similar rates. Combined capital outlay across the two projects runs to roughly $10bn.

That figure alone explains the logic of finding partners. BP's net debt rose to $25.3bn at the end of the first quarter of 2026, up from $22.2bn at year-end 2024, driven by lower operating cash flows and Gulf spill-related settlement payments of approximately $1.6bn pre-tax this year alone. The board suspended share buybacks earlier in 2026 and cut capital expenditure guidance to the lower end of its range, targeting a return to a debt band of $14-18bn through a $20bn divestment programme running to 2027.

By farming down, BP retains operatorship — and thus production-target credit — while transferring a material share of development spending to a partner. The company has said it aims to lift US upstream output to around one million barrels of oil equivalent per day by 2030, with offshore Gulf production accounting for more than 400,000 boe/d of that figure. Giving up equity in Kaskida and Tiber is the price of keeping that ambition alive without blowing the balance-sheet repair.

Meg O'Neill, who became BP's chief executive in April — the company's first external appointment to the top job in over a century — has framed the strategic pivot as building a "simpler, stronger, more valuable" company. Her predecessor Murray Auchincloss, who oversaw both project sanctions, had already signalled openness to deal-making, noting BP would welcome a "knockout bid" on a 25% Kaskida stake.

The timing question

The sale process launches against an ambiguous commodity backdrop. Oil prices surged more than 40% in the first half of 2026, partly on tensions in the Strait of Hormuz — but an Iran nuclear agreement now close to signing has begun unwinding that premium, with Brent and WTI both tracking lower this week as supply-disruption risk fades. For BP, that creates a narrow window: valuations for deep long-cycle assets are tied to long-run price assumptions rather than spot, but a sustained oil selloff would still dampen appetite among potential co-investors.

Shell's concurrent exit from offshore wind has redirected attention back to oil-and-gas fundamentals across the sector, and the Paleogene acreage is regarded as among the highest-quality remaining unpartnered inventory in the US Gulf. That scarcity value may prove BP's strongest negotiating card as it tests the market.
