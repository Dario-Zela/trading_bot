China cuts bank reserve requirements in fresh push to revive sluggish domestic demand

The People's Bank of China's headquarters on Beijing Financial Street, inaugurated in 2021 — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The PBOC cut commercial bank reserve requirements for the fourth time in two years, injecting ~1 trillion yuan as Beijing battles 32 consecutive months of falling home prices and near-zero consumer inflation.

The People's Bank of China moved again on Thursday to push more cash into its financial system, announcing a fresh cut to the reserve requirement ratio — the share of deposits commercial banks must hold idle rather than lend. The reduction, which takes effect later this month, is expected to release roughly 1 trillion yuan (approximately $138 billion) in long-term liquidity, according to figures consistent with similar moves in recent quarters. For a central bank that has already cut the same ratio twice within the past year, the announcement is less a surprise than a confirmation: Beijing believes the domestic demand problem is not going away on its own.

The RRR now stands at an average of roughly 6.3 percent across major commercial banks — historically low, and with limited room to fall much further. PBOC governor Pan Gongsheng said in January that "there is still room for further RRR and interest rate cuts this year," a formulation that has since become something of a mantra from Jinrong Jie, Beijing's financial district. Thursday's move converts that language into action.

The twin drags

The structural diagnosis underneath the cut is familiar and, after several years, increasingly hard to dispute. China's property sector — once the engine room of domestic investment, government land revenue, and household wealth — remains mired in a correction that shows little sign of completing on Beijing's preferred timeline.

New home prices across China's 70 major cities fell 3.2 percent year-on-year in February, the thirty-second consecutive monthly decline. S&P Global Ratings expects primary home prices to drop a further 1.5 to 2.5 percent over 2026 as a whole, with secondary market prices off by as much as 5 percent. Inventory sits approximately 45 percent above pre-downturn averages, suppressing the price recovery that would normally follow a period of policy support. Primary home sales are forecast to fall another 10 to 14 percent this year.

The IMF has urged China to "hasten property sector recovery" through direct government financing, arguing that market-led stabilisation alone cannot absorb the overhang at a pace consistent with healthy growth. Beijing has responded with incremental support — relaxing purchase restrictions, cutting mortgage rates, expanding the urban redevelopment programme — but has stopped well short of a comprehensive state buyout of unsold inventory.

The consumption side is, if anything, more discouraging. Retail sales growth in China barely exceeded 1 percent in year-on-year terms heading into 2026, and sequential momentum has been negative. Consumer price inflation was essentially flat in 2025, touching 0.0 percent for the annual average before a modest uptick to 1.2 percent in April 2026 — still far below levels that would indicate a healthy domestic demand cycle. Falling property values feed the malaise directly: households that have watched their most significant asset depreciate for nearly three years are not natural candidates for discretionary spending.

The policy calculus

Why a reserve ratio cut rather than a more direct instrument? The RRR operates as a blunt lever: releasing reserves cheapens the marginal cost of bank lending and increases the pool of money chasing borrowers, without requiring the PBOC to pick sectoral winners through targeted facilities. In an environment where credit demand is itself weak — businesses and households deleveraging rather than borrowing — the transmission mechanism is imperfect at best. Cheaper money only stimulates if someone wants to borrow it.

That limitation is not lost on policymakers. The January 2026 central bank work conference acknowledged that monetary stimulus would need to be complemented by fiscal expansion and structural reform to generate durable demand. China's 2026 budget has expanded the deficit to its widest level in years, with infrastructure and social spending the primary channels. The RRR cut provides liquidity headroom for the banks financing that fiscal push.

The pattern of cuts also carries a signal of its own. Each successive easing move — the PBOC has cut the RRR in both February and September 2024, again in May 2025, and now in June 2026 — narrows the distance between the stated optimism in official communiqués and the implied urgency of the policy response. China's economy is officially projected to grow 4.4 percent this year, down from 5.0 percent in 2025. Analysts at Rhodium Group and the World Bank have placed the structural floor somewhat lower, particularly if property stabilisation takes another one to two years, as S&P suggests it might.

What markets are watching

For investors beyond China's borders, the PBOC's recourse to successive easings carries a set of implications that extend well past the yuan's daily fixing.

Commodity markets are the most direct transmission channel. Iron ore and copper — both heavily leveraged to Chinese construction and manufacturing — have been on alert for Beijing policy signals throughout 2026. Iron ore prices were supported earlier this year when Beijing announced a broad fiscal stimulus package in January, with the May 2026 contract on China's Dalian Commodity Exchange briefly pushing above 822 yuan per metric ton. Copper, meanwhile, has had a strong year driven partly by supply disruptions and partly by persistent expectations of Chinese infrastructure-led demand. A further loosening of liquidity conditions feeds that narrative, even if the property sector specifically generates less new steel demand than it once did.

LNG markets and broader emerging-market asset flows are more sensitive to the growth trajectory signal than to the raw liquidity injection. If Beijing's successive easings are eventually interpreted as evidence that 4.4 percent GDP growth is optimistic rather than achievable, risk appetite for EM assets denominated in commodity-exporting currencies softens accordingly. For now, markets appear to be reading Thursday's move as supportive — more stimulus rather than distress signal — but the threshold where the reading flips is worth monitoring.

The next data points to watch: China's May retail sales and industrial production numbers, due later this month, will provide the clearest near-term read on whether consumer spending is stabilising or continuing to deteriorate. Property investment data for May — tracking both new starts and completions — will test whether Beijing's existing support measures have arrested the sector's sequential decline. If both readings disappoint, the market debate will shift rapidly to the timing of the next move, and whether it involves interest rates rather than reserve ratios.
