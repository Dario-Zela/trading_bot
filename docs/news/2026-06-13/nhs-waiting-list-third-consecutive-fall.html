NHS elective waiting list falls for third consecutive month

An NHS integrated care centre in Oldham, England — one of the facilities at the front line of the government's elective waiting list reduction programme — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
NHS England reported a third consecutive monthly fall in the elective waiting list but opposition parties and health analysts question whether administrative removals, not more treatment, are driving the headline number.

NHS England figures released this week showed the elective care waiting list falling for a third consecutive month, a run of improvement the Starmer government has moved quickly to frame as a sustained health milestone — the first it can credibly claim since taking office. For a government whose approval ratings are under acute pressure, the data lands at a useful moment.

The waiting list stood at roughly 7.1 million treatment pathways in March 2026, down from a post-pandemic peak of 7.8 million in September 2023 and approximately 330,000 lower than when Labour came to power. In May, NHS England declared that the health service had hit its interim target of 65% of patients treated within 18 weeks — a threshold not met since 2016. Health and Social Care Secretary Wes Streeting called it "the fastest reduction in waiting times in the history of the NHS," adding that the government was "right on track" toward the constitutional 92% standard the manifesto pledged to restore by 2029.

That framing has not gone uncontested.

Counting the question

The official headline figure — an aggregate of Referral to Treatment (RTT) pathways — masks several layers of complication. The most direct involves administrative removals: NHS England data shows 268,283 patients were taken off waiting lists in January 2026 alone, a 15% jump on the previous month. Trusts receive approximately £33 for each patient removed, an incentive critics argue makes the mechanism vulnerable to gaming. Removals include patients who opted for private care, those who died, and anyone unresponsive to text-message follow-ups.

Sarah Scobie, deputy director of research at the Nuffield Trust, offered qualified acknowledgement: "It's a legitimate process, but the NHS and the government need to be clear with the public if this plays a big role in getting the overall size of the list down." NHS England has rejected the characterisation that removals are driving the headline reduction, attributing progress to record elective activity in 2025.

Shadow Health Secretary Stuart Andrew went further. He argued the government was taking credit "for progress that simply hasn't happened," noting that hospitals were carrying out around 10% fewer operations than in the comparable month, and that without systematic list validation the waiting list would have risen since Labour took office.

A separate methodological concern cuts deeper still. Analysis by MBI Health, using NHS England's own data, estimated that approximately 3 million patients currently lack any specialist appointment after a GP referral — an "invisible waiting list" that sits entirely outside the official RTT count. The Patients Association described the situation as "staggering," with those patients "checking their phones daily for hospital calls that never come." The Department of Health and Social Care declined to comment on that figure.

The Health Foundation, while welcoming the 65% milestone, noted in a press release that the NHS "falls short on other key measures" — including diagnostics, where nearly a fifth of patients on the 15 key-test list have been waiting more than six weeks against an operational standard of under 1%.

The next monthly RTT release, expected in July, will show whether April's figures held the fall or reversed it. Summer months historically see waiting lists rise as elective activity dips — a seasonal pattern that will test whether the government's three-month run survives its first warm-weather data point.
