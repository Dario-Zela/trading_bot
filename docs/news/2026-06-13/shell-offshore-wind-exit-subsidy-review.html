Shell's $1bn offshore wind exit triggers UK subsidy review as fossil fuel strategy reasserted

Sheringham Shoal offshore wind farm in the North Sea — one of several UK sites where major energy companies have scaled back commitments — Geograph / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Shell's $1B+ offshore wind sale, alongside its $14B Canadian gas buy, signals the FTSE 100 major has concluded near-term fossil fuel returns outpace the energy transition timeline.

Shell is simultaneously selling wind farms and buying gas fields. Last month the company agreed to acquire Canada's ARC Resources — a natural-gas-heavy Montney play — for approximately $14 billion. This week, Bloomberg reported that Shell has engaged Rothschild & Co. and PJT Partners to manage the disposal of its offshore wind portfolio, a process expected to launch before the end of 2026 with a sale likely to complete in 2027. The ask is over a billion dollars. The contrast is not incidental. It is the strategy.

Shell plans to sell its offshore wind assets for over $1 billion while simultaneously completing a $14 billion Canadian gas acquisition — the clearest signal yet that management believes fossil fuel returns will outpace renewables for years to come.

For energy markets, the move raises a pointed question: if one of the largest and best-resourced energy companies in the world cannot make offshore wind work at scale, what does that mean for the investment case that has underpinned sector valuations, UK policy targets, and the broader energy transition timeline?

A three-year unwind

The sell-off did not emerge overnight. When Wael Sawan became chief executive in early 2023, Shell's stated ambition was to become the world's largest electricity producer, underpinned by a multi-gigawatt renewables buildout. Sawan shelved that vision and replaced it with a narrower proposition: capital discipline, shareholder returns, and LNG.

"LNG would be Shell's biggest contribution to the energy industry over the next decade," Sawan told delegates at the Japan Summit last year, a line that has since become something close to the company's internal manifesto.

The divestments followed in sequence. In March 2024, Shell sold its 50% stake in SouthCoast Wind in Massachusetts. In October 2025, it took a $1 billion write-off on Atlantic Shores, a New Jersey project that never reached a final investment decision. The following month it exited both CampionWind and MarramWind off the Scottish coast — two floating wind leases it had won with significant fanfare in Crown Estate Scotland's 2022 ScotWind round — returning one lease to the Crown Estate and selling the other to ScottishPower Renewables. By February, Sprng Energy, the Indian renewables platform acquired for $1.55 billion in 2022, was under strategic review. The offshore wind portfolio now in play represents the last substantial piece of a green energy architecture that Shell spent much of the previous decade assembling.

In terms of capital, the directional signal is unambiguous: the ARC Resources deal adds roughly two billion barrels of oil-equivalent proved-plus-probable reserves and underpins Shell's ambition to grow LNG sales by 4–5% per year through 2030. Against that, the offshore wind portfolio — worth somewhere north of $1 billion in a market where assets remain in demand — looks like a rounding error. What matters is the sequential logic: every exit from renewables frees capital and management bandwidth for the fossil-fuel side of the ledger.

SHEL shares have appreciated roughly 27% over the past twelve months, touching an all-time high of 3,591 pence on 31 March 2026. Markets, at least, appear satisfied with the direction of travel.

The CfD fracture

In the UK, Shell's departure carries a second dimension that is harder to price: what it means for the government's offshore wind subsidy architecture.

The Contracts for Difference mechanism — the fixed-price guarantee that underpins almost all UK offshore wind deployment — has come under sustained criticism from developers over the past two years. The root of the problem is timing: strike prices agreed under earlier allocation rounds were locked in before the inflation surge, supply-chain bottlenecks, and rising interest rates that reshaped project economics after 2022. Prices that fell to £37.35 per megawatt-hour in earlier rounds proved structurally inadequate for many operators when input costs spiked. Ørsted halted Hornsea 4 — a 2.4GW North Sea project — with its chief executive citing insufficient returns. The 32MW TwinHub floating wind project had its CfD terminated in April.

Shell's exits from MarramWind and CampionWind in Scotland, attributed internally to a desire to "shift away from capital-intensive generation projects," were widely read by industry observers as a commentary on CfD adequacy as much as on company strategy. Westminster lawmakers have since called for a formal audit of whether the subsidy mechanism can retain the calibre of investor that UK clean energy targets require.

The government's position has been to accelerate rather than pause. Allocation Round 7, concluded in January 2026, secured 8.4 gigawatts of capacity — a record for a single auction — with RWE alone capturing nearly 7GW. Allocation Round 8 is set to launch in July. The Energy Secretary, Ed Miliband, has a stated target of 43–50GW of offshore wind capacity by 2030, revised down from an earlier 55GW goal after grid connection delays and a shortage of specialist installation vessels began to compress the achievable pipeline.

The tension in those two data points — AR7's headline success and the quiet exit of a major from the same market — reflects a structural problem the CfD framework has not resolved: the scheme is good at attracting bids from developers willing to bear construction risk at low strike prices, but less successful at retaining the integrated energy majors who bring balance-sheet depth and long-term asset management capabilities. Shell and BP are one category of participant; RWE and Ørsted (notwithstanding Hornsea 4) are another. The departure of the former category is not, on its own, fatal to the 2030 target — but it does narrow the investor base at a moment when the pipeline needs to widen.

What the pivot signals

Shell is not acting in isolation. BP chief executive Murray Auchincloss acknowledged last year that his company had gone "too far, too fast" in transitioning away from fossil fuels, subsequently cutting more than $5 billion from BP's renewable energy budget and raising oil and gas investment by roughly 20% annually. Norway's Equinor has followed a comparable path.

The shared logic across these companies is a recalibration of the energy transition timeline rather than a denial that it will happen. Near-term fossil fuel demand has held up more stubbornly than many internal models projected five years ago. Renewable project economics — particularly for offshore wind — have proved more cost-sensitive than the pre-2022 buildout implied. And LNG, positioned by Sawan as a coal-displacement fuel for Asia's growing electricity demand, offers Shell a credible bridge narrative that satisfies both investor return requirements and a defensible climate contribution claim.

For equity markets, the practical implication is a re-rating of how to weight energy sector exposure. SHEL's 27% twelve-month run reflects the market's endorsement of Sawan's capital allocation choices. The question now is whether that re-rating holds if oil prices slide — Shell's Q1 2026 adjusted earnings of $6.9 billion were already down from $9.6 billion a year earlier, tracking softer commodity prices. The ARC Resources acquisition, due to close in H2 2026, adds gas-weighted reserves at a moment when LNG pricing is itself volatile, partly because of the Iran nuclear deal optimism currently pushing oil and gas prices lower. The fossil-fuel bet is not without its own risks.

For the UK specifically, the subsidy audit that Westminster is now pressing for will determine whether the CfD framework gets a structural reform ahead of AR9 or merely a budget increase for AR8. The July launch of AR8 will be the first significant test of whether the scheme retains credibility with the investor community after a year of high-profile departures. If bid volumes disappoint, the pressure on the government to choose between higher consumer-funded strike prices and lower clean energy ambition will become acute. The outcome of that auction — expected before year-end — is the number to watch.
