Rolls-Royce wins first SMR export as Sweden ends its 40-year nuclear moratorium

Ringhals nuclear power plant on the Värö Peninsula, Varberg — two units still run; three Rolls-Royce SMRs are planned for the same site — Tubaist / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Vattenfall picked Rolls-Royce over GE Vernova for three 470 MW reactors near Ringhals — the UK firm's third European win and first overseas sale, with shares touching March highs.

Sweden's state-owned utility Vattenfall has picked Rolls-Royce to supply three small modular reactors at a site on the country's west coast — the British firm's first overseas order and, by competitive record, its strongest validation yet. The announcement came on 15 June from Videberg Kraft, a project vehicle 80 per cent owned by Vattenfall and the remainder by a consortium of Swedish industrial companies, ending a three-year evaluation that ran in parallel with Stockholm's broader effort to reboot a nuclear programme idle since the mid-1980s.

Each reactor will produce 470 megawatts of electricity. Three together will generate around 12 terawatt hours a year — roughly what two million Swedish households consume — and the plant will sit on the Värö Peninsula near Ringhals, where Sweden currently operates its last two working reactors. A first unit is expected on the grid in the mid-2030s, depending on permitting.

Sweden's long road back

Sweden has not built a nuclear power station in over four decades. The arc from that standstill to Monday's contract runs through a 1980 referendum that endorsed a phase-out, a Chernobyl effect that deepened public reluctance in 1986, and a parliamentary resolution in 2003 that restated the direction — before a formal reversal in 2009 that produced a policy change but no actual construction.

The decisive shift was not legislative but economic. Europe's gas crisis from 2022 onward put energy security back at the centre of Swedish politics. The centre-right government that took office in October 2022 made nuclear expansion a coalition commitment, and the carbon-neutral-by-2045 target gave it a deadline. By November 2023, Stockholm had published a roadmap targeting the equivalent of at least two large reactors by 2035 and up to ten by 2045. State aid legislation took effect on 1 August 2025; Videberg Kraft submitted Sweden's first application under it in December.

The Ringhals location is a practical choice as much as a symbolic one. Nuclear engineering skills and grid infrastructure already exist there. Two of the original four Ringhals units remain in service. Building alongside them shortens the regulatory learning curve and avoids the additional permitting burden of a greenfield site.

Three for three in Europe

The Sweden deal completes an unbroken run for Rolls-Royce SMR Ltd in competitive European tenders. CEZ in the Czech Republic selected the company in October 2024 for up to 3 gigawatts, with first deployment planned at Temelín. Great British Energy — Nuclear chose Rolls-Royce for three units at Wylfa in North Wales in June 2025, with a final investment decision targeted for 2029. Sweden is the third win, and Rolls-Royce now holds the only multiple binding commitments to deliver SMR units anywhere in Europe.

"Selection by Videberg Kraft reinforces the status of Rolls-Royce SMR as the only company with multiple contractual commitments to deliver SMR units in Europe."
— Tufan Erginbilgic, Rolls-Royce CEO

Videberg Kraft evaluated 75 technology options over three years before narrowing to two: Rolls-Royce's design and GE Vernova Hitachi's BWRX-300. What tipped the balance, according to the buyer, was execution confidence. The Rolls-Royce design is around 90 per cent factory-assembled — each unit roughly 16 metres tall and 4 metres across — with controlled manufacture reducing the site-construction risk that has plagued conventional large reactor projects.

UK government ministers moved quickly to claim the win. Business Secretary Peter Kyle, who had visited Stockholm as part of a targeted export push, said he was "unashamedly backing British business on the world stage." Prime Minister Starmer called it "a major win for Britain's economy — showcasing UK engineering on the world stage." Tom Greatrex, chief executive of the Nuclear Industry Association, described the deal as "a significant boost for Britain's nuclear export ambitions." Industry bodies project the global SMR market reaching £500 billion by 2050; the Wylfa project alone is expected to employ 3,000 people at construction peak.

What the shares are pricing in

Rolls-Royce shares closed up 3.8 per cent to 1,357p on Monday, back to levels last seen in March. The contract value was not disclosed; the two parties confirmed only that it runs to "multibillion" pounds.

The market's logic is coherent in outline. A company that wins every European SMR tender it enters is developing something close to a structural position in a market the continent's governments are actively subsidising into existence. That is not a fantasy thesis.

The numbers sitting underneath it are harder. Rolls-Royce trades at around 35.7 times forward earnings — more than twice its ten-year average of 17.4 times. The first SMR revenues from any of the three contracted projects will not arrive until the mid-2030s at the earliest, and that assumes no permitting delays — a significant assumption for a technology that has never been built at commercial scale in Europe. Analyst price targets bracket the current level uncomfortably: JPMorgan at 1,320p, Morgan Stanley at 1,280p, Citi at 1,101p. All three sit below Monday's close.

The risk is that a premium this compressed leaves little room for execution slips. A hold-up in the UK Generic Design Assessment — still in its final stages — or delays in Sweden's own regulatory process, which is untested for this reactor class, would reprice the stock faster than the underlying programme would justify.

The next hard catalyst is the Wylfa final investment decision, currently targeted for 2029. Before then, the variable to watch is how quickly Sweden's permitting machinery — newly adapted to accommodate new nuclear after four decades without it — can move a first-of-kind reactor design through approval. That timeline will determine whether "mid-2030s" is a schedule or an aspiration.
