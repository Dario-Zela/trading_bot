Barclays buys GoHenry UK to deepen its youth banking presence

In one sentence
Barclays pays £180m for GoHenry's UK operations, adding 500,000 child users as banks race to lock in young customers before rivals do.

Barclays has agreed to pay £180 million for the United Kingdom operations of GoHenry, a pocket-money and financial-education app for children aged 6 to 18. The deal, announced on June 12 and expected to close in the fourth quarter pending regulatory approval, marks the bank's most direct move yet to build a customer pipeline that begins well before adulthood.

GoHenry was founded in 2012 and counts more than 500,000 active users in the UK — over two million children and teenagers have used the platform since launch. Its offer is straightforward: a prepaid Visa debit card with parental controls, in-app savings goals, gamified financial-literacy lessons and a Junior ISA wrapper. It is, in other words, a product designed to make banking feel routine to someone who has never needed a current account.

Barclays intends to retain the GoHenry brand and run it as a standalone app. Vim Maru, Barclays UK chief executive, called GoHenry "a pioneering" force in youth-focused financial services with "a market-leading brand for children," and framed the acquisition as a way to serve customers "through all of life's big moments, whether opening a very first account, saving for retirement, and everything in between."

The seller, US-based Acorns — which acquired GoHenry in 2023 — walks away with its US operations (now branded Acorns Early, with 1.4 million customers) and Pixpay, its European platform. The geographic carve-out is clean: Barclays gets the UK name and user base, Acorns keeps the rest.

The CET1 impact is modest — approximately 5 basis points, leaving Barclays' 2026 and 2028 guidance unchanged. For a £180 million outlay the bank is buying a loss-making business: GoHenry posted a £21.9 million net loss in 2024, narrowed from £48 million a year earlier, with a workforce of around 200. The platform's Net Promoter Score of +58 suggests genuine user attachment, but profitability remains a project, not a fact.

The M&A Playbook

The GoHenry deal fits a recognisable pattern. NatWest acquired RoosterMoney, a smaller pocket-money app with roughly 130,000 UK users, in 2021. Banks have been circling the youth segment for years, drawn by a logic that predates digital wallets: customers acquired early tend to stay. Financial inertia is a structural feature of retail banking, and it works whether the initial product is a mortgage or a prepaid card for a nine-year-old.

Barclays' turn to M&A also follows a conspicuous miss elsewhere. The bank was outbid for wealth manager Evelyn Partners, which NatWest won for £2.7 billion. GoHenry is cheaper and earlier-stage, but the strategic ambition — securing customer relationships before competitors do — is the same thread.

The execution risk here is real. Integrating a fintech with a distinct brand identity and a cash-burning P&L into a 330-year-old high-street institution requires more than a press release. Whether GoHenry's users and their parents remain loyal once Barclays' presence becomes visible is an open question at announcement. Louise Hill, GoHenry's founder, said "financial education shouldn't have a start or end date" — a clean articulation of what the platform promises and what Barclays is betting on.

The immediate market read is limited: 5 basis points on CET1 is noise, and guidance is unchanged. What matters is the Q4 2026 regulatory close, followed by Barclays' next retail update, where any early signal on integration costs or user-retention rates will deserve more than a line in the footnotes.
