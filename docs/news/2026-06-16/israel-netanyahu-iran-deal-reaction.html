Netanyahu says Israel was not consulted and warns Tehran will never get the bomb — with or without a deal

Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, whose public pushback on the US-Iran nuclear framework has exposed visible strain in his alliance with Donald Trump. — Israeli Government Press Office / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Netanyahu said Israel was not consulted on Trump's Iran deal, warned Tehran will never get the bomb regardless, and admitted he and Trump 'do not always see eye to eye.'

Benjamin Netanyahu has publicly broken with the script. In an evening press conference on Monday — his first in nearly three months — the Israeli prime minister acknowledged that Israel was not consulted on the nuclear framework the United States and Iran signed over the weekend, conceded that he and Donald Trump "do not always see eye to eye," and declared, with the flat certainty of a man setting a red line, that Iran will never acquire nuclear weapons "with or without a deal."

The statement amounted to an extraordinary piece of public diplomacy: a nominal ally of the sitting American president distancing himself from the most significant diplomatic achievement of Trump's second term, without quite denouncing it. Netanyahu chose his words carefully — he neither condemned the agreement nor endorsed it — but the subtext was plain enough.

Excluded from the room

The core of Netanyahu's complaint was procedural as much as substantive. Israel, he said, does not yet know the full terms of the memorandum of understanding Washington and Tehran concluded after weeks of shuttle diplomacy. For a country that has spent two decades treating Iranian nuclear ambitions as an existential threat — and whose fighter jets, according to Netanyahu's own account, participated in the largest strike mission in Israeli history against Iranian targets this year — to be left outside the final negotiating room is a pointed humiliation.

Netanyahu framed the gap between himself and Trump with characteristic understatement. "In the US, they say that President Trump does everything I ask, and in Israel, they say the opposite, that I do everything he asks," he told reporters. "Neither is true." He added that when the two leaders diverge, "Israel's security interests need to be defended wisely."

That is a notable formulation. It is not the language of a close alliance moving in lockstep. It is the language of a partner managing distance.

The divergence has not been merely rhetorical. Trump publicly warned Netanyahu against military strikes on Iran as the nuclear talks approached their conclusion, saying he considered such action "not appropriate right now" because a deal was "very close." Trump has also reportedly described Netanyahu as "a very difficult guy" in private, a characterisation that leaked with unusual speed. When a sitting US president airs frustration with an Israeli prime minister through back-channels and social posts rather than quiet diplomatic pressure, something has shifted in the relationship.

The nuclear red line — and what lies beyond it

On the substance of the deal itself, Netanyahu's warnings were more pointed. "With or without an agreement, Iran will never have nuclear weapons," he said. "Not today and not tomorrow." He described the military campaign against Iran — the strikes on nuclear scientists, the destruction of enrichment facilities, the degradation of the missile programme — as having achieved its core objective: removing "the immediate threat of annihilation" from Israel.

But the mission, he insisted, is not finished. "The struggle is not over."

The implicit threat is one that has hung over Middle East diplomacy for years: if the diplomatic process allows Iran to preserve enrichment capacity that could eventually be reconstituted into a weapons programme, Israel reserves the right to act unilaterally. Netanyahu did not use those words on Monday, but his track record and the logic of his framing left little ambiguity.

This matters for the deal's durability. Senior US administration officials have been careful to clarify that the Iran nuclear agreement does not constrain Israeli military action — specifically against Hezbollah. Israel's withdrawal from southern Lebanon is not a condition of the accord, officials said, and Israel retains the right to respond to Hezbollah threats. That clarification was directed partly at Tehran, which had sought protections for Hezbollah as part of any ceasefire, and partly at Jerusalem, as a signal that Washington is not asking Israel to surrender its military posture in exchange for a diplomatic arrangement it did not design.

Israeli Defense Minister Israel Katz reinforced the point, announcing that IDF forces would remain in southern Lebanon security buffer zones "without any time limit." National Security Minister Itamar Ben Gvir went further, declaring the agreement "not binding" on Israel and asserting that "Israel is not subject to the United States." The political right in Netanyahu's coalition has been even less guarded than the prime minister himself.

What comes next

The immediate question is whether the gap between Washington and Jerusalem widens or stabilises. Vice President JD Vance said on Tuesday that he was "confident" Israel would join the broader framework of the Iran deal "further down the road" — a formulation that simultaneously acknowledges the current distance and treats it as temporary. Whether that optimism is warranted depends heavily on what the deal's full terms, once disclosed, reveal about Iran's residual enrichment capability.

For Netanyahu, the domestic calculus is equally fraught. Opposition leader Yair Lapid argued that the agreement achieved "none of Israel's war goals," since the Iranian regime survives and retains the theoretical ability to reconstitute its nuclear programme. The prime minister's defence — that Israel has destroyed enough of the infrastructure to have bought significant time, and that it retains freedom of action — is coherent but politically precarious.

The deeper question the coming weeks will answer is whether Trump's Iran strategy has a stable internal logic or is genuinely subject to revision under Israeli pressure. If Netanyahu's public pushback is the opening move in a negotiation over the deal's implementation details, the alliance may yet find a workable equilibrium. If it reflects an irreversible divergence on whether a deal with Iran — any deal — is acceptable, the strain will compound. The first test will be the full publication of the memorandum's text. Israel, and markets pricing geopolitical risk across the region, are waiting to read it.
