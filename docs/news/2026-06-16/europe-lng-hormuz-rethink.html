Hormuz deal may ease pressure on Europe's costly LNG import build-out

The Strait of Hormuz, the narrow waterway between Iran and Oman through which roughly one-fifth of global LNG trade transits. — CIA / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
The US–Iran Hormuz deal eased European gas prices sharply but threatens to undercut the strategic case for Europe's expensive LNG import infrastructure expansion.

European gas prices fell more than five percent on Monday to around €44 per megawatt hour — their lowest level since May — after Washington and Tehran announced a preliminary agreement to end their three-and-a-half-month conflict and reopen the Strait of Hormuz. The immediate market relief was real, if partial. But for European energy policymakers, the diplomatic breakthrough carries a less comfortable implication: it places a large question mark over tens of billions of euros in liquefied natural gas import infrastructure committed since 2022.

The EU has been on what analysts describe as a historic LNG buildout. Having cut Russian pipeline gas imports from 150 billion cubic metres in 2021 to roughly 40 bcm by 2025, Brussels accelerated spending on floating storage and regasification units, onshore terminals, and long-term supply contracts with US and Qatari producers. Total EU spending on LNG imports between 2022 and 2025 reached approximately €281 billion. Germany alone committed €9.8 billion for LNG purposes through to 2038, including a permanent onshore terminal at Brunsbüttel — estimated at €1.5 billion — expected to come online later this year.

The crisis logic was straightforward: diversify away from Russia at any cost, build enough regasification capacity to absorb supply shocks from any direction. Then the Hormuz blockade arrived and appeared to validate every alarm about maritime chokepoints. Attacks on Qatar's Ras Laffan complex in early March removed an estimated one-sixth of global LNG supply, sending the Dutch TTF benchmark from €32/MWh to above €50/MWh and prompting Goldman Sachs to warn that a two-month disruption could push prices above €100/MWh.

The overcapacity trap

What Monday's deal has exposed is that the Hormuz shock, rather than justifying an accelerated buildout, may have already made the case against it. Before the conflict, European LNG terminal utilisation rates had already fallen from 58 percent in 2023 to 42 percent in 2024. The Institute for Energy Economics and Financial Analysis estimated that by 2030, Europe's regasification capacity could reach 405 billion cubic metres per year — against projected LNG demand of roughly 150 bcm. That is a threefold overcapacity. Even during the crisis, FSRUs in Le Havre had been decommissioned and FSRU charter agreements cancelled on grounds of financial unviability.

"LNG shipments to Central and Eastern Europe are reliable as long as those gas markets are not overly dependent upon one supplier," said Anne-Sophie Corbeau, a global research scholar at Columbia University's Center on Global Energy Policy — a warning that cuts both ways now that the US provides 58 percent of EU LNG imports and Gulf supply is being restored.

With Persian Gulf flows set to resume — though full traffic is not expected to recover until early 2027 given ongoing mine-clearance operations estimated at up to six months — the geopolitical-hedging rationale for further buildout weakens. Qatar is pushing ahead with its North Field expansion, which would raise its LNG export capacity from 77 to 142 million tonnes per year; once operational, it will add further downward pressure on the global price at which European terminal investment pencils out.

The EU's AccelerateEU strategy, unveiled in April, steers explicitly toward renewables, nuclear, and electrification rather than more fossil-fuel import infrastructure. What Monday's deal changes is the political price of staying that course: with Gulf gas flowing again, the emergency rationale for fast-tracked terminal approvals and state subsidy fades. The Brunsbüttel permanent terminal, the Gdańsk FSRU due for commissioning in 2028, and a pipeline of planned European projects may each now face a harder financing and regulatory environment.

The formal signing of the Hormuz accord is scheduled for Geneva on June 19. The nuclear question — and with it, the durability of the energy normalization — enters a 60-day negotiation track. Until those talks conclude, European infrastructure decisions will remain suspended between two futures: a Middle East stabilised enough to make costly LNG independence look like over-insurance, and one volatile enough to make it look prudent.
