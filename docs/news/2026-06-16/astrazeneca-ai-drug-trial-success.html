AstraZeneca says AI is lifting drug-trial success rates — without offering the numbers

AstraZeneca's Discovery Centre campus in Cambridge, UK, where much of the company's AI-assisted R&D is based. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
AstraZeneca's CEO claims AI is lifting drug-trial success rates but won't say by how much — a familiar gap between pharma's AI pitch and its evidence.

Drug development is a business built on failure. More than nine in ten drug candidates that enter clinical testing never reach patients — they fail at the point of proving efficacy or safety, often after years of work and hundreds of millions of dollars have been spent. That stubborn statistic, sometimes described as the industry's original sin, is what AstraZeneca's chief executive Pascal Soriot now says machine learning is beginning to fix.

In a recent CNBC appearance, Soriot told viewers that AI is reshaping how drugs are designed and tested — helping AstraZeneca identify better targets, remove likely side effects earlier, and, crucially, predict which Phase 3 trials are most likely to succeed. "We have developed an agent that takes all this data together and helps us predict the probability of success," he said. Phase 3 is the final and costliest stage of human testing; a single trial runs between $300m and $500m, and a late failure there destroys more value than a rejection at any earlier gate. Soriot framed the calculus simply: "If you increase the probability of success, the productivity improvement is enormous."

What he did not say was how much that probability has actually improved. No before-and-after success rate was disclosed. No trial cohort was named. No regulator, outside academic, or competitor offered independent corroboration.

An incentive to talk it up

That absence is worth noting, because AstraZeneca has made large, public bets on AI — a $200m partnership with Tempus AI, a collaboration with Pathos, and the recent acquisition of Boston-based Modella AI, which the company describes as the first acquisition of an AI firm by a major pharmaceutical company. Modella's foundation models are being integrated into AstraZeneca's oncology pipeline for quantitative pathology and biomarker discovery. When a company has disclosed those investments to analysts and shareholders, its executives have every incentive to argue the thesis is playing out.

The underlying problem they are trying to solve is real and severe. Pharmaceutical R&D productivity has declined in inflation-adjusted terms for decades — a trend so persistent it has its own name, Eroom's Law, which is Moore's Law backwards. The number of new drugs approved per billion dollars of real R&D spending has fallen roughly a hundredfold since the 1950s, even as screening technology, genomics, and now AI have each been heralded as productivity inflection points. Each wave has improved certain steps of the process without decisively moving the overall attrition curve.

AstraZeneca is not the only large pharma making AI claims. Nor is it the only one declining to back those claims with hard numbers. The pattern has become familiar enough to warrant a simple test: a company that has genuinely raised its clinical success rate with AI should be able to say by how much, for which therapeutic area, across how many trials, and over what time window. None of those specifics appear in Soriot's remarks.

The more meaningful data point will come when the company's AI-selected Phase 3 programmes report out. AstraZeneca has a dense late-stage pipeline — multiple high-value readouts are expected through 2026 and into 2027. Those results, tracked against historical benchmarks, will say more than any CNBC appearance.
