Shell sells Indian renewables stake, cuts $1bn green budget, and pauses $3bn buyback — a fossil-fuel pivot in a single week

Shell Centre, the company's global headquarters on Belvedere Road in London, where Wael Sawan has reshaped capital allocation decisively toward fossil fuels since 2023. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Shell is selling its Indian renewables arm for $1.8B, pausing a $3B buyback for its C$22B Canadian gas deal, and slashing green spending — a clean fossil-fuel pivot in a single week.

Shell has had a busy week rearranging its balance sheet. In the space of a few days, the British oil major launched a formal sale process for a $1.8 billion Indian renewable energy platform, paused a $3 billion share buyback programme under legal constraints tied to its blockbuster Canadian gas acquisition, and continued pulling capital away from low-carbon energy at a pace that marks one of the sharpest fossil-fuel re-orientations among the supermajors.

Taken separately, each move has a technical explanation. Together, they draw a clear picture of where Shell's leadership believes the returns are — and where they are not.

Exiting India's renewables boom

Shell acquired Sprng Energy from private equity firm Actis in May 2022 for $1.55 billion, a deal that tripled its operational renewable capacity at the time and handed it 2.9 gigawatts of wind and solar assets spread across Gujarat, Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, Karnataka and Tamil Nadu. The pitch was clear: India was — and remains — one of the fastest-growing renewable markets on earth, its government targeting 500 GW of clean power by 2030.

Shell is now walking away from that bet at roughly a 16 per cent premium to what it paid. The company is running a formal sale process, with Aditya Birla Group emerging as the lead buyer at a valuation of approximately $1.7–$1.8 billion. Other binding offers were submitted by KKR, Actis — the original vendor, now apparently interested in buying back in — and a Temasek-NIIF consortium. Shell confirmed it is "in initial discussions with potential partners interested in Sprng Energy" but declined to comment further on the outcome.

For Aditya Birla, the acquisition would be transformative. The conglomerate is building out Aditya Birla Renewables through its Grasim Industries arm, targeting over 10 GW of clean energy capacity by 2030, partly to power its own operations in metals, cement and chemicals. Sprng, which has 2.3 GW currently operational and a contracted and pipeline portfolio well exceeding 5 GW, would compress that timeline considerably.

The asymmetry is telling. India's conglomerates — capital-patient, vertically integrated, familiar with the regulatory terrain — are natural long-term holders of utility-scale renewable assets. Shell, a global operator competing across dozens of markets, found that the platform required the kind of localised development expertise and political capital that does not slot neatly into its portfolio.

Shell's investments in its Renewables and Energy Solutions division have fallen to around 8 per cent of its total capital expenditure, down from 14 per cent in 2022 and 11 per cent in 2023, according to shareholder advocacy group Follow This. CEO Wael Sawan, who took over in January 2023, has cut offshore wind spending, scaled back hydrogen investments, and exited power markets in Europe and China. The Sprng sale is the most concrete divestment to date from that retreat.

The buyback pause and what it reveals

On June 12, Shell announced that it was pausing its current $3 billion share repurchase programme through July 14, citing "related securities law requirements" triggered by the publication of the shareholder circular for ARC Resources — a Canadian natural gas producer Shell agreed to acquire on April 27 for C$22 billion (roughly $16.4 billion).

The suspension is procedural in origin: once a takeover circular circulates, acquirers in many jurisdictions face trading restrictions. But the timing lands with significance for income-focused shareholders who had built Shell's quarterly buyback cadence into their return expectations. Any repurchases not completed in the window will roll into later 2026 programmes, subject to board approval, the company said.

The ARC deal itself is a bet of a different order. ARC is the largest pure-play producer in Canada's Montney shale basin, a formation straddling Alberta and northeast British Columbia that produced 374,000 barrels of oil equivalent per day last year. The acquisition boosts Shell from seventh to second among Montney producers — behind only Ovintiv — and, more significantly, provides the feedstock Shell would need to pursue a Phase 2 expansion of LNG Canada, which would double the facility's current output of 14 million tonnes per annum. Shell expects the deal to add $1.5 billion in annual cash flow once completed, expected in the second half of this year.

The capital allocation calculus being expressed here is the mirror image of the Sprng sale. Upstream gas reserves in a politically stable jurisdiction with a direct offtake route to LNG export markets: that earns a C$22 billion cheque. A renewable platform in a high-growth emerging market: that gets divested at a small premium after four years.

The shape of the pivot

Shell is not the first supermajor to trim its direct clean-energy holdings. BP scaled back its offshore wind ambitions in 2023 and 2024 under pressure from activist investors. TotalEnergies has largely kept its renewables build-out intact but has been more cautious about new commitments. What distinguishes Shell's week is the simultaneity — the Sprng exit, the buyback pause, and the ongoing capital shift away from low-carbon investments all landed together, making the direction of travel hard to misread.

For the oil and gas establishment, the argument runs that renewables — solar, wind, green hydrogen — are businesses that reward scale, patience and cost of capital advantages that nimble infrastructure investors or state-backed funds hold more naturally than listed majors with quarterly earnings obligations. The numbers seem to support that framing, at least in the short term: Shell's return on capital employed in its upstream segment has consistently outrun what its low-carbon division has generated.

The open question is what this retreat means at the macro level. India's renewable sector is not short of capital — the Aditya Birla-KKR-Actis competition for Sprng confirms that. But if major Western energy companies step back from direct ownership precisely as deployment needs to accelerate, the financing ecosystem becomes more fragmented: more dependent on local conglomerates, infrastructure funds and development finance, and less anchored by the deep technical and operational expertise the supermajors nominally bring.

For Shell shareholders, the near-term read is straightforward: the ARC deal closes in the second half of 2026, the buyback resumes, and Sprng proceeds arrive on the balance sheet. The harder question — whether the fossil-fuel tilt looks as sensible in five years as it does today — will depend on where oil and gas prices are when India's next gigawatt of renewable capacity comes online.
