Anthropic to brief Trump administration on AI safety and national-security concerns

Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei, whose company has held three rounds of talks with the Trump administration since April over AI safety constraints and federal access to its models. — TechCrunch / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Anthropic met Trump administration officials this week after the Commerce Dept ordered it to pull its two most powerful AI models over a contested jailbreak claim.

Anthropic sent senior technical staff to Washington on Monday for talks with Commerce Department officials — the latest attempt to defuse a confrontation with the Trump administration that has, in four months, cost the AI lab its largest federal contracts and forced it to pull two of its newest models off the market entirely.

The meetings, first reported by Bloomberg, follow a June 12 letter from Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick to Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei ordering the company to suspend access to its most capable models — Fable 5 and Mythos 5 — for any foreign national, anywhere in the world. The stated trigger: a claim by an unnamed company that it had successfully jailbroken Mythos 5, creating potential national security exposure. Anthropic disputed the severity, saying the government had provided only "verbal evidence of a potential narrow, non-universal jailbreak." With no practical way to segregate users by nationality in real time across a customer base running to hundreds of millions, the company took both models offline for everyone.

How it got here

The dispute traces to a request the Pentagon made earlier this year: that Anthropic allow its Claude models to be used for "all lawful purposes," effectively sweeping away guardrails Amodei describes as his company's two absolute limits — no mass surveillance of Americans, no fully autonomous weapons systems without human control. When Anthropic refused, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth labeled the company a "supply chain risk" and directed federal agencies to phase out its technology over six months. Hegseth called the company "sanctimonious"; the president called it "radical left."

A federal judge blocked the supply chain designation. Anthropic sued the administration. In April, Amodei met with White House Chief of Staff Susie Wiles and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent — a session described afterward as "productive," though no policy changes followed. The Commerce Department sessions this week appear to be the next step in that diplomatic sequence.

What's at stake

The immediate stakes are concrete: Anthropic loses federal procurement work as long as the standoff persists, and enterprise customers lose access to models they had just begun deploying. DoD Chief Information Officer Kirsten Davies offered the administration's defence of the approach bluntly: "some things are simply more important than revenue cycles, clickbait, and pre-IPO valuation." Critics including Brad Carson of Americans for Responsible Innovation countered that the directive "risks America's edge in AI innovation."

The policy stakes run deeper. How this administration ultimately handles Anthropic's safety constraints will signal whether guardrails are treated as a legitimate condition of government AI procurement, or as a commercial inconvenience to negotiate away. For competitors — OpenAI, Palantir, and others with fewer stated refusal policies — a framework that marginalises safety floors narrows the gap with Anthropic considerably. One that accommodates them gives Anthropic's approach structural value in the federal market.

No official timeline for restoring access to Fable 5 or Mythos 5 has been disclosed. The next legible inflection point will be whether Anthropic's lawsuit against the Pentagon's supply chain designation advances — or whether the Commerce Department talks produce a framework that renders it moot.
