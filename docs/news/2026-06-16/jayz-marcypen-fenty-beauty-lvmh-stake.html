Jay-Z's MarcyPen Capital in talks to buy LVMH's stake in Fenty Beauty

Fenty Beauty products on display — the brand launched in 2017 with 40 foundation shades and generated around $570 million in sales by end-2018. — Wikimedia Commons / Creative Commons
In one sentence
MarcyPen Capital Partners, Jay-Z's $1.1bn investment firm, is in talks to buy LVMH's 50% stake in Fenty Beauty, valued between $1bn and $2bn.

Jay-Z's investment vehicle MarcyPen Capital Partners is in discussions to acquire LVMH's 50% stake in Fenty Beauty, the cosmetics brand Rihanna has built into one of the sector's most consequential franchises since its 2017 launch. The talks, reported by Black Enterprise and picked up across multiple industry publications, represent a potential turning point in who controls a brand that proved, at scale, that inclusion sells.

Neither party has confirmed the discussions. But the shape of the deal is striking: LVMH, which co-created Fenty Beauty through its Kendo incubator in a 50/50 partnership with Rihanna, is said to have retained the investment bank Evercore in October 2025 to explore strategic options for its stake — a mandate that typically signals seller intent. If MarcyPen closes the transaction, Rihanna and Jay-Z's firm would jointly own one of the few beauty unicorns that actually delivered on its original promise.


Rihanna at a Fenty Beauty event in 2018, the brand's breakout year

The buyer in context

MarcyPen Capital Partners was assembled in late 2024 from a merger of Jay-Z's Marcy Venture Partners — founded in 2018 alongside Jay Brown and Larry Marcus — and Pendulum Opportunities, the investment arm of Pendulum Holdings, co-founded by Robbie Robinson, a former financial adviser to Barack Obama, and D'Rita Robinson. The combined firm manages roughly $1.1 billion in assets and targets growth-stage consumer businesses that, in its own language, "create, move, and lead culture."

MarcyPen is not arriving cold to the Rihanna portfolio. Marcy Venture Partners backed Savage X Fenty, the lingerie business, in both 2019 and 2022. That prior relationship is said to give MarcyPen an edge in what appears to be a competitive process. The firm typically writes cheques of $5 million to $15 million — a range that makes a Fenty stake acquisition transformative in scale for the firm itself, requiring outside financing partners.

What LVMH's exit would signal

The more counterintuitive half of this story is the conglomerate's apparent willingness to part with a genuinely productive asset. Fenty Beauty launched in September 2017 with 40 foundation shades, sold out within days, and generated around $570 million in sales by end-2018. At peak, in 2021, the brand carried a $2.8 billion valuation. Current estimates place it at $1 billion to $2 billion, a compression that tracks the wider correction in beauty valuations — though $450 million in net sales in 2024 is far from distressed.

LVMH's logic likely lies elsewhere. The group has been concentrating its portfolio and directing capital toward its highest-margin maisons in fashion, jewellery, and spirits. A beauty incubator stake — even a lucrative one — sits at the periphery of that identity. Letting go of Fenty, at a moment when a Black-owned investment firm steps in to carry the brand forward, also sidesteps any awkwardness of a European luxury house holding the controlling reins of a brand whose entire founding proposition was cultural.

Watch LVMH's next earnings call for commentary on beauty segment economics and any hint of a writedown or gain on the stake. The deal's main friction point is the valuation gap: what LVMH expects for a half-share of a $1 billion-plus business, and what MarcyPen, at its current size, can credibly finance.
