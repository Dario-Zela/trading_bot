BP and Shell drag the FTSE 100 lower as oil's fall overwhelms deal-day rally

The London Stock Exchange at Paternoster Square — home to FTSE 100 constituents whose energy-heavy weighting turned deal-day into a drag. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Shell fell 4.2% and BP dropped 3.9% in London as the Iran nuclear deal torpedoed oil prices, pulling the FTSE 100 lower on a day global markets otherwise rallied.

London's benchmark index slipped on Monday even as the broader global market took a risk-on turn, held back by something structural: Shell and BP, two of the FTSE 100's largest constituents, fell sharply as the same geopolitical news that lifted every other major equity market drove crude prices lower.

Shell shed 4.2% to 3,083p. BP dropped 3.9% to 514p. Those moves — simultaneous, sizeable, and sector-specific — were enough to pull the FTSE 100 down roughly 0.4% to around 10,430, even as the Nasdaq surged and European bourses broadly advanced on deal-day euphoria.

The catalyst was the US-Iran nuclear framework, confirmed over the weekend and formalised Monday, which opens a pathway for commercial tanker traffic to resume unimpeded through the Strait of Hormuz. The strait in normal times channels around 20 million barrels per day of crude oil and LNG — roughly a fifth of global supply. Conflict-related disruptions had compressed that flow to approximately 14.6 million barrels per day in the first quarter of 2026, according to EIA tracking data. The prospect of a return toward pre-conflict volumes sent Brent crude down 4.9% to $83 a barrel; WTI fell 5.7% to $80.06 — the lowest print since the opening weeks of the conflict.

For a broadly diversified index, those oil moves are one input among many. The FTSE 100 is not broadly diversified.

Composition as constraint

The energy sector accounts for roughly 10% of the FTSE 100 by weight — more than double its equivalent share in the S&P 500. Shell alone has ranked among the index's three largest constituents for the better part of this decade; BP adds another significant slug. Together the two oil majors represent the equivalent of a single enormous macro bet on crude. When oil prices move sharply, that bet expresses itself at the index level regardless of what financials, healthcare or consumer staples are doing.

Monday illustrated the asymmetry. Barclays gained ground following its separate acquisition of GoHenry. Defensive sectors held up. The broader impulse across global markets was unambiguously positive. None of it was enough to offset a synchronised four-percent decline in two top-five index names.

For systematic traders, the session is a clean example of why running broad index momentum signals alongside disaggregated sector signals matters. A long-FTSE position triggered by global risk-on momentum ran directly into an energy-specific headwind. A model that priced the oil-index correlation separately would have seen the divergence coming.

The formal 30-day Hormuz reopening timeline agreed under the deal is the next variable to watch. If Iranian exports accelerate faster than the market currently prices — Iranian production was running well below pre-conflict capacity — the pressure on Shell and BP may not be fully reflected in Monday's moves. Conversely, any slip in implementation would reverse the oil decline rapidly, and with it the drag on the index.
