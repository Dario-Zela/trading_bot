Anthropic sued over alleged hidden usage caps on its $200-a-month Claude subscription

The Claude AI logo — Anthropic's chatbot is at the centre of a class-action dispute over how its premium subscription tiers are marketed — Anthropic / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
A class action alleges Anthropic's $200-a-month Claude Max plan delivers far less usage than advertised, potentially forcing AI vendors to disclose subscription limits upfront.

A paying customer of Anthropic's premium Claude subscription has filed a proposed class-action lawsuit accusing the company of selling usage promises it cannot — or does not — deliver. The case lands squarely on a question that will matter to anyone buying AI services in subscription form: what does "20 times more" actually mean, and must a company disclose it upfront?

Karl Kahn, a Washington D.C. resident, filed the complaint on June 14 in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California. His target is Anthropic's Claude Max plan — specifically the $100-a-month Max 5x tier and the $200-a-month Max 20x tier, which the company markets as offering five and twenty times the usage capacity of the standard Pro subscription respectively. Kahn says what he received fell well short of that. After upgrading to the $200 Max 20x plan in April, he burned through 15 percent of his weekly allowance in a single five-hour coding session using Claude Code.

The fine print Anthropic didn't print

The mechanics matter here. Claude governs heavy usage through a two-layer system: a rolling five-hour window that resets periodically, and a separate weekly cap on total activity. That second layer — the weekly ceiling — was not part of the Max plan when it launched in April 2025. Anthropic added it in August 2025, after a cohort of subscribers began running Claude Code continuously around the clock. The company's public line at the time was that fewer than five percent of users would notice a difference.

What the complaint seizes on is not the caps themselves but the opacity surrounding them. Anthropic's marketing for Max describes usage in relative terms — five times, twenty times — without specifying what unit is being multiplied, how that unit is measured, or what happens when the five-hour limit and the weekly ceiling interact. "The actual usage provided by the Max 5x and Max 20x plans is far below the advertised amount of usage," the complaint states. It is, the suit contends, "really not easy for a normal consumer to know if they're getting the amount they were promised."

Plaintiff's attorney Kati Daffan put it plainly: "Companies have to be honest about their advertising and marketing. If they're not, they can be held liable, need to change their ways, and refund money."

The suit seeks class-action status covering all U.S. residents who bought either Max tier since the plans launched in April 2025. It asks for damages, restitution, and injunctive relief — meaning, at minimum, clearer terms. Kahn also alleges he was at times forced to purchase additional usage after hitting undisclosed ceilings. Anthropic declined to comment.

The case fits a pattern regulators have been watching. The FTC's Operation AI Comply has targeted companies that made misleading claims about AI-powered services, and the agency has stated explicitly that consumer protection law applies to AI products without modification. A private class action carries different stakes than a federal enforcement action, but a ruling or settlement requiring specific disclosure of usage constraints — token counts, reset windows, weekly caps — would set a practical benchmark. OpenAI, Google, and others sell tiered subscriptions with similarly relative-and-opaque limit language.

The first significant procedural test will be whether the court grants class certification — the moment that determines whether this stays a one-subscriber dispute or becomes a live industry reckoning.
