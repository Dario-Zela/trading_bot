Saudi Arabia and UAE offer a measured welcome to the Hormuz agreement

The Strait of Hormuz, between Iran and the Omani exclave of Musandam, through which roughly one-fifth of global daily oil exports pass. — Perry–Castañeda Library, University of Texas / Public domain
In one sentence
Saudi and UAE statements welcoming the Hormuz accord were loaded with conditions — a sign that Gulf-driven energy risk could reprice fast if Iran falters.

Saudi Arabia and the UAE publicly welcomed the US-Iran nuclear accord but stopped well short of endorsing it — their carefully hedged statements are a warning that market optimism may be running ahead of facts on the ground.

When the United States and Iran announced their framework agreement on June 11 and the Strait of Hormuz was declared fully reopened on June 14, the two largest Arab economies in the Gulf were among the first to respond. The response, however, was not quite the endorsement the market rally might have implied.

The UAE Ministry of Foreign Affairs said the country was "closely following" the ceasefire and would seek "further clarification on the agreement's provisions" before forming a fuller judgment. Saudi Arabia, whose Foreign Ministry called for the agreement to "address all issues" contributing to regional stability, offered language that was warmer in tone but similarly conditional in substance. Neither capital said the deal was sufficient. Neither committed to changing its own security posture in response.

A costly conflict — and long memories

The hedging is not diplomatic theatre. Both countries have spent the past several months absorbing real damage. Iranian strikes in April severed part of Saudi Arabia's Petroline pipeline, cutting output by an estimated 600,000 barrels per day. Saudi Arabia's first-quarter budget deficit reached 125.7 billion riyals — roughly $33.5 billion — its worst quarterly shortfall in nearly eight years. The UAE, for its part, faced 2,819 ballistic missiles, cruise missiles, and drones over the course of the conflict and has been the most forthright of the Gulf states in naming what it wants from any settlement.

Yousef al-Otaiba, the UAE's ambassador to Washington, made the country's position explicit weeks before the June deal was signed: "A simple ceasefire isn't enough." Abu Dhabi's Ministry of Foreign Affairs followed with a formal statement demanding that Iran be "held accountable and fully liable for damages and reparations," and calling for a comprehensive approach addressing Tehran's "nuclear capabilities, ballistic missiles, drones, military capabilities, and affiliated proxies" — a bar that the June framework, focused on Hormuz access and nuclear restrictions, does not obviously clear.

Saudi Arabia's discomfort runs deeper still. The three-track negotiating framework that produced the agreement — assembled through Oman, Pakistan, and Qatar — included no Saudi participation in any channel. When Trump outlined the deal framework on a group call with regional leaders in late May, an Abraham Accords normalization requirement appeared that Riyadh had not been consulted on. Saudi Arabia's formal position ties any normalization with Israel to an independent Palestinian state with East Jerusalem as its capital — a condition the current US administration has not endorsed and that the draft memorandum of understanding does not address. One US official told Axios the call produced silence on the line before Trump asked if everyone was still there.

The market read may be too clean

The strait carries around one-fifth of global daily oil flows in normal conditions. The 5 percent drop in Brent crude on deal day reflects the unwinding of a genuine war premium, and that move is defensible. What is harder to justify is pricing the situation as settled.

Both Saudi Arabia and the UAE have signalled — in language careful enough to leave them room to manoeuvre — that their own regional security and energy export strategies will follow observed Iranian compliance, not the signing of an agreement. ADNOC's Fujairah terminal, expanded during the conflict to handle 1.6 million barrels per day independent of Hormuz transit, is not being wound down. Saudi alternative pipeline capacity has not been stood down either. These are the physical expressions of a hedged posture.

For energy markets, the practical question is what happens at the first meaningful compliance test. If Iran moves to expand enrichment or obstructs inspection access in the months ahead, the Gulf states have already positioned themselves to reprice quickly — and have told anyone paying attention that they will. The deal day rally was real. The stability it implies is still provisional.
