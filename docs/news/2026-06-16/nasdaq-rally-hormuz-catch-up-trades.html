Nasdaq surges on Iran relief; analysts flag twenty large-caps still below pre-conflict levels

The Nasdaq MarketSite tower at 4 Times Square, New York City — Monday's session was the index's strongest since March. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Twenty large-caps remain 15–24% below pre-conflict levels even as Nasdaq surged 3%, raising the question of how much their weakness was ever really about Hormuz.

The Nasdaq Composite added 795 points on Monday, rising 3.07% to close at 26,684 — its strongest session since March — as investors registered the weekend announcement that Washington and Tehran had agreed to a ceasefire framework and the imminent reopening of the Strait of Hormuz. The S&P 500 gained 1.65%, a number that flatters how unevenly the relief was distributed: technology and communication services led, while energy stocks lagged as crude fell 5.4% to roughly $80 a barrel, about 30% off its wartime peak.

Not every corner of the market collected a peace dividend. A Benzinga Pro screen of large-capitalisation names — those above $100 billion in market value — identified twenty stocks that closed Monday still 15% to 24% below their February 27 level, the last trading session before the conflict began. The group spans consumer staples (PepsiCo, Unilever, McDonald's), healthcare (Abbott Laboratories, the deepest laggard at minus-24%), precious metals and mining (Newmont, SPDR Gold Shares), Chinese technology (Alibaba, PDD Holdings), and enterprise software (Salesforce, SAP, Accenture). The framing from Benzinga is a "recovery watchlist" — the premise being that sustained Hormuz peace removes the weight these names have been carrying since late February.

The trouble with that assumption

The screen is useful as a map of where price discovery has stalled; it is less persuasive as a trade thesis. The Benzinga analysis itself concedes that the group "kept sliding over the past quarter, extending a downtrend that was already in place before the first shot was fired." That qualifier carries considerable weight. For the Chinese technology names, the energy-cost transmission channel is at least legible: China is the world's largest crude importer, and elevated oil fed directly into corporate cost bases. For US consumer staples or a gold exchange-traded fund, the causal link to Hormuz is thinner. Structural headwinds — consumer spending deceleration, dollar strength, earnings shortfalls — do not dissolve because tankers start moving again.

A second narrative thread may have coloured Monday's mood beyond what the macro facts warranted. SpaceX — which completed its Nasdaq IPO on June 12 at a $1.75 trillion implied valuation under the ticker SPCX — gained roughly 6% after Elon Musk posted on X that he thought the company could reach approximately $1 trillion in annual revenue by 2030. Morgan Stanley's published estimate for that year is $330 billion. The gap between the two figures is wide enough that investors are pricing the aspiration rather than a consensus model. Beyond SpaceX, Monday's standout gainers included semiconductor stocks with no direct exposure to Persian Gulf oil flows — a pattern analysts have previously flagged as a sign that geopolitical relief rallies tend to run ahead of selective fundamental repricing.

Iran's formal deal-signing is scheduled for Friday in Switzerland. If it proceeds cleanly, the broad Hormuz premium in equity markets should consolidate rather than extend. Whether the twenty-stock watchlist translates into a genuine recovery trade depends on the harder analytical step: separating how much of those losses traces to the conflict itself, and how much was already in the price before February 27.
