EU AI Act's high-risk rules take binding effect across the single market

The European Parliament hemicycle in Strasbourg, where lawmakers voted the AI Act into law in 2024 and struck the May 2026 Omnibus revision. — Diliff / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
The EU AI Act's August deadline is real but narrower than planned — most high-risk obligations were quietly pushed to 2027, and only 8 of 27 states have enforcement contacts in place.

The European Union's AI Act has spent the past two years as a law in waiting — formally on the books, structurally immense, and largely theoretical for the companies it targets. That changes in August, when its first substantive enforcement provisions take binding hold. The question is how much it changes.

A staged arrival, not a single deadline

The original design called for a comprehensive set of high-risk AI obligations to become enforceable on August 2, 2026. But in May, EU lawmakers struck a provisional deal under the so-called Digital Omnibus revision, pushing the heaviest requirements back to December 2027 for standalone high-risk systems — those covered by Annex III, including AI used in credit decisions, employment screening, and education — and to August 2028 for AI embedded in regulated products like medical devices.

What does take binding effect on August 2 is narrower: Article 50's transparency requirements, which compel companies to disclose when users are interacting with AI and require machine-readable labels on synthetic audio, video, and image content. The deal also formally bans AI systems that generate non-consensual intimate images. Narrower than originally planned — but legally binding, provided the Omnibus itself is formally adopted, which requires European Parliament and Council approval expected in the weeks before the deadline. If it slips, the original August 2026 high-risk timetable goes live with roughly two weeks' notice.

The enforcement gap

The delayed substance has not bought much breathing room, because the infrastructure to enforce even the current provisions barely exists yet. Only 8 of the EU's 27 member states have designated the single enforcement contact points required by the Act; at least 12 missed their own August 2025 deadline for appointing national competent authorities. Standardisation bodies CEN and CENELEC, which were supposed to deliver the technical compliance standards companies need to demonstrate conformity, pushed their timeline to the end of 2026.

On the corporate side, a readiness assessment by Vision Compliance found 78% of affected enterprises had taken no meaningful steps toward compliance. More than half lacked a basic AI system inventory. Enforcement — when it materialises — will be delegated to those same lagging national authorities, without a GDPR-style one-stop-shop mechanism to concentrate cross-border cases.

The penalty schedule is formidable on paper. Violations of the high-risk requirements can draw fines of €15 million or 3% of global annual turnover, whichever is larger. Deploying a prohibited AI practice — social scoring, subliminal manipulation — triggers the higher tier: €35 million or 7% of global revenue. A fine schedule, however, is not the same as a fine.

For firms in financial services, the classification question carries real stakes. AI systems used to assess individual creditworthiness fall squarely in Annex III's high-risk category, triggering a full compliance stack: bias-audited training data, risk-management documentation, immutable audit logs, and mandatory human override capability. Algorithmic trading systems operating on market data rather than individual credit decisions sit in a greyer zone — but firms using AI in client-facing portfolio recommendations should conduct their own Annex III mapping before December 2027 arrives. Compliance costs are not trivial, and they fall proportionally harder on smaller firms without dedicated legal and engineering capacity.

The near-term date to watch is formal Omnibus adoption, expected before August 2. Everything else — real fines, real case law, real deterrence — follows from whether national authorities are staffed and willing to use what the law gives them.
