AstraZeneca tests Roche's grip on the follicular lymphoma market with EHA26 data

Histological section of follicular lymphoma, showing the characteristically enlarged and crowded lymphoid follicles — the disease AstraZeneca and Roche are competing to treat in the first-line setting — Wikimedia Commons / Ed Uthman
In one sentence
AstraZeneca's surovatamig posted a 96–100% response rate with zero treatment discontinuations in early Phase III data, but tiny cohorts and a 7.9-month follow-up leave Roche's grip on first-line follicular lymphoma intact for now.

At the 31st European Haematology Association congress in Stockholm this June, AstraZeneca presented early Phase III safety data for surovatamig — a bispecific T-cell engager that targets CD19, rather than the CD20 antigen targeted by virtually every approved first-line therapy in follicular lymphoma. The drug, acquired when AstraZeneca purchased bispecific antibody start-up TeneoTwo in 2022, sets up a rare direct competitive test against Roche in a space the Swiss company has controlled for three decades.

Follicular lymphoma is the second most common non-Hodgkin lymphoma in the United States and Europe, accounting for roughly 100,000 new diagnoses annually worldwide. It is treatable but rarely curable under today's standard regimens — R-CHOP, R-CVP, and rituximab-bendamustine, all of which pair a CD20-directed antibody with chemotherapy. The global treatment market runs to approximately $3.3 billion in 2025 and is forecast to approach $5 billion by 2030. Roche's anti-CD20 agents, rituximab and obinutuzumab, have anchored first-line care since the late 1990s; more than 65% of newly diagnosed patients still receive a rituximab-based induction regimen.

The gap in the safety data

What AstraZeneca put before haematologists at EHA26 was preliminary: safety data from the Phase III SOUNDTRACK-F1 trial in 43 previously untreated patients across two dosing cohorts. The numbers, however, looked notable when set against Roche's own bispecific programme.

Surovatamig achieved overall response rates of 96% and 100% in cohorts DL1 (N=22) and DL2 (N=21), with progression-free survival maintained at 100% in DL2 at a median follow-up of 7.9 months. Grade 3 or higher adverse events appeared in roughly 40% of patients; cytokine release syndrome — a feared complication of T-cell engagers — occurred in 36–38%, though only one episode reached Grade 2 severity, and no patient discontinued treatment owing to an adverse event.

That last number registers differently when placed beside Roche's competing drug. Lunsumio (mosunetuzumab), a CD20-directed bispecific being evaluated in first-line follicular lymphoma through the Morninglyte Phase III trial, reported a 90% overall response rate in earlier Phase I/II work but also a 14% discontinuation rate attributable to adverse events. The comparison is not head-to-head, and some of the difference may reflect trial design rather than pharmacology — this deserves noting.

Several further caveats are worth carrying forward. The SOUNDTRACK-F1 cohorts are small: 22 and 21 patients. Seven months of median follow-up is insufficient to assess durability in a disease where remissions in clinical practice routinely extend across years. Market analysts currently project surovatamig reaching $261 million in global annual sales by 2032, against $826 million for Lunsumio — the gap reflecting that Roche's drug is already approved in the relapsed and refractory setting, carries a recently licensed subcutaneous formulation, and has a longer track record with regulators and payers alike.

Mature progression-free survival data from both Morninglyte and SOUNDTRACK-F1 are what oncologists and investors will need before the competitive picture clarifies. Until full Phase III readouts land — plausibly within two to three years — the clinical significance of AstraZeneca's Stockholm presentation remains, on the evidence available, genuinely open.
