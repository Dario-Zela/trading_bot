Oil slump revives disinflation read in UK bonds; rate-cut bets edge forward

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, whose rate path has become hostage to energy price swings far beyond its control. — Diliff / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
UK gilts rallied and BoE rate-cut bets revived after oil's 10%-plus Hormuz-driven plunge erased the inflation risk premium that had pushed 10-year yields above 5% earlier this year.

The drop in oil prices triggered by the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz delivered an abrupt lesson in how quickly an exogenous shock can reorder a sovereign bond market. UK gilts firmed sharply on the news — pushing the 10-year yield down around 20 basis points to 4.65% — as traders recalibrated inflation expectations and pulled forward the point at which the Bank of England might begin easing.

The repricing was a near-mirror image of what the same waterway had done to gilts earlier in the year. When Iran first restricted tanker traffic through Hormuz, 10-year gilt yields surged above 5% for the first time since 2008, as energy-cost pass-through threatened to keep headline CPI elevated well into 2027. Market pricing flipped: from two anticipated Bank of England cuts to bets on two or three hikes. The disinflationary narrative that had dominated BoE communication through late 2025 was unceremoniously shelved.

The energy pivot

The Hormuz deal reverses much of that, and quickly. Brent crude fell more than 10% to around $89 a barrel; WTI dropped a similar margin to roughly $81. That strips away the principal near-term upside risk to UK inflation projections, particularly in the two-to-three-year horizon where gilt markets concentrate expectations. The forward curve shifted accordingly — rate-cut pricing re-emerged where there had been none 48 hours prior.

Going into the Bank's next Monetary Policy Committee meeting on 18 June, markets are widely pricing a hold at 3.75%. ING economist James Smith noted that a summer rate rise now "looks unlikely" given the oil slide and softening economic data. But the vote composition matters: the expected 7-2 hold versus a closer split would signal something meaningfully different about where the MPC's centre of gravity lies as energy dynamics shift again.

Fiscal headroom enters the frame

A second thread runs beneath the gilt market's reaction: fiscal fragility. The Office for Budget Responsibility's March 2026 forecast put the government's headroom under its own fiscal rules at £23.6 billion — real, but not a generous cushion against the kind of yield volatility that defined the first five months of 2026. When gilts approached 5%, that headroom was arithmetically squeezed; the Hormuz-driven compression eases the pressure, without eliminating it.

The OBR now assesses fiscal rules just once a year, reducing the frequency with which market moves can force a formal official reckoning. That insulates the Chancellor from emergency adjustments between reviews, but it also means traders are setting gilt yields against outdated official arithmetic as the macro picture shifts beneath them.

The disinflationary window that opened overnight is real but demonstrably fragile. If oil retraces — or if wage data due later this month confirms the services CPI stickiness that troubled the MPC through spring — the repricing could reverse as fast as it came. The 18 June vote split will be the first hard read on whether the Bank views this as a durable shift in the inflation outlook or a temporary reprieve dressed up as one.
