UK economy cools on soft jobs and retail data as Bank of England holds rates

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, which held Bank Rate at 3.75% on 30 July amid a 6-3 vote split. — George Rex / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Unemployment held at 4.9% while vacancies fell to an 11-year low and payrolls shrank 78,000 — yet the BoE's dissenters voted for a hike, not a cut.

Britain's labour market is not collapsing. It is doing something slower and, in its way, more unsettling: quietly running out of new jobs to offer, while the headline unemployment rate stays put and gives little away.

In one sentence: Unemployment held at 4.9% while job vacancies fell to a 707,000, an 11-year low, and payrolled employment shrank 78,000 year-on-year — a divergence the Bank of England is watching closely even as it holds rates at 3.75%.

Three data points landed on the Bank of England's desk this month, each unremarkable alone, unsettling together. Job vacancies fell to their lowest level since late 2014. Retail sales slipped for the first time in three months. And the Bank's own rate-setters, far from uniting behind the case for lower borrowing costs that a cooling jobs market would suggest, split three ways in the opposite direction — worried enough about inflation to vote for a hike.

A vacancy board that keeps thinning

The headline unemployment rate, 4.9% in the three months to June, has barely moved in over a year and gives the impression of a labour market holding steady. The vacancy numbers tell a different story. ONS data released 18 August showed job openings fell to 707,000 in the May-to-July period, down 6,000 on the quarter and 19,000 on the year — the weakest reading since the September-to-November window of 2014, excluding the pandemic collapse.

Payrolled employment, which the ONS tracks through real-time tax data, fell by 78,000 between June 2025 and June 2026, a 0.3% year-on-year decline. Early estimates for July suggest the drop had widened to 94,000. Wholesale and retail bore the brunt, shedding 75,000 employees, while administrative and support services added 63,000 — evidence of churn as much as outright contraction, but churn tilted the wrong way.

The ratio of unemployed people to each vacancy has sat at roughly 2.5 since the autumn of 2025, a level of tightness that has stopped easing further but shows no sign of reversing either. The ONS pointed to smaller firms pulling back from hiring altogether: "Feedback from our Vacancy Survey suggests that some firms may not be recruiting because of increases in labour costs and other operating expenses," the agency said in its release. Rob Wood, chief UK economist at Pantheon Macroeconomics, read the numbers as confirmation of a familiar trend rather than a fresh alarm: "The slightly weaker jobs picture than expected suggests the labour market is still easing, but only very gradually now." Sterling still marked it down, slipping to $1.3526 against the dollar on the day.

Wage growth, notably, has not cracked. Regular pay rose 3.5% in the three months to June, up from 3.4%, with total earnings growth at 4.1%. That combination — falling vacancies, flat unemployment, resilient pay — is the signature of an economy where employers are freezing new hiring long before they resort to layoffs.

The high street loses a step

Three days after the jobs data, on 21 August, the ONS reported that retail sales volumes fell 0.5% in July, the first monthly decline since April and a reversal after gains of 0.7% in June and 1.3% in May. Clothing sales fell hardest, down 2.7%, as promotions that retailers had pulled forward into June left July looking soft by comparison, compounded by an unusually hot month that kept shoppers away from the high street and, apparently, in front of televisions for the football.

Economists were largely inclined to read the fall as noise rather than a turn in the consumer story. "Consumers have not stopped spending, but the boosts from weather, events and summer demand are now fading," said Cande Cooper of Deloitte. Hai-Ly Nguyen at McKinsey went further: "July's monthly fall looks less like a collapse in demand, but more of a timing effect of the heatwave and previous promotions pulled forward into June." Supermarkets, boosted by warm-weather drinks sales, were the one bright spot. Taken over the three months to July, sales volumes were still up 1.1% on the prior quarter — evidence that consumers are pulling back at the margin, not retreating.

A hawkish split, not a dovish one

What makes the picture harder to read cleanly is the Bank of England's own posture. At its 30 July meeting, the Monetary Policy Committee held Bank Rate at 3.75% for a fifth consecutive time, a decision that surprised nobody. The vote split, 6-3, did surprise: the three dissenters — Megan Greene, Huw Pill and Catherine Mann — voted not for a cut but for a quarter-point hike, to 4%.

Their reasoning had little to do with the jobs market and everything to do with the risk that Middle East conflict-driven energy prices feed into a longer inflation problem. Minutes from the meeting noted the dissenters were "less reassured on the underlying disinflationary process," concerned that "second-round effects could be material," and mindful that inflation had already run above the 2% target for more than five years. CPI inflation, at 2.6% in June, jumped to 2.9% in July — the first acceleration since March — largely on the back of a 13% rise in Ofgem's energy price cap, which pushed gas prices up 14.7% on the month. Core inflation held at 2.6%, still above forecast.

Governor Andrew Bailey had already signalled in July that cuts were "off the table at the moment." Gilt markets have registered the tension: the 10-year yield pushed above 5%, its highest since late July, as renewed Iran-related oil price pressure added to inflation-hawk arguments even as the labour-market data built a case in the opposite direction.

What ties it together

The uncomfortable read for the Bank is that its two main inputs are now pulling apart. The labour market — vacancies at an 11-year low, payrolls shrinking, hiring frozen at the margin — is the kind of signal that has historically preceded rate cuts by two or three quarters. The inflation backdrop, aggravated by an energy shock tied to Middle East tensions rather than domestic demand, is instead pushing three of nine committee members toward a hike. Retail sales, wobbling but not breaking, sit in between, offering no tiebreaker.

The next Monetary Policy Committee decision falls on 17 September, with markets currently pricing roughly a 76% probability of another hold. Between now and then, the August CPI print and a fresh vacancies reading will do much of the talking: if job openings keep falling at their current pace while inflation stays elevated on energy costs, the Bank will be forced to choose which risk it fears more — a cooling jobs market it has so far described as "gradual," or an inflation problem that has now outlasted its target for half a decade.
