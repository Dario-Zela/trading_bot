Thousands of sex offenders in England and Wales could get libido-suppressing drugs

HM Prison Whatton in Nottinghamshire, the original site of the libido-suppressing medication pilot for sex offenders. — Donnylad / Geograph, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
A voluntary prison scheme could offer libido-suppressing drugs to roughly 3,750 sex offenders in England and Wales from December 2028, reviving questions about consent behind bars.

About one in four of England and Wales's roughly 15,000 imprisoned sex offenders — some 3,750 men — could be offered libido-suppressing medication under a prison scheme officials hope to roll out nationally from December 2028, according to reporting on the government's sentencing reforms.[^1] The drugs are already being trialled on a voluntary basis in a handful of prisons in the south-west of England, and the pilot is due to expand to 20 prisons across the north-west and north-east from November 2026.[^2]

The medication comes in two forms: SSRIs such as sertraline, which are more familiar as antidepressants but also appear to dull intrusive sexual thoughts, and anti-androgens, which suppress testosterone production and, with it, libido.[^2] Officials call the programme MMPSA — Medication to Manage Problematic Sexual Arousal.[^2] Forensic psychologist Prof Belinda Winder, who has evaluated the treatment at its original site, HMP Whatton in Nottinghamshire, described prisoners for whom "images of sex flood their minds all day"; one participant told researchers the medication let him watch a film without constantly scanning it for sexual content.[^3]

Voluntary, for now

The scheme's architects are careful to stress that participation is a choice, not a condition of parole or release. But the label sits awkwardly with the setting. A prisoner offered a treatment that might be read, rightly or wrongly, as improving his odds with a parole board is not choosing from a position of ordinary freedom — a tension Oxford researcher Lisa Forsberg has pointed to directly, noting the state already imposes plenty on prisoners without consent, from confinement to quarantine, which makes the insistence on voluntariness for this one intervention worth scrutinising rather than taking at face value.[^4]

That tension will sharpen if ministers act on a recommendation, floated last year by then justice secretary Shabana Mahmood, to examine a mandatory version for the most serious offenders. Mahmood told the Commons that studies pointed to a 60% reduction in reoffending among treated men.[^5] Forensic psychiatrist Prof Don Grubin was sceptical mandatory treatment would ever get off the ground, arguing that "to simply make somebody take the treatment would be very unethical and most doctors would be resistant to it."[^5] Government sources now suggest the mandatory strand is being quietly re-evaluated under the administration of Prime Minister Andy Burnham, even as the voluntary expansion presses ahead — driven in part by the more prosaic pressure of a prison system still short of cells.[^6]

Not new, and not simple

England and Wales would not be pioneering here. Germany's Dunkelfeld project offers anti-androgen therapy alongside counselling on a voluntary basis; South Korea and Poland have gone further, writing court-ordered chemical castration into law for repeat or serious offenders.[^7] The Czech Republic remains the only European state that still permits surgical castration, a practice the Council of Europe has repeatedly criticised.[^7] The variation matters: voluntary programmes with genuine psychological support behind them look ethically and clinically quite different from state-compelled ones, even when the drugs involved are the same.

The treatment is not without medical cost. Long-term anti-androgen use is linked to osteoporosis, cardiovascular strain, fatigue and mood changes, and clinicians caution it is unsuitable for offenders whose behaviour is driven by anger or substance misuse rather than compulsive arousal.[^2] [^3] A 2010 Cochrane review of drug treatments for sexual offenders found the evidence base thin — few randomised controlled trials, mostly small and dated — a gap that has not been substantially closed since.[^8]

What to watch

The practical test comes well before December 2028. The 20-prison expansion beginning in November 2026 will generate the first real data on uptake and reoffending at scale, and that interim evidence — not the original south-west pilot — is what officials say will determine whether the national rollout proceeds on schedule. Whether the mandatory question resurfaces, and whether "voluntary" survives contact with a system where offenders have every incentive to say yes, are the threads worth following as the pilot grows.
