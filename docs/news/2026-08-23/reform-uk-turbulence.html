Reform UK's rise hits turbulence: Labour narrows the gap, infighting and Badenoch rebuff

Prime Minister Andy Burnham, whose Labour Party briefly drew level with Reform UK in YouGov's tracker this month — Alecsandra Dragoi / No 10 Downing Street
In one sentence
Labour briefly drew level with Reform UK in one poll before Reform retook the lead a week later — noise, not yet a trend, amid Reform infighting and a defiant Badenoch.

Andy Burnham has been prime minister for barely a month, and already the pollsters are arguing about whether he has changed the shape of British politics or merely borrowed some rounding error from Reform UK. A YouGov poll for The Times and Sky News, conducted August 9–10 among 2,417 adults, put Labour on 24% and Reform on 22% — the first time Labour has led that pollster's tracker since Reform overtook it in early 2025. A week later, the same tracker had Reform back in front, 24% to 22%. The lead changed hands twice in a fortnight, which tells you less about a realignment than about the width of a two-point margin of error.

In one sentence: Labour has briefly drawn level with Reform UK in the polls and Burnham's ratings have jumped, but a single volatile poll, a feuding Reform UK and a defiant Badenoch add up to noise, not a clear election trajectory.

A bounce, not yet a trend

What is not in dispute is that Burnham's personal numbers have moved. YouGov's August favourability tracker gives him a net score of +13 — the best reading for any prime minister since Boris Johnson's early-pandemic honeymoon in 2020 — against -16 for Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch and -44 for Nigel Farage. Separately, Opinium has him at net +11 approval, ahead of Badenoch by 20 points and Farage by 22 as best prime minister. Burnham, who took over from Keir Starmer on July 20 after Starmer's resignation in the wake of Labour's losses in May's local and regional elections, has leaned on cost-of-living measures, including a VAT cut on energy bills, to reset the party's image. Even so, YouGov's pollster noted voters still see the government as behind on the two issues that matter most to them — the cost of living and immigration — which is a reminder that a leader's personal glow and a party's vote share can move at different speeds. Reform, for its part, has spent the past six months averaging between 22% and 26% on YouGov's tracker; a single poll putting Labour ahead is a data point, not a pattern.

Farage's discipline problem

Reform's own week has been rockier than its topline numbers suggest. Tim Montgomerie — a former Conservative strategist who defected to advise Farage — was suspended from the party on August 17 after giving an interview to The Spectator in which he raised concerns about the party's tone, its approach to tactical voting, and its internal structures. The suspension, ordered by general secretary Tom Waterhouse and party chair Lee Anderson, reportedly caught Farage himself off guard, and Montgomerie says he is contesting it. It lands awkwardly: Reform's annual conference opens in Birmingham on September 3, billed internally as the test of what the party calls its "professionalisation drive" — the effort to look less like an insurgency and more like a government-in-waiting. A senior adviser being shown the door days before that showcase, amid renewed scrutiny over undeclared gifts to the party, is not the kind of story Farage wanted heading into it.

Badenoch closes the door

The third thread running through the week is the Conservative Party's attempt to stop the bleeding to Reform. Speaking at the Edinburgh Fringe, Badenoch ruled out any post-election arrangement with Farage's party, saying she would rather force another general election in a hung parliament than preside over what she called a "monstrous Reform/Conservative hybrid" government. She was blunter still about the MPs who have already jumped ship, calling them "some of the most dishonest people I have ever met." It is a defensive posture — a leader trying to hold a shrinking party together by drawing a hard line against the movement absorbing its voters and its politicians — and it leaves the Conservatives with little room to manoeuvre if the next election produces the kind of fragmented parliament the current polling implies.

What it means for the risk premium

Taken together, the week is less a turning point than a snapshot of a three-way race that has not settled: a popular new prime minister whose personal ratings outrun his party's, a Reform UK managing internal strain just as it tries to present itself as fit to govern, and a Conservative Party boxed in by a leader who has publicly foreclosed the coalition option that might otherwise cushion a bad result. For sterling and UK assets, none of this resolves the underlying uncertainty that has kept a political risk premium in the currency and in gilts — it merely shows that premium is being priced off genuinely volatile inputs. A poll swinging the lead twice in two weeks is not a basis for repricing anything; what would move the needle is a sustained run of polls showing the same order, or a Reform conference in September that either papers over the Montgomerie affair or deepens it. Both are worth watching before drawing any conclusion about where this parliament is heading.
