SEC proposes new 'Regulation Crypto Assets' framework

The Securities and Exchange Commission's headquarters in Washington, D.C. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The SEC proposed its first bespoke crypto fundraising rule, but left exchange and broker-dealer registration for trading venues unresolved.

The Securities and Exchange Commission has spent most of the past decade regulating crypto by lawsuit — suing exchanges, token issuers and lenders one at a time and letting judges sort out which coins counted as securities. On Tuesday it tried something different: a rule.

The proposal, formally titled Regulation Crypto Assets, would create the SEC's first bespoke offering regime for what it calls "covered investment contracts" — tokens sold to fund a project, as opposed to tokens that function more like commodities or software. It replaces years of speeches, no-action letters and enforcement settlements with something issuers can actually read in advance.

Two lanes, a safe harbor, and a $75 million ceiling

The mechanics are narrower than the framing suggests. Issuers get two new exemptions from Securities Act registration: a "startup" lane capped at $5 million raised over four years, with no accredited-investor screening, and a "fundraising" lane capped at $75 million every 12 months, split into a $20 million tier with no audit requirement and a $75 million tier that does require audited financials and ongoing reporting. A separate safe harbor lets a token exit securities status entirely once its issuer has finished — or permanently abandoned — the managerial work it promised investors. The rules would also preempt state-level registration, ending a scenario where a token compliant in Washington could still trigger paperwork in Sacramento.

Chairman Paul Atkins, who has pushed the SEC's "Project Crypto" agenda since taking over in April 2025, called the framework a way to give crypto projects "bespoke pathways to raise capital in the U.S., while providing appropriate investor protections." The proposal advanced by written vote from Atkins and commissioners Hester Peirce and Mark Uyeda, with no dissent recorded — a function of the Commission now being entirely Republican since Caroline Crenshaw, its last Democratic member and most consistent crypto skeptic, departed in January.

What it doesn't settle

The gap is on the trading side, which is where the day-to-day compliance headaches actually live. The proposing release says plainly that Regulation Crypto Assets "does not address" whether the venues where these tokens change hands afterward must register as exchanges, brokers or dealers under the Exchange Act — the questions that determine how Coinbase, Kraken and their peers structure their businesses. Tokenized stocks, a product category exchanges have been racing to launch, remain blocked by existing Reg NMS trading rules the proposal leaves untouched. TD Cowen's Jaret Seiberg described the release as "the first of several rulemakings the SEC will undertake to provide regulatory certainty for crypto assets" — a way of saying the issuance side is done and the harder, secondary-market side is still to come.

Comments are due October 20. Nothing in the proposal takes effect until the Commission votes again to adopt a final rule, likely months away, and that vote will land whenever the exchange-registration question finally reaches the agenda — the piece that will decide whether trading venues, not just issuers, get the clarity this proposal promises.
