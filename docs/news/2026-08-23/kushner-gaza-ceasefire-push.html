Kushner presses Netanyahu, meets Hamas leader in Gaza ceasefire push

Jared Kushner, Trump's Middle East envoy, led the diplomatic push that included a rare direct meeting with Hamas's leader. — Andrea Hanks / The White House, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Kushner held rare direct talks with Hamas leader Khalil al-Hayya in Egypt, then met Netanyahu, but secured no firm commitment to the stalled Gaza disarmament roadmap.

The last time an American envoy sat face to face with a Hamas leader was hard to find precedent for. Washington has spent two decades routing every word to the group through intermediaries — Qatari ministers, Egyptian intelligence chiefs, the occasional Turkish official — because Hamas sits on the US terrorism list and direct contact has long been treated as politically radioactive. So when Jared Kushner, Donald Trump's son-in-law and Middle East envoy, spent more than two hours in a room with Khalil al-Hayya, Hamas's newly elected overall leader, in the Egyptian resort town of Alamein on August 16, it registered as something unusual even by the standards of a conflict that has produced no shortage of diplomatic firsts.

What it has not yet produced, ten months after a ceasefire quieted the worst of the fighting, is a settled peace. Kushner's trip — Hamas one day, Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu in Jerusalem the next — was the latest attempt to drag a stalled US-backed roadmap back to life. The question hanging over both meetings is whether this round looks any different from the ones that preceded it.

A room that doesn't usually get used

Kushner arrived in Egypt with Nickolay Mladenov, the Bulgarian diplomat serving as the Board of Peace's High Representative for Gaza, and Tony Blair, the former British prime minister who — despite the baggage of his own record in the region — has become one of the plan's most visible international faces. On the other side of the table was al-Hayya, who was elected Hamas's overall leader on July 20 after a run-off against Khaled Meshaal and has operated mostly from Doha since leaving Gaza before the October 7, 2023 attack. Egypt's intelligence chief Hassan Rashad, a senior Qatari diplomat and a Turkish official filled out the room — the mediating architecture that has underpinned nearly every round of Gaza diplomacy since the war began.

According to officials briefed on the session, Hamas reaffirmed a commitment to disarm and demilitarize Gaza, and the two sides worked through what a US statement called turning the roadmap's obligations into "concrete, verifiable steps." Kushner also met separately with Egyptian President Abdel Fattah el-Sisi. The following day he flew to Jerusalem for a nearly four-hour session with Netanyahu — read against the scale of the shuttle diplomacy, itself a marker of how much remains unresolved.

What moved, and what didn't

The Netanyahu meeting produced one concrete shift: Kushner and the prime minister agreed that a US military general should oversee the initial handover of Hamas weapons, with the arms destroyed rather than transferred to the Palestinian oversight committee originally envisioned in the plan. It is a small mechanical change, but a telling one — it signals Israel wants an American officer, not a Palestinian body, holding custody of anything Hamas gives up. The two sides also agreed there will be no Israeli troop withdrawal from Gaza under the current terms, walking back an element that had been central to the 15-point roadmap unveiled by the Board of Peace on July 30.

What didn't happen was a firm commitment to the plan itself. Netanyahu had publicly rejected the 15-point document on August 9 — "Israel does not accept the 15-point document," he said at the time — a rare open break with the Trump administration from an Israeli leader who has otherwise worked to stay in Washington's good graces. Kushner described the Jerusalem meeting as a chance to clear up "misunderstandings and misinformation," language that suggests persuasion rather than resolution.

Inside Netanyahu's own coalition, the resistance is louder than diplomatic phrasing lets on. National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir, whose party quit the government once already over a Gaza ceasefire he called a "capitulation to Hamas," has condemned the renewed push. That opposition matters more than usual just now: Israel's parliament has been dissolved and the country heads to an election on October 27 that is widely framed as a referendum on Netanyahu's handling of the war. A coalition partner willing to walk again gives him little room to make concessions Hamas would recognize as real.

The ceasefire that never quite becomes a peace

The roadmap being negotiated over is itself a sequel. Trump's original 20-point plan produced a ceasefire that took effect on October 10, 2025, followed by a UN Security Council resolution endorsing it that November and the return of the last hostages' remains in January. But the pause in major fighting never evolved into the reconstruction, withdrawal, and governance steps the plan promised. Gaza's health ministry says roughly 1,200 people have been killed in near-daily Israeli strikes since the ceasefire nominally began — a figure that sits uneasily next to the word "ceasefire" itself, and underscores why the Board of Peace felt compelled to write a second, narrower roadmap in July focused specifically on disarmament.

That is the pattern this latest round has to break: an agreement in principle, a burst of shuttle diplomacy, incremental technical fixes — a general instead of a committee, weapons destroyed instead of stored — and then a stall once the harder political questions resurface. Kushner's meeting with al-Hayya was rare enough to make headlines on its own terms. Whether it was also the meeting that finally moved Gaza past the pattern is a different question, and one the coming weeks, not the meeting itself, will answer.

What comes next

Watch for whether Hamas takes any visible step toward the weapons handover under the US general's oversight — the clearest test of whether Alamein produced substance or just atmosphere. Netanyahu's political calculus will keep tightening as the October 27 election nears, giving him less incentive to move before voters do. And the Board of Peace, having now written two roadmaps in as many months, will face pressure to show the second one lands where the first one didn't.
