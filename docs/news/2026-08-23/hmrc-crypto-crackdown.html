HMRC sends 81,000 warning letters to crypto holders in tax crackdown

HM Revenue & Customs headquarters at 100 Parliament Street, Westminster — Carlos Delgado / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
HMRC's crypto warning letters have nearly tripled since 2023-24, and from 2027 automatic reporting from 52 countries will remove the need to ask nicely.

HM Revenue & Customs sent 81,172 letters, emails and texts to suspected crypto tax dodgers in the year to April 2026 — up 25% on the 65,000 sent the year before, and nearly triple the 27,714 issued in 2023-24. The figures, obtained by accountancy group UHY Hacker Young under freedom of information rules, mark the sharpest escalation yet in HMRC's pursuit of the estimated seven million UK adults who now hold crypto assets, worth roughly £12.9bn between them.

The letters are not tax bills. They tell recipients that HMRC's records show crypto trades that don't match what was declared on a tax return, and give 60 days to review and, if necessary, disclose. Nobody who gets one has automatically been found to owe anything. But the substance behind them is blunt: any disposal of crypto — selling for cash, swapping one token for another, spending it — can trigger UK capital gains tax, even if the holder never converted a penny into sterling. The annual tax-free allowance for gains has been cut sharply in recent years, from £12,300 to just £3,000, catching far more casual investors in the net than before.

From nudge to net

What changes next year is where HMRC's information comes from. Under the OECD's Cryptoasset Reporting Framework, exchanges in 52 countries — including the Channel Islands, Cayman Islands, Ireland and Liechtenstein — must start handing UK customer data directly to HMRC, with first reports due by 31 May 2027. UK-based platforms are already required to collect verified tax ID numbers and wallet data under equivalent domestic rules that took effect on 1 January 2026.

That is the real pivot in this story. Warning letters rely on HMRC noticing a mismatch and asking politely. Automated third-party reporting removes the noticing problem entirely — the data arrives whether or not a taxpayer has been forthcoming.

"Once HMRC has this data, then tax investigations into cryptocurrency investors will be like shooting fish in a barrel," said Neela Chauhan, a partner at UHY Hacker Young.

What holders are weighing

For now, the incentive runs toward getting ahead of the data rather than waiting for a letter. HMRC operates a voluntary disclosure route for undeclared crypto gains, and penalties for unprompted disclosure are markedly lower than those imposed after an investigation is opened — where fines can run up to 100% of the tax owed, on top of the tax itself. Once cross-border reporting goes live in 2027, that calculus tightens: HMRC won't need a tip-off or a data mismatch flagged by its own systems to open a case, because the exchange data will already be sitting in front of it.

That timing detail is what turns a compliance story into a markets one. If a meaningful slice of UK holders opt to settle liabilities or unwind positions ahead of the 2027 reporting deadline rather than risk a post-hoc investigation, the selling would be concentrated and preemptive rather than driven by price. It's a small, UK-specific pressure next to the forces moving crypto prices globally, but it is a genuine one, and it has a fixed date attached to it.

What's next

The next marker is 31 May 2027, when the first CARF reports land at HMRC from dozens of jurisdictions simultaneously. Between now and then, expect the letter count to keep climbing — HMRC has scaled up nudge campaigns before each major reporting change lands, using them as a bridge until the automated data does the work instead.
