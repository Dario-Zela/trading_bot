Eurozone business activity hits nine-month high on German manufacturing surge

The European Central Bank's headquarters on the Frankfurt skyline, where policymakers weigh the eurozone's factory-led rebound against sticky inflation. — Daniel Vorndran / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
The flash composite PMI rose to 52.1 in August with German manufacturing output growing at its fastest pace since January 2022.

The eurozone economy picked up more speed than expected in August, and this time the lift came from an unlikely source: German factories.

S&P Global's flash composite Purchasing Managers' Index, compiled from surveys of thousands of firms across the currency bloc, rose to 52.1 this month from 52.0 in July — a nine-month high, its best reading since last November. Anything above 50 signals expansion, and the eurozone has now sat above that line for eight straight months. The surprise was where the strength came from. For most of the post-pandemic recovery, services — tourism, banking, consulting — carried the eurozone while manufacturing languished. In August, the reverse was true.

Germany's factories do the heavy lifting

Germany's flash manufacturing PMI jumped to 54.1 from 52.2, its strongest reading in 51 months — a run stretching back to before the energy-price shock of 2022. Factory output itself grew at the fastest pace since January 2022, according to the survey panel, with firms pointing to demand for AI-linked technology equipment and defense hardware as the two swing factors. Germany's post-2022 rearmament push and a broader European drive to rebuild domestic capacity in chips, sensors and industrial electronics appear to be showing up in hard order books rather than just political announcements.

The employment numbers back up the signal. Eurozone-wide hiring expanded for the first time in 2026, and manufacturers ended a 38-month streak of net job losses — the longest contraction in factory payrolls the survey has recorded. New export orders rose for the first time since February 2022, a detail worth sitting with given how much of the past three years has been dominated by European exporters losing ground to Chinese competition and US tariff threats.

But the German number is lopsided. Services activity in Germany actually fell, with that sub-index dropping to 48.5 — meaning the country's headline strength is a manufacturing story, full stop, not a broad-based upswing. Eurozone-wide services held at 51.7, a steady but unspectacular pace.

Not every corner of the bloc is joining in

France moved the opposite direction. Its composite reading fell further into contraction than forecasters expected, with heatwaves cited as a drag on services activity through the month — a reminder that flash PMIs pick up real-world noise, not just business-cycle signal. Manufacturers across the bloc also flagged supply-chain disruption tied to the Middle East, where the Iran ceasefire framework has been fraying and Strait of Hormuz shipping risk has stayed elevated; firms reported extended supplier delivery times and thinning finished-goods inventories, an echo of the Red Sea disruptions of 2024.

Price data offered the one piece of good news for the European Central Bank. Input-cost inflation slowed to a six-month low, and output-price inflation eased to its weakest pace since March, led by German producers cutting prices to move goods. Eurozone headline inflation stood at 2.9% in July, still above the ECB's 2% target but no longer accelerating.

Why traders are hedging their bets

"The euro zone economy has been quite resilient so far, and momentum remains surprisingly decent," said Bert Colijn, an economist at ING, of the release. Not everyone read the details as an unambiguous green light. Claus Vistesen of Pantheon Macroeconomics flagged the inflation undertow, warning that near-term risks are tilted toward headline inflation pushing back above 3% and toward 4% before it settles — a scenario that would complicate any dovish turn from Frankfurt.

That tension is precisely why this is a flash reading, not the final word. S&P Global's preliminary PMI surveys roughly 85% of the eventual panel; the final composite print, due September 1, can and does move — sometimes by several tenths of a point, occasionally enough to flip a marginal signal. Markets reacted anyway: the euro firmed against the dollar and bond yields ticked higher as traders trimmed bets on near-term ECB rate cuts, wagering that a central bank facing a 52.8 manufacturing print and sticky prices has less room to ease than it did a month ago.

The eurozone GDP arithmetic implied by August's composite reading — roughly 0.3% quarter-on-quarter growth in the third quarter — would mark a respectable, if unspectacular, pace. Whether it holds depends on two things traders will be watching closely: the final PMI print on September 1, which will show whether the German manufacturing surge survives revision, and the ECB's Governing Council meeting on September 10, where policymakers will have to weigh a stronger factory sector against inflation that has not yet retreated to target.
