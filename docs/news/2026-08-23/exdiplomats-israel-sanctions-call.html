Former UK and French diplomats urge sanctions on Israel over Palestine 'erasure'

Ma'ale Adumim, the Israeli settlement adjoining the E1 corridor whose tendering for new construction prompted the diplomats' letter — Labour Palestine / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Over 100 former UK and French ambassadors, including ex-UN envoys, urged their governments to suspend trade and arms deals with Israel over West Bank settlement expansion.

The letter runs to seven concrete demands and lands at a pointed moment. Two days after Israel's housing ministry opened bidding on 1,200 settler homes in the E1 corridor east of Jerusalem — land that would sever the West Bank in two — more than 100 former French and British ambassadors have told their governments that recognising a Palestinian state on paper means nothing if nobody stops the ground being taken out from under it.

"Palestine is being erased before our eyes."

That line, from the open letter published in Le Monde on Saturday, is the closest thing the document has to a thesis. It was signed by 52 former French diplomats and 50 former British ones — a list heavy with people who spent careers either in Jerusalem or in the rooms where Middle East policy gets made. Sir Jeremy Greenstock and Sir Emyr Jones Parry, both former UK ambassadors to the United Nations, are on it. So are Sir Dominick Chilcott and Sir Kieran Prendergast, and a run of former French and British consuls-general who served in Jerusalem itself — Stanislas de Laboulaye, Frédéric Desagneaux, Régis Koetschet, Alain Rémy, Sir Vincent Fean. People, in other words, who watched the West Bank settlement project up close for years before deciding, now retired and free to say so, that it has passed the point where statements of concern do any good.

What the letter actually asks for

Addressed to Prime Minister Andy Burnham, with copies to President Emmanuel Macron and Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband, the letter does not stop at rhetoric. It sets out seven specific measures: uphold rulings from the International Court of Justice and International Criminal Court; suspend the EU-Israel and UK-Israel trade agreements; halt arms transfers and military cooperation with Israel; guarantee humanitarian access to Gaza through UNRWA and other UN agencies; ban trade — goods, investment, insurance, financial services — with Israeli settlements; warn companies bidding on the E1 project that they face legal exposure; and criminalise the purchase of occupied Palestinian land by French or British citizens, stripping charitable status from organisations that fund settlement activity.

On the substance of the accusation, the signatories are blunt. They describe Palestinian house demolitions and settler violence in East Jerusalem and the rest of the West Bank as amounting to ethnic cleansing, and argue that Israel can no longer credibly be treated as a democracy operating within the rule of law. The letter's core argument is procedural as much as moral: Britain and France, as permanent members of the UN Security Council, share both the legal standing and — the diplomats say — an obligation to use it. Recognition of a Palestinian state, which both governments extended in 2025, risks becoming what one signatory called an empty gesture unless it is backed by measures that actually change Israeli policy on the ground.

Why now, and why this pairing of governments

The timing follows directly from the E1 tender. On 20 August, the leaders of Canada, France, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands, Norway and the UK issued a joint statement calling the settlement plan "unacceptable," warning it would drive a wedge through the West Bank and undercut the territorial contiguity a Palestinian state would need to function. London went further, summoning Israel's ambassador. Israel's housing ministry set its bidding deadline for just before the country's own elections in late October — a detail that has fed the diplomats' argument that the government in Jerusalem is racing to lock in facts on the ground before any external pressure can bite.

The choice of Burnham and Macron as addressees is not incidental either. Burnham became prime minister in July, and one of his first acts was to install Ed Miliband — a figure with a long personal history of criticising Israeli settlement policy — as foreign secretary. Both men have signalled since taking office that British policy toward Israel needs to shift, though the Foreign Office's public response to an earlier letter from backbench MPs demanding sanctions was notably cautious, reiterating only a commitment to work toward "a sustainable peace between Israel and Palestine." Whether the government treats a letter from 102 former ambassadors — career officials, not politicians — differently remains to be tested.

What separates this from the usual open letter

Governments field open letters from advocacy groups and even sitting MPs routinely, and mostly absorb them without changing course. What makes this one harder to wave off is who wrote it. These are not campaigners; they are the people who used to sit across the table from Israeli officials on behalf of their governments, and who understand exactly how far London and Paris have historically been willing to go — trade suspension and an arms embargo sit well beyond anything either government has done to date, even after two years of war in Gaza and mounting casualties from settler violence in the West Bank. The letter was also placed simultaneously with France 24 and Al Jazeera picking it up within hours of the Le Monde publication, suggesting the signatories coordinated a media rollout rather than simply mailing a letter and hoping it surfaced.

None of that guarantees policy follows. Burnham's government has shown willingness to escalate rhetoric — the ambassador summons over E1 is real — but arms embargoes and trade suspension are a different order of action, one that would ripple into defence-industry contracts and the UK's wider relationship with Washington, which has shown no comparable appetite for restrictions. The next marker to watch is whether Israel proceeds with the E1 tender award despite the joint condemnation from seven governments, and whether Burnham's cabinet responds to this letter with anything beyond the boilerplate it gave the MPs' version. A parliamentary debate or a formal government reply, if either materialises in the coming weeks, will show whether the ex-diplomats' intervention was a signal that moved policy or one more open letter absorbed into the pile.
