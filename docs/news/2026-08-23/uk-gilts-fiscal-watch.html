UK fiscal jitters deepen: 30-year gilt yield at 1998 high, July borrowing overshoots

HM Treasury's entrance at 1 Horse Guards Road, London, seat of Chancellor John Healey's department ahead of the 28 October Budget. — Wutthichai Charoenburi / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
Long-dated gilt yields hit a 28-year high near 5.82% as a July borrowing overshoot squeezes Chancellor Healey's room before his 28 October Budget.

Britain's longest-dated government debt is now more expensive to hold than at any point since 1998. The 30-year gilt yield touched 5.82% this week, edging past the 5.78% peak set in May and the 5.75% level hit last September — each time on the same underlying worry: that the government's sums do not add up, and that the autumn Budget will not be enough to fix them.

In one sentence: Long-dated gilt yields have climbed to a 28-year high near 5.82% as a July borrowing overshoot squeezes Chancellor John Healey's room before his 28 October Budget.

The timing is unkind. Chancellor John Healey, appointed by Prime Minister Andy Burnham in July, has fixed his first Budget for 28 October and has already ruled out raising income tax, VAT or national insurance — the three taxes that raise the most money with the least drama. That leaves him hunting for revenue in smaller, messier places just as the bond market decides to test his credibility.

A borrowing overshoot that wasn't supposed to happen

July is normally a good month for the Treasury's books: self-assessment tax receipts, worth £17.1bn this year, usually tip the monthly balance into surplus. Instead, the Office for National Statistics recorded borrowing of £1.8bn for the month, against an Office for Budget Responsibility forecast of a £500m surplus and City expectations of a flat reading. It was a miss of roughly £2.3bn against forecast in a single month.

The year-to-date picture is a little less bleak — borrowing of £56.7bn since April is about £6bn lower than the same point last year — but it still sits more than £2bn above where the OBR expected it to be. Ashley Webb, an economist at Capital Economics, said the deficit was "on track to be above 4 per cent of GDP for the seventh year in a row," and warned the overshoot would "probably get bigger" as growth slows and the government extends cost-of-living support. Government debt now stands just short of £3 trillion, or 94.1% of GDP.

The practical effect is to narrow Healey's headroom against Labour's own fiscal rules from roughly £24bn in March to about £17bn now, according to Treasury estimates cited by analysts — and some at the Institute for Fiscal Studies think he may need to find £25bn or more in additional revenue simply to stand still. This would be Labour's third tax-raising Budget in three years, following packages of £41.5bn in 2024 and £26bn last November, and with income tax, VAT and national insurance off-limits, the search is turning to less politically obvious levers such as pension tax relief.

Why investors are charging more to lend

The gilt market's response predates the borrowing figures and reaches beyond them. Roughly a quarter of outstanding UK gilts are inflation-linked — the largest share of any major developed economy — which means every fresh inflation scare, including the oil-price jump that followed the collapse of the Iran-US ceasefire and a projectile strike near the Strait of Hormuz, flows almost mechanically into the government's real interest bill. Debt interest payments already exceed £100bn a year.

Nigel Green, chief executive of deVere Group, put the underlying vulnerability bluntly: "Britain carries more debt, less headroom, and a bond market that reacts to inflation more violently than its peers." Analysts estimate that around two-thirds of the recent jump in gilt yields reflects investors simply demanding more compensation to hold UK debt at all — a term-premium effect that the US and eurozone are not paying to nearly the same degree, even as Treasury and Bund yields also climb toward multi-decade highs of their own.

What the Budget now has to do

The fiscal arithmetic and the market's verdict on it are feeding each other. A wider borrowing overshoot narrows the headroom Healey has to work with; a narrower headroom raises the odds of a Budget that disappoints either bond investors or Labour's own backbenchers; and every basis point added to the 30-year yield adds directly to the debt-interest bill that squeezed the headroom in the first place. Healey has called "fiscal discipline the bedrock of our economic stability and national security," but discipline is easier to promise than to fund when three of the biggest revenue-raisers are off the table by manifesto pledge.

The next hard data points are the August borrowing figures, due from the ONS in September, and the OBR's full forecast that will accompany the 28 October Budget itself. Until then, every gilt auction between now and the Budget will be read as a referendum on whether Healey can close the gap — or whether the 1998-era yields are the market's way of pricing in a wider one.
