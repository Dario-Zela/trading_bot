Brussels sets October deadline for China to rebalance €360bn trade gap

Maroš Šefčovič, the EU's trade commissioner, has set an October deadline for Beijing to show progress on rebalancing trade — European Commission / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The EU gave China until October to narrow a record €360bn trade deficit, setting up a joint monitoring system and a possible tariff escalation if talks stall.

Brussels has put a date on the calendar. By October, the European Union wants to see "tangible results" from China on narrowing a goods trade deficit that hit €360 billion in 2025 — a gap that widened 15% in a single year and now runs at roughly €1 billion a day. If Beijing hasn't moved by then, EU officials have made clear they are prepared to reach for tools they have so far kept in the drawer.

The deadline emerged from talks in Brussels between EU Trade Commissioner Maroš Šefčovič and Chinese Commerce Minister Wang Wentao, who met on 29 June and issued a joint statement — the first of its kind since 2019. "Our teams have a clear mandate and an ambitious timetable to deliver tangible results by October this year," Šefčovič said afterward, adding a blunter line for domestic consumption: "A trade deficit of €1 billion a day is unsustainable."

Four workstreams, one clock

The framework agreed in June splits the standoff into four strands: rebalancing trade and investment flows, managing China's export controls on rare earths and other critical materials, protecting intellectual property, and pushing reform at the World Trade Organization. The most concrete deliverable is a joint monitoring mechanism — both sides will work from the same trade-flow data, watching for import surges that cross into a defined "red zone" and trigger political talks before they escalate into disputes.

That data-sharing arrangement matters because it is a genuine change in posture from Beijing, which has historically waved away European complaints about market access as protectionist noise. Wang told his counterpart that existing rare-earth export limits would not disrupt EU supply chains, and Chinese officials have since signalled they may speed up approval of rare-earth export licences for European firms — an olive branch offered against the backdrop of last October's export-control clampdown that briefly shut off supply and forced an emergency round of talks.

The October timing is not incidental. It lands just before EU leaders gather for a summit on 15 October, and Šefčovič has said he will travel to Beijing in the autumn to present what he expects will be the first concrete results of the process — giving the deadline both a political audience and a built-in check-in point.

The deindustrialisation argument

What has hardened Brussels' position is less the headline deficit number than where the imbalance is concentrated. Chinese firms now dominate European supply in solar panels, rare earths, industrial chemicals and robotics, categories the EU considers strategically sensitive rather than merely competitive. In autos, Chinese brands — BYD, Geely, Chery — passed 10% of EU car sales for the first time this year, despite tariffs on Chinese electric vehicles running as high as 35.3%.

The knock-on effect is showing up in European payrolls. Volkswagen has outlined plans to cut up to 100,000 jobs, roughly 15% of its workforce; BMW is trimming headcount by 5% by the end of 2026; Mercedes-Benz has frozen bonuses and is offering voluntary redundancy. Germany, which recorded an €87 billion bilateral deficit with China last autumn and has historically been Europe's most China-friendly capital, is described by officials involved in the talks as having reached a "wake-up call" moment — a shift that has made it easier for Šefčovič to hold a harder line without being outflanked by Berlin's export lobby.

Commission officials frame the underlying structural problem in a single statistic: China accounts for roughly 30% of global manufacturing output but only about 13% of global consumption, a gap that has to clear somewhere, and increasingly it clears through European ports.

What Brussels is building if talks stall

Behind the October deadline sits a piece of leverage the Commission has been quietly assembling: an "overcapacity instrument" that would function as something close to the EU's own version of Section 301 of the US Trade Act, giving Brussels legal authority to impose tariffs, restrict Chinese investment and bar Chinese technology firms from public procurement across the bloc, on a sector-wide basis rather than case by case. The Commission has said it intends to present new trade-defence tools by September — timed, deliberately, to land just before the October checkpoint. Separately, tariff-free steel quotas are already being cut by 47% from July, with out-of-quota duties doubling to 50% through 2031.

Beijing has responded with its own warning, threatening "resolute countermeasures" should the EU proceed with the overcapacity tool, which it characterises as discriminatory. That leaves both sides with an incentive to show the joint monitoring mechanism working before autumn hardens into confrontation — but also little room for either to be seen conceding first.

The next fixed point is Šefčovič's autumn trip to Beijing, expected to land ahead of the 15 October EU summit. Whether he arrives with data showing the trade-flow mechanism catching genuine progress, or with a case for triggering the overcapacity instrument, will shape how European industrial policy — and EUR-denominated assets exposed to the outcome — trade through the final quarter of the year.
