Iran ceasefire memorandum lapses as Hormuz tensions simmer, oil above $86

The Strait of Hormuz, seen from NASA's MODIS satellite, carries roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil trade. — NASA / MODIS, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The June US-Iran ceasefire memorandum expired unrenewed, Trump threatens Hormuz and Oman while Iran threatens to halt oil flows, and crude has climbed above $86.

The truce that briefly calmed the Gulf has run out. The Islamabad Memorandum, signed by Washington and Tehran on June 17 to formalise a ceasefire and open a 60-day window for a permanent settlement, expired on August 17 with the two sides deadlocked over who controls shipping through the Strait of Hormuz and what happens to billions in frozen Iranian assets. President Trump declared the arrangement "over" the following day at a NATO summit in Turkey, branding Iran's leadership "evil, sick people."

Crude has been climbing ever since. West Texas Intermediate settled above $86 a barrel this week — $87.06 on Thursday — while Brent pushed past $94, as traders price in the risk that a chokepoint carrying roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil stays disrupted. Traffic through the strait has not stopped outright, but it has thinned sharply: analysts estimate the conflict has already pulled some 8 million barrels a day of flow out of a corridor that normally carries close to 20 million, with daily transits down from 130-140 before the fighting to as few as 16 on some days this month. Iran has been letting selected cargoes through — Iraqi oil tankers moved unimpeded this week — while warning that could stop.

Threats on both sides

Trump has escalated in kind, threatening Oman with airstrikes "if it gets in the way" of Hormuz talks, floating a plan to designate the strait itself a US territory, and promising what Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent called economic measures "never before seen" — Trump has called it an "ECONOMIC D-DAY" for Tehran. Iran's Supreme National Security Council secretary responded in kind this week, vowing that if any Gulf neighbour joins Washington's pressure campaign, "we will not allow a single drop of oil to pass through the Persian Gulf." Tehran's chief negotiator, Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, has said Iran is prepared for "all-out defense" should the US move further.

The practical effect has been a shadow fleet: oil companies, aided by the US Navy, have taken to running tankers with transponders switched off through the strait to the Gulf of Oman, transferring crude to waiting ships before doubling back — a workaround that keeps some barrels moving but adds cost, delay and risk that shows up in the price at the pump. US gasoline is already running nearly a dollar a gallon above year-ago levels.

With no talks currently scheduled and both governments trading threats rather than proposals, the next marker is whether Washington's promised sanctions package — expected within days — pushes Tehran toward a narrower Hormuz-only arrangement, or hardens the standoff further. Until then, the strait remains the market's clearest geopolitical risk gauge, and oil traders are treating every statement from either capital as a price signal.
