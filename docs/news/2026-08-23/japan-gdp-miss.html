Japan's economy unexpectedly slows as Middle East conflict hits capital spending

The Bank of Japan's Tokyo headquarters, where policymakers face a narrowing path between inflation and a capex slump. — Katorisi / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Japan's GDP grew just 1.1% annualised in Q2 as capex contracted and Middle East-driven energy costs offset the weak yen's export boost.

Japan's economy grew at an annualised 1.1% in the second quarter, the government's Cabinet Office reported this month — a marked deceleration from the revised 1.9% pace in the first quarter and well short of the 2.0% economists had pencilled in. On a quarterly basis, output rose just 0.3%, against forecasts of 0.5%. It marks the third straight quarter of expansion, but the composition of the miss is the story: this was not a demand shock so much as a confidence shock, and it lands at an awkward moment for a central bank trying to normalise policy after a generation of near-zero rates.

Capex goes into reverse

The headline number obscures the more telling detail underneath it. Capital expenditure — spending by companies on equipment, factories and technology — fell 1.2% quarter-on-quarter, equivalent to a 4.6% annualised contraction, against forecasts for a 0.4% gain. It was the second consecutive quarterly decline in business investment, a sequence Japan hasn't managed cleanly to shake since interest rates began rising last year.

That matters more than the topline miss itself. Japan's growth model for much of the past three years has rested on a straightforward trade: a weak yen makes exports and inbound tourism cheap, corporate profits recycle into wages and investment, and the Bank of Japan gradually withdraws stimulus without choking off the recovery. Capex contracting for two straight quarters says companies aren't playing along. Norihiro Yamaguchi, lead Japan economist at Oxford Economics, pointed to a related strain on the export side, noting that "sluggish non-AI-related global economic activities will limit overall export gains" even as semiconductor and AI-linked shipments hold up — meaning the one export engine still firing is a narrow one.

Private consumption, which accounts for more than half of Japanese output, made no contribution to growth in the quarter — flat in real terms for the first time in eight quarters, according to the Cabinet Office breakdown. Domestic demand overall contracted 0.2%. Net trade was the sole positive contributor, adding 0.5 percentage points as exports rose and imports fell — usually a sign of resilience, but here partly a function of households and firms pulling back on purchases of imported goods rather than exporters surging ahead.

The Middle East channel

The conflict radiating from Iran is doing more to shape this data than most trading desks initially priced in. Japan imports nearly all of its crude oil, and the US-Israel confrontation with Iran pushed energy costs higher through the first half of the year, a burden that lands squarely on an economy with almost no domestic energy alternative. Exports to the Middle East fell 17.6% in the first half of 2026 versus a year earlier. Tokyo's own government cut its fiscal-year growth forecast from 1.3% to 0.9% this summer, citing the energy bill from the conflict directly.

The yen compounds rather than offsets this. It touched a 40-year low near 163 to the dollar in late July — its weakest level since 1986 — before Japan and the US mounted a joint intervention on 30-31 July, selling an estimated $85 billion to buy yen, the first such coordinated action since 1998. The relief was partial and short-lived: the currency has already surrendered roughly half its post-intervention gain, trading back near 159 by mid-August. A cheap yen is supposed to be a release valve for exporters. Instead, it is now doing as much to inflate the cost of imported oil and raw materials as it is to cheapen Japanese goods abroad — which is precisely the bind that shows up in the GDP data as flat consumption and shrinking capex, even as headline export volumes tick up.

A narrower path for the BoJ

Governor Kazuo Ueda's central bank raised its policy rate to 1% in June, the highest since 1995, and held it there at its 31 July meeting while flagging core inflation running above the 2% target — ordinarily a green light for a further hike. The next decision falls on 17-18 September, and strategists had leaned toward reading Ueda's tone as mildly hawkish going in. This GDP print complicates that calculus without killing it outright: inflation pressure from energy costs argues for tightening, while a capex contraction and stalled consumption argue for caution. Raising rates into weakening investment risks reinforcing the pullback; holding risks letting an already-weak yen slide further and feeding the same imported-inflation problem the hike was meant to address.

That is the bind worth sitting with. Currency depreciation was supposed to be Japan's cushion — a way to keep exporters competitive while the BoJ inched rates up from zero. What the second-quarter data shows is a version of that trade breaking down when the depreciation is happening alongside an external energy shock rather than instead of one. Companies facing higher input costs and an uncertain geopolitical backdrop are not investing more because their goods are cheaper abroad; they are investing less because the cost side of the ledger has become harder to plan around.

What comes next

The September 17-18 BoJ meeting is the immediate marker, with markets watching for any signal on whether Ueda treats this GDP miss as reason to pause. Beyond that, Yamaguchi's expectation that firms will pass rising energy costs through to consumers in the second half points toward a squeeze on real incomes that could show up in the next round of retail and wage data before the third-quarter GDP release. For traders, the read-through is that yen weakness is unlikely to reverse cleanly even with intervention, and that Japanese corporate earnings — particularly outside the AI-linked export names — carry more downside risk than a 1% policy rate would normally imply.
