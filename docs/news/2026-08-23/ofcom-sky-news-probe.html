Ofcom opens formal investigation into Sky News over Farage harassment complaint

Nigel Farage, whose complaint to Ofcom over a Sky News report has triggered a formal broadcasting investigation. — Laurie Noble / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Ofcom confirmed a formal probe into Sky News after Reform UK alleged a reporter's visit to Farage's daughter's home breached privacy and impartiality rules.

Ofcom has opened a formal investigation into Sky News over a report that Reform UK says amounted to the harassment of Nigel Farage's daughter — the latest skirmish in a increasingly tetchy relationship between the broadcaster and Britain's insurgent party.

The complaint traces back to 6 July, when a Sky News reporter visited a property where Farage's daughter lives, while the Reform leader was abroad. Sky said it was trying to reach Farage for comment on donations he had received from George Cottrell, a longtime associate who has a US fraud conviction. In the broadcast segment that followed, an anchor noted Sky had not contacted Farage's family about the story — a line Reform says left the impression Farage was lying when he denied being reachable, while stripping out the context that, in his own account, his daughter felt "harassed and unsafe" after reporters showed up at her home.

Farage did not mince words about it. "You tell your bosses, you harass my family any more, there will be serious consequences," he said, adding that Sky had "broken all the rules, Leveson and everything else" — a nod to the 2012 inquiry into press standards that still shapes how British broadcasters think about doorstepping and privacy.

What Ofcom is actually weighing

Reform's complaint invokes three separate strands of the broadcasting code: accuracy, due impartiality, and respect for privacy. That breadth is itself notable — fairness-and-privacy complaints are usually narrow, but this one asks the regulator to rule on both how the story was told and whether the news-gathering itself crossed a line.

Sky has pushed back hard on the facts. The broadcaster says it was not at the property at 7.41am as alleged, that its reporter never interacted with Farage's daughter, and that approaching a public figure's associates for comment on a story of "legitimate public interest" is, in its words, "a well-established and routine journalistic practice." True to form, Ofcom has declined to confirm any details beyond acknowledging the case has been accepted for investigation, citing its standard confidentiality around fairness and privacy complaints.

Context, not coincidence

The Cottrell donations that triggered the original inquiry are not the only money trail attached to Farage this year. He is separately under investigation by the Commons standards watchdog over an undeclared £5 million gift from crypto financier Christopher Harborne, received before he took his seat in Parliament. None of that bears directly on the Ofcom case, but it explains why Sky News was pursuing him for comment in the first place — and why Reform, mid-surge in the polls, has an incentive to make the story about media conduct rather than donor scrutiny.

Ofcom investigations of this kind typically run for months and rarely produce anything more dramatic than a published finding and, occasionally, a requirement that the broadcaster air a correction. There is no realistic path from this case to a licence sanction, let alone anything that touches Sky's ultimate owner, Comcast, or the wider media sector. The interest here is less financial than political: a test of whether Ofcom applies its rules evenly to a broadcaster the loudest voices in Reform have already cast as an adversary.

Ofcom has given no timeline for a decision. The next marker will be whichever side leaks first — Reform has shown no reluctance to publicise the complaint, and Sky, having already laid out its defence in public, is unlikely to go quiet either.
