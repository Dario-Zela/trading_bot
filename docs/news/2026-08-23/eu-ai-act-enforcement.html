EU AI Act transparency rules now being enforced

The Berlaymont building in Brussels, headquarters of the European Commission, which houses the EU's AI Office. — Sarra Benyaich / Euro Pictures, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
EU regulators began enforcing AI Act transparency rules on 2 August, requiring chatbots and deepfakes to disclose themselves or face fines up to €15m.

Since 2 August, any company running a chatbot, an AI agent or an image generator serving people in the European Union has been legally required to say so — clearly, upfront, not buried in a settings menu. That is the short version of what changed when the EU's AI Office and national market watchdogs began enforcing Article 50 of the bloc's AI Act, the provision governing transparency. Three weeks in, the shift from written rule to active oversight is starting to bite.

The rules themselves are not complicated. Chatbots and AI agents must tell users, at first contact, that they're talking to a machine. Deepfakes — audio, video or images altered or generated to depict real people, places or events — need a label, regardless of whether anyone intended to deceive. AI-written text published to inform the public on matters of public interest has to disclose its artificial origin. Emotion-recognition and biometric-categorisation systems must tell the people they're scanning what's happening to them. None of this bans a technology; it just insists the technology introduce itself.

From guidance to enforcement

What makes this month different is not the text of the rule — the Commission published detailed guidelines on Article 50 back in June — but who now has the power to check compliance and punish its absence. From 2 August, the AI Office in Brussels and national market surveillance authorities in each of the 27 member states can request technical documentation, evaluate systems and demand fixes. Companies that don't comply face fines of up to €15 million or 3% of global annual turnover, whichever is higher — a formula that scales the penalty to a company's size rather than capping it at a flat sum that a trillion-dollar firm could shrug off.

That 3%-of-turnover mechanism matters more than the headline figure. For a mid-sized SaaS company, €15 million is real money. For Alphabet or Meta, it's the turnover percentage that stings — and it moves the calculus from "pay the fine and move on" toward genuine compliance spend: legal review, product changes, engineering time spent bolting disclosure notices and machine-readable watermarks onto systems that were never built with them in mind.

An important caveat sits alongside the rollout, and Brussels has been careful not to bury it. The AI Act's toughest obligations — the "high-risk" requirements covering AI used in hiring, credit scoring, education, migration and border control — were also due to start on 2 August. In May, EU lawmakers pushed those back to December 2027 as part of a broader deregulatory package known as the Digital Omnibus. Digital rights groups have criticised the delay; Stefi Richani of the Equinox Initiative argued that "no amount of safeguarding or guidelines can circumvent structural biases against migrants" facing automated decisions in the meantime. The transparency rules that did arrive on schedule are, in other words, the easier half of the AI Act — labelling is cheaper and less contentious than auditing a hiring algorithm for bias.

Who has to comply, and how

The obligation falls on both the companies that build AI models and the businesses that deploy them — a distinction the Act draws precisely and that has tripped up plenty of firms building on top of someone else's API. A startup calling OpenAI's or Anthropic's models to power its own customer-service bot cannot simply assume the underlying model provider has handled disclosure; as a "deployer," it carries its own non-delegable duty to tell its own users they're talking to AI.　

The major labs have responded in visibly different ways. OpenAI published a framework in the run-up to the deadline laying out how its safety and transparency practices map onto the Act's requirements. More than 180 organisations — including Google, Microsoft, Anthropic, Amazon, IBM, Mistral AI and, eventually, Meta — signed the Commission's voluntary Code of Practice on Transparency of AI-Generated Content, which offers companies a measure of regulatory goodwill in exchange for adopting a common set of labelling standards, including the EU's own icon set for marking AI-generated images and text. Meta's signature is notable mainly because the company refused, a year earlier, to sign a related code covering general-purpose AI model obligations, calling it legally uncertain and broader than the Act itself required. This time it signed — evidence, perhaps, that the calculus changes once an obligation moves from voluntary guidance to something a regulator can actually fine you over.

What it costs, and what's next

The practical cost for large AI companies is less about the fines — few expect an eight- or nine-figure penalty on day one — and more about the plumbing: audit trails for every chatbot interaction, machine-readable metadata embedded in generated images and video, disclosure banners translated into 24 official languages, and legal teams parsing which of their products count as "deployers" under EU definitions. For a company the size of Google or Meta, that's a manageable, if unwelcome, engineering sprint. For smaller AI startups serving EU users, it's a compliance bill that lands well before most of them are profitable.

The next test is whether Brussels follows through with actual enforcement actions rather than compliance letters. National regulators have a backlog of complaints to work through, and the AI Office's first public findings — expected in the coming months — will show whether the €15 million ceiling gets used or stays theoretical. Watch also for December 2027, when the delayed high-risk obligations are due to land; if the Digital Omnibus package sets a precedent for postponement, that date is itself not guaranteed.
