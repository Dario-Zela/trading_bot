US Treasury yields climb toward multi-decade highs despite buyback relief

The US Treasury Department building in Washington, D.C., where Secretary Scott Bessent has moved to expand debt buybacks as long-term yields hit multi-decade highs. — 颐园居 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
A $42bn Treasury auction cleared at 4.683% — the highest since 2007 — and Bessent's doubled buybacks only briefly slowed the climb toward 5.3% on the 30-year.

The bond market's most basic transaction — the government asks for money, investors decide the price — has been sending an uncomfortable signal all month. On August 12, the Treasury sold $42 billion of 10-year notes at a yield of 4.683%, the highest rate paid at auction since 2007. Demand held up: the bid-to-cover ratio came in at 2.53, and indirect bidders, a proxy for foreign central banks and overseas funds, took 76.73% of the offering. Nobody stayed home. They just charged more to show up.

That combination — record borrowing costs, absorbed without a hitch — is the puzzle at the center of this month's bond selloff. Yields this high should, in theory, be a symptom of market stress: a buyers' strike, a credit scare, a run for the exits. Instead the 10-year and 30-year are climbing in an orderly fashion, auction after auction, even as the government they're financing crossed $40 trillion in total debt on August 18. Whether that orderliness reflects confidence in US growth or investors quietly pricing in a fiscal problem they don't yet want to punish outright is the question hanging over every trading desk this week.

A rate last seen before the crash

Treasury yields move for reasons that are rarely singular, and this climb has at least three feeding it at once. The Congressional Budget Office now projects a federal deficit of roughly $2.1 trillion for the current fiscal year, with annual shortfalls averaging $2.4 trillion through 2036 — a trajectory that would push debt held by the public above 120% of GDP, eclipsing the record set just after the Second World War. Net interest costs are on track to exceed $1 trillion in 2026, overtaking Social Security as a line item, which means an increasing share of every new bond sale goes toward servicing the last one.

Layered on top of that is a demand-side shock that has nothing to do with Washington's spending habits: corporate America's AI buildout. Big technology companies are turning to bond markets to fund data centers and chip capacity at a pace expected to roughly double issuance from 2025, competing directly with the Treasury for the same pool of long-duration capital. When Microsoft, Meta or Oracle come to market with multibillion-dollar offerings, that is money not chasing the next government auction — economists have started calling it a slow-motion crowding-out of sovereign debt by private capex.

The result, by mid-August, was a 30-year yield touching 5.34% — its highest since 2007 — and a 10-year that breached 4.75% before settling back. A survey of 392 market participants found two-thirds now expect the 10-year to clear 5% before year-end, territory it has scarcely visited since the eve of the financial crisis.

Bessent's buyback, and its short half-life

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent's response was to reach for a tool built for exactly this moment. On August 19, the department announced it would at least double the size of its long-end liquidity-support buybacks — from $2 billion to a minimum of $4 billion per operation — for purchases of 10- to 30-year nominal securities running from September 9 through November 4. The logic of a buyback is straightforward: the Treasury steps in as a buyer of its own longer-dated debt, thinning the supply investors have to absorb and, in theory, pulling yields down without the Federal Reserve lifting a finger.

It worked, briefly. The 30-year yield fell nearly 10 basis points toward 5.19% on the announcement, and equities ticked higher. Bessent followed up the next day with a more pointed message, telling reporters the operation "could be more than the $4 billion per issue," and adding: "Part of it is signalling here, and to show that we believe that the yields don't reflect the underlying fundamentals."

"We believe that the yields don't reflect the underlying fundamentals." — Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent

The market's answer came within 48 hours. By August 21, the 30-year had climbed back to roughly 5.25–5.33%, erasing most of the relief, and the 10-year was again testing 4.7%. One Bloomberg headline captured the mood bluntly: the buyback was, at best, a circuit breaker for a global bond slump, not a fix for what's driving it. A signalling operation can move a market for a session or two. It cannot, on its own, shrink a $2 trillion-plus annual deficit or slow an AI investment cycle that is pulling capital toward corporate balance sheets instead of Treasury coupons.

Confidence or complacency

That leaves two readings of the same set of facts, and the bond market has not yet chosen between them. The first is essentially benign: strong, orderly demand at ever-higher yields signals that investors still trust US growth and institutional credibility enough to lend at these rates, confident that nominal GDP expansion — AI investment included — will eventually outrun the interest bill. Foreign buyers taking three-quarters of a 10-year auction is not the behavior of a market losing faith in the issuer.

The second reading is less comfortable: that yields keep clearing smoothly precisely because nobody expects a disorderly repricing to happen on any single day, even as the slow accumulation of $2 trillion-plus deficits, $1 trillion interest bills and AI-driven private issuance quietly resets what "normal" borrowing costs look like. On this view, 4.68% and 5.3% aren't temporary peaks to be bought on dips — they're a preview of a structurally higher-rate decade, and the buyback's fade within a week is the tell.

Either way, the repricing doesn't stay contained to government bonds. A 10-year anchored near 4.7–4.75% resets the discount rate under every equity valuation model, widens the hurdle for corporate borrowing, and raises the cost of the carry trades that have quietly financed a chunk of this year's risk-asset rally. The next test arrives at the September refunding announcement, when the Treasury sets its borrowing calendar for the fourth quarter, and again when the enlarged buybacks actually begin on September 9 — the first real chance to see whether size, rather than signalling, can hold the line this time.
