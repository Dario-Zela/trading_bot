TikTok agrees $400m settlement with US Justice Department over child privacy

The Robert F. Kennedy Department of Justice Building in Washington, D.C., which announced the settlement on August 21. — APK / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
TikTok and ByteDance will pay $400m to settle a DOJ lawsuit over children's data collection — fines, not restructuring, closing out the case.

TikTok and its Chinese parent company ByteDance have agreed to pay $400 million to resolve a Justice Department lawsuit accusing the platform of harvesting children's data without parental consent — one of the largest recoveries the government has ever secured under the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act.

The deal, announced August 21, closes a case the DOJ filed in 2024 alleging TikTok let children under 13 open ordinary accounts, collected their personal information without parental sign-off, and ignored parents who asked to have their kids' data deleted. Under COPPA, any online service aimed at children must get verifiable parental consent before collecting personal data from under-13s — a rule TikTok has now twice been found to have broken. Its predecessor app, Musical.ly, paid the FTC $5.7 million over similar allegations back in 2019.

"This settlement is a major victory for American children and parents," said Associate Attorney General Stanley E. Woodward Jr. "The Department's priority is ensuring that children are protected online and that companies entrusted with their personal information meet their legal obligations."

The money and the mechanics

The $400 million splits into two payments: $300 million due immediately, and a further $100 million contingent on a court vacating the older 2019 consent decree against Musical.ly. There's no admission of wrongdoing baked into the settlement, and — notably — no operational overhaul attached to it. No monitor, no mandated redesign of the app's age-verification systems, no fresh restrictions on how TikTok handles teen accounts beyond what it says it has already built. The penalty is the entire remedy.

That's a meaningful signal in itself. Since the DOJ filed its complaint in 2024, TikTok has told regulators it has tightened age-related controls and parental oversight tools. Whether those changes were sufficient to satisfy the government, or whether the government simply decided a nine-figure fine was punishment enough, the settlement reads as Washington choosing a financial penalty over a structural mandate — a pattern regulators have leaned on before with tech platforms that are too embedded, or too complicated, to force into a compliance straitjacket.

A separate fight, already settled

It's worth separating this case from the other TikTok drama that dominated headlines for the past two years: the fight over Chinese ownership itself. That one is, for now, resolved. A joint venture — TikTok USDS, backed by Oracle, Silver Lake and Abu Dhabi's MGX, each holding 15% — closed in January, leaving ByteDance with a 19.9% stake, just under the threshold that would trigger the divest-or-ban law Congress passed in 2024. Larry Ellison's Oracle now handles security; a majority-American board runs the venture.

That deal drew its own criticism — Senator Bernie Sanders flagged the optics of Oracle's involvement, and critics have questioned whether TikTok's algorithm, reportedly "leased" back from ByteDance rather than transferred outright, really severs the operational tie to Beijing that the law demanded. But those are separate questions from the one this settlement answers. COPPA is about how TikTok treats children's data, full stop — it doesn't touch who owns the company or where its recommendation engine is trained.

What it signals

Taken together, the two resolutions point the same direction: after years in which TikTok's survival in the US looked genuinely uncertain, the platform is now operating inside a settled framework rather than an existential one. The ownership question has a structure, even if an imperfect one. The child-safety question has a price tag. Neither leaves TikTok's business model — a recommendation algorithm built to maximise engagement among a userbase that skews young — untouched by future scrutiny, but neither treats that model as disqualifying either.

What's still open is enforcement of the age and consent commitments TikTok says it's made. COPPA settlements typically come with no ongoing audit regime beyond the FTC's general authority to reopen a case if violations recur, and this one appears to follow that pattern. The next test isn't a court date — it's whether complaints about teen accounts and data handling start turning up again once the December glow of the ownership deal fades from view.
