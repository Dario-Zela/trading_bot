Arkansas 'Whittlin' Fiddler' Violet Hensley dies at 109

Violet Hensley, the 'Whittlin' Fiddler,' who hand-carved 69 instruments over seven decades. — Wikipedia
In one sentence
Violet Hensley, the Arkansas hand-carver of fiddles who made her Grand Ole Opry debut at 99, has died at 109.

Violet Brumley Hensley, who spent seven decades turning blocks of Ozark wood into fiddles and became, in her final years, one of the last living carriers of a nearly extinct American folk tradition, died August 7 in Yellville, Arkansas. She was 109.

Her death was confirmed by Silver Dollar City, the Missouri theme park where she carved instruments and played for visitors for more than fifty years, and where she remained a fixture — the plaza set up with a hatchet, hand planes and a half-finished fiddle — well into her tenth decade of life.

A craft learned by watching

Hensley was born October 21, 1916, near Mount Ida, in the Ouachita National Forest, to George Washington Brumley, a musician and fiddle maker, and Nora Springer. She learned the trade the way most Ozark crafts were once passed down — not through instruction so much as proximity, watching her father split wood by the fireplace, plane it by hand, and shape it with homemade curved knives. At 15 she told him she wanted to build one of her own. She finished it, then three more before she turned 18.

Marriage to Adren Hensley in 1935 and nine children put the fiddle down for 27 years. She picked it back up in 1961, and by the time she stopped counting she had built 69 numbered instruments, each one cut, dried and carved with the same tools she'd learned on as a teenager — no power lathe, no template beyond memory.

Fame arrived late

Hensley's public career began almost by accident in 1967, when she was hired at Silver Dollar City, the Branson-area theme park built around Ozark heritage crafts. She was in her fifties. What followed was a slow, steady accumulation of recognition rather than a single breakthrough: National Geographic profiled her in 1970; she cut an album of old-time fiddle tunes in 1974 and two more over the following decades; Charles Kuralt featured her on his "On the Road" series, and she turned up on The Beverly Hillbillies, Captain Kangaroo and To Tell the Truth, striking up a friendship along the way with Irene Ryan, who played Granny Clampett.

The honor that framed how most people came to know her arrived only in 2016, when Hensley made her debut on the Grand Ole Opry stage at age 99 — a first performance, at an age when most performers' careers are long closed, that turned her into a minor viral sensation and introduced her to an audience well beyond the Ozarks. She was inducted into the National Fiddler Hall of Fame two years later, at 101, and had already been named an Arkansas Living Treasure by the state's Department of Arkansas Heritage in 2004.

By the time of her death she had appeared at Silver Dollar City's National Crafts Festival 41 years running and was survived by 32 grandchildren, 85 great-grandchildren, 29 great-great-grandchildren and two great-great-great-grandchildren — a family tree nearly as sprawling as her instrument count.

Silver Dollar City, in a statement, remembered her as embodying "genuine kindness, quiet humility and an unwavering love for others," calling her "the heart of an Ozarks tradition." It is a tradition with few practitioners left who learned it the way she did: by hand, by ear, from a parent who learned it the same way before power tools and mail-order kits made hand-carved fiddles a novelty rather than a necessity. What survives of that knowledge now lives mostly in the 69 instruments she numbered and signed, and in whatever her children and students managed to absorb before the workshop went quiet.
