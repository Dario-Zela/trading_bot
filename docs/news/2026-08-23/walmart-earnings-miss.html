Walmart earnings miss signals US consumer slowdown

A Walmart storefront in the United States. The retailer's US comparable sales growth slowed to its weakest pace in more than six years. — Walmart Corporate / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Walmart's slowest US sales growth in six years triggered a 9% stock drop, its worst day since 2022, and renewed fears of a broader consumer slowdown.

Walmart shares fell roughly 9% on August 20, wiping out more than $80 billion in market value in a single session — the retailer's worst one-day decline since 2022. The trigger wasn't a profit miss. Walmart beat earnings estimates and raised its full-year guidance. What spooked investors was slower: the pace at which American shoppers are spending money at the country's largest retailer, which fell to its weakest level in more than six years.

For a company whose stock had shrugged off tariff scares, inflation spikes and a pandemic, that combination — beat the quarter, miss on the trend — is the kind of signal markets take seriously. Walmart sells to roughly 90% of US households across every income bracket. When its comparable sales growth slows this sharply, the read isn't "Walmart problem." It's "consumer problem."

The numbers behind the rout

Walmart's US comparable sales, excluding fuel, rose 2.6% in the fiscal second quarter ended in July — down from 4.1% the previous quarter and short of the roughly 3.7% Wall Street had penciled in. It was the first time in recent memory the company missed comparable-sales expectations outright, according to Bloomberg and Axios reporting on the release.

The rest of the report looked, on paper, fairly strong. Adjusted earnings per share of $0.81 beat the $0.74 consensus. Revenue climbed 6% year-over-year to $187.94 billion, and operating income rose 30%. But a chunk of that profit strength came from a one-time $2.9 billion tariff refund, not underlying momentum, and roughly a fifth of a percentage point of the sales shortfall traced to price caps on certain drugs that hit Walmart's health and wellness unit. Strip those out and the core trend — a large-format, everyday-goods retailer selling less growth than it has in six years — is what markets chose to price.

Third-quarter guidance added to the unease. Walmart's outlook came in below what analysts had modeled, even as full-year guidance moved up. Some of Wall Street's largest desks pushed back on the scale of the sell-off — Bank of America and JPMorgan both reiterated buy ratings — but that didn't stop the stock from posting its steepest single-day drop since mid-2022.

An uneven consumer, not a broken one

Walmart's own executives framed the slowdown carefully rather than sounding an alarm. Chief financial officer John David Rainey told analysts the environment was "arguably a softer consumer environment than in February," before gasoline prices climbed again this summer.

"When fuel prices increase and get above $4, perhaps there's a psychological impact to that … consumers are making trade-offs," Rainey said on the earnings call.

The pressure, he said, is concentrated rather than universal. "We see with our customers that the high-income customer is spending with confidence into many categories, while the lower-income consumer is more budget conscious and perhaps navigating financial distress," Rainey said — a split that Walmart has increasingly leaned on to explain results, given that it has been gaining market share among higher-income households even as its traditional lower-income base pulls back. Speaking to CNBC separately, Rainey insisted the underlying business was sound: "Our business is strong. We feel really good about the progress we're making."

Walmart's response has been to spend some of that tariff refund on price cuts across thousands of items, betting that value-seeking behavior — rather than an outright pullback — is what's driving the softness, and that undercutting on price will hold share even if household budgets stay tight into the holidays.

Why the market treated this as bigger than one retailer

Walmart's report landed alongside broader evidence pointing the same direction. US retail sales fell unexpectedly in July, the first monthly decline in nine months. Goldman Sachs economists have warned that real consumer spending growth could slow to as little as 1% in the second half of 2026 as elevated fuel and food costs eat into discretionary budgets. Against that backdrop, a six-year-low sales quarter from the retailer best positioned to capture trade-down shopping — the destination consumers flock to precisely when money is tight — reads less like company-specific friction and more like confirmation.

That matters for how investors are positioning heading into the back half of the year. Consumer spending accounts for roughly two-thirds of US GDP, and Walmart's results arrive as the Federal Reserve weighs its next move against a labor market showing its own cracks and inflation that has proven sticky. A weakening consumer complicates that calculus in both directions — it argues for rate cuts to support growth, even as tariff-driven price pressure argues for caution.

What's next

The next test comes from Walmart's peers: Target and other big-box retailers report in the coming weeks, and their comparable-sales trends will show whether the slowdown is broad-based or specific to Walmart's low-income-heavy customer mix. August retail sales data, due from the Commerce Department in mid-September, will be the first hard read on whether July's dip was a blip or the start of a trend. And Walmart's own third-quarter results, when they land in November, will show whether the price cuts funded by its tariff refund are enough to keep budget-conscious shoppers walking through the door heading into the holiday season.
