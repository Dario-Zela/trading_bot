BEYOND THE TAPE · DEVOLUTION · By L. Bordeaux · 2026-08-23 · 3 min read

Andy Burnham to give England's mayors new powers over local planning decisions

Andy Burnham, whose government is handing mayors the planning and fiscal powers he long sought as Greater Manchester's own mayor. — UK House of Commons / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Mayors gain planning override powers and a future cut of business rates and income tax, but the government has yet to specify which sites, which areas first, or when overrules actually kick in.

There is a particular kind of irony in Downing Street this month, and it wears a Manchester accent. For nine years, Andy Burnham stood on the other side of the table from Whitehall, needling successive governments for hoarding powers that, in his telling, belonged closer to the people they affected. Now he is the government. And the devolution package his administration unveiled in late July — with the technical detail still being filled in through August — hands mayors the kind of authority he spent almost a decade asking for himself.

The headline power is straightforward to describe and hard to pin down in practice: mayors of strategic authorities will be able to overrule local councils on planning decisions, gaining something close to the "call-in" power Sadiq Khan has held in London since 2008, which lets the mayor step in on applications a borough has rejected or is dragging its feet on. Housing minister Matthew Pennycook has called it "an essential part of the toolkit," and the government's own housing secretary, Angela Rayner, has framed the whole package as ending local government's "begging-bowl culture."

What's actually been specified

Strip away the rhetoric and a few real dates emerge. Mayors are due to start retaining a share of business rates generated in their areas from spring 2027, with a share of income tax revenue following from 2028 — the first time English regions outside London have been offered a cut of national tax receipts tied to local economic performance. Ben Houchen, the Tees Valley mayor, has already floated using his eventual share to fund a local tax rebate, which tells you plenty about how mayors intend to spend political capital on this once the money starts flowing.

What's missing is everything a property investor would actually need. The government insists councils will still decide "the majority" of applications, which is a way of saying the overrule power is meant for the exceptions — stalled sites, contested regeneration schemes, the sort of stuck development that makes headlines in a housing crisis. But which sites, which mayors get the power first, and what triggers a call-in versus a routine refusal all remain open questions. A fuller roadmap is promised for the autumn Budget white paper.

The critics and the gap

The scheme applies to roughly a dozen mayoral authorities — Greater Manchester among them, now led by Bev Craig after Burnham's own move to Westminster forced a by-election in his old patch — plus places like Liverpool, the West Midlands and the West of England. Areas without a mayor get nothing, which is precisely the complaint from the Liberal Democrats and some Labour backbenchers: a two-tier England where investment follows the map of devolution deals rather than need.

History offers a rough guide to how markets might eventually respond. When an earlier round of planning reform eased brownfield approvals in England's biggest cities, housebuilders rallied on the news — Persimmon, Taylor Wimpey and Barratt all moved higher within days. But that reform came with a specific, nationwide rule investors could price. This one is a promise of local variation, phased in over two Budget cycles, administered by mayors whose individual appetite for overruling their own council colleagues is untested. Watch the autumn white paper for the mechanism — until then, this is a structural shift still waiting for its fine print.
