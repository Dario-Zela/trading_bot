Fed's Jackson Hole message strains as jobs crack and inflation stays sticky

The Federal Reserve's Eccles Building in Washington, D.C., seat of a central bank now split over which way to move. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
July payrolls fell 23,000 and prior months were revised down 103,000, even as inflation holds near 3.4% — leaving new Fed Chair Warsh's first Jackson Hole speech unable to point a clear way forward.

The Federal Reserve is supposed to have a playbook for moments like this. Weak jobs numbers call for lower rates; hot inflation calls for higher ones. Central bankers spend careers training for exactly one of those two problems at a time. What they are not built for is both at once — which is precisely the position Kevin Warsh occupies as he prepares to deliver his first Jackson Hole address as Fed chair on Friday.

The numbers behind that bind are not subtle. July nonfarm payrolls fell by 23,000, against consensus forecasts of an 80,000-plus gain — a swing of more than 100,000 jobs from what economists expected. The unemployment rate ticked down to 4.1%, but only because the labor force participation rate slid to 61.4%, its lowest in more than five years, meaning fewer people are working partly because fewer are looking. Underneath the headline, the Bureau of Labor Statistics revised May's job gain down by 66,000 and June's by 37,000 — a combined 103,000 jobs that turned out never to have existed. Job losses concentrated in local government education and retail trade, sectors that don't usually move together, which is its own small warning sign about breadth.

Inflation, meanwhile, has refused to cooperate with the idea that the Fed can simply ride to the rescue. Headline CPI is running at 3.4% year-over-year — nowhere near the Fed's 2% target, and the number most likely to anchor Warsh's remarks. Core CPI told a gentler story in July, cooling to 2.5%, its slowest pace since March 2021. But a chair who leans on that softer core figure risks looking like he is cherry-picking the number that lets him do what markets want, at a moment when his own credibility is already under strain.

A five-meeting hold, and a rare revolt

That strain has a specific origin point: the Federal Open Market Committee's July 29 meeting, where the Fed held its benchmark rate at 3.5%–3.75% for a fifth consecutive meeting — but not without incident. Three regional bank presidents, Cleveland's Beth Hammack, Minneapolis's Neel Kashkari and Dallas's Lorie Logan, dissented in favor of a hike, arguing the current stance still isn't restrictive enough against inflation that won't come down. It was the first time since September 2016 that three FOMC members dissented in the same direction, and by some counts the most contested vote against a sitting chair since 1970.

Warsh, characteristically unbothered, embraced it. "I asked for a good family fight, and I got one," he told reporters — a line that plays well as a rhetorical flourish about institutional pluralism, and rather less well as a description of a central bank that can't agree on which way to move. Markets did not take it well either: the Dow fell 1,153 points that day and the 30-year Treasury yield touched its highest level since 2007, as investors read the split as evidence the Fed was, if anything, behind the curve on inflation rather than ahead of a slowdown.

Warsh's debut, under maximum scrutiny

Warsh took the oath as Fed chair on May 22, following one of the more divisive confirmations in the institution's history — a 54-45 Senate vote, the closest margin for a Fed chair in the modern era. He arrived with a public record as a rate-policy hawk turned occasional dove, and a president who nominated him expecting exactly the opposite of what July's data now argues for: Donald Trump has pushed openly for lower rates, and campaigned against Warsh's predecessor, Jerome Powell, for withholding them.

Friday's speech, delivered at 10 a.m. Eastern at the Kansas City Fed's Jackson Hole Economic Symposium — this year themed around financial innovation in payments and policy — is being watched less for content than for tone. Warsh has spent his three months in office giving unusually short statements and evasive press conferences, and has explicitly said he does not want to be "constrained by market prices" when setting policy — an implicit rebuke of the market's habit of front-running Fed guidance. He has signaled the speech will step back from "near-sighted debates" toward "big questions," language that reads as an attempt to avoid committing to anything specific before the September 16 meeting.

That restraint is itself a market event. Futures pricing has moved to roughly 70% odds of a hold at the September meeting, with hike odds around 28.5% — down sharply from where they sat immediately after July's dissent-marked decision — while a rate cut is barely priced at all. Gold has rallied toward $4,380 an ounce on the retreat in hike expectations, since a lower probability of tightening compresses real yields and makes non-yielding assets like bullion more attractive. That rally, and the currency and rate moves sitting behind it, will reverse hard in whichever direction Warsh leans on Friday, which is exactly why traders describe this speech as the single richest scheduled signal before the September decision.

The trap, and what it means for guidance

What makes this moment different from the Fed's usual two-sided balancing act is that the two data points aren't offsetting each other gently — they're actively pulling in opposite directions with unusual force. A shortfall of 106,000 jobs against expectations, compounded by a 103,000-job downward revision to the two prior months, is not a soft patch; it is the kind of deterioration that would ordinarily trigger a rate-cut discussion within weeks. Headline inflation running 140 basis points above target is not a rounding error either. Historically the Fed has been able to point to one clean signal and communicate around it. Here, any sentence in Warsh's speech that leans toward the jobs numbers risks reading as tolerance for above-target inflation; any sentence that leans toward inflation risks reading as indifference to a labor market that is now shedding jobs.

The practical consequence is that forward guidance — the tool by which the Fed has, since the 2010s, tried to manage market expectations between meetings — becomes far less reliable exactly when investors need it most. Warsh's preference for ambiguity, dressed up as independence from market pricing, may be less a strategic choice than the only position available to a chair who cannot honestly promise either more hikes or a cut without contradicting one half of the data. The July dissent already showed that the committee itself can't settle the question internally; Jackson Hole was supposed to be where the chair settled it publicly. Instead, the more likely outcome on Friday is a speech engineered to commit to nothing, leaving the September 16 meeting — and the August payrolls and CPI reports due before it — as the actual venue where this gets decided.
