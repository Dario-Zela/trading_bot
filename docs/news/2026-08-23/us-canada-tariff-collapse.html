US-Canada trade talks collapse as Trump imposes 50% tariffs, Carney vows retaliation

The Ambassador Bridge linking Detroit and Windsor, one of the busiest trade corridors between the US and Canada. — Ken Lund / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Talks collapsed, US 50% tariffs on ~$20bn of Canadian goods took effect, and Carney pledged dollar-for-dollar retaliation starting Sept. 8.

Trade negotiations between Washington and Ottawa collapsed late Friday, just hours before a midnight deadline, triggering 50% US tariffs on roughly $20 billion of Canadian goods and prompting Prime Minister Mark Carney to suspend talks and promise a matching response. It is the sharpest break yet in a trade relationship that has spent the better part of two years lurching between deals and threats — and this time neither side is pretending a fix is imminent.

How the talks fell apart

The two governments' negotiators had reportedly come close to an agreement earlier in the week. Then, according to Canadian officials, Washington introduced last-minute changes to the terms on the table — demands Carney later called "uneconomic" and "unfair," adding that they called into question the reliability of any deal reached with the current administration. US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer offered the mirror-image account, saying Saturday that "Canada declined to finalize the trade deal under the terms agreed earlier this week."

Carney, at a news conference in Ottawa, put it more bluntly: "They asked too much and offered too little." He said Canada had been prepared to drop its remaining retaliatory tariffs on steel, aluminum and autos if Washington cut its own rates substantially — an offer he said the US side did not meet. Disagreements over access to Canada's critical minerals were also a sticking point, according to people briefed on the talks.

With no deal reached, Trump's threatened 50% tariff took effect just after midnight Saturday, covering a wide range of Canadian exports — among them hockey sticks, building materials, spirits and certain clothing categories. Notably, goods that had previously been exempt under the Canada-United States-Mexico Agreement lost that protection for many product lines, a departure from how the tariff regime had operated through most of the year.

The retaliation, and what it targets

Carney has directed Canada's negotiating team to return home and pledged that Ottawa will match the US tariffs "dollar for dollar," with the retaliatory measures set to take effect September 8 — the Tuesday after Labor Day. He named the sectors in the crosshairs: steel, dairy, appliances, agricultural machinery, pulp and paper, and electronics. Specific tariff lines and rates have not yet been published; Ottawa says those details will follow in the coming days.

The two-week gap between the US tariffs taking effect and Canada's response landing is itself a signal — Carney is leaving a window, however narrow, for Washington to reconsider before the retaliation becomes irreversible. Whether that window functions as a genuine off-ramp or simply as a staging period for Canadian industry to brace for impact is the open question hanging over the next fortnight.

Markets and the mechanics of a wider spiral

The currency and equity reaction has been muted so far but directionally clear: the Canadian dollar has come under renewed pressure through the week as the tariff deadline approached, and traders have pared back expectations for a Bank of Canada rate move, removing one lever that might otherwise cushion exporters. The S&P/TSX had rallied as much as 1% earlier this month on hopes that a deal was close — a reminder of how quickly sentiment can reverse once headlines move the other way.

The economic mechanics of a 50-plus-50 tariff exchange are straightforward and unforgiving. Roughly $20 billion in US-bound Canadian goods now face a 50% levy; Canada's promised dollar-for-dollar match implies a comparable hit to US exporters in steel, dairy, farm equipment and paper once the September 8 measures land. Firms on both sides of the border that built supply chains around decades of largely tariff-free trade — automakers, appliance makers, food processors — are the ones absorbing the cost in the near term, either through margin compression or price increases passed to buyers.

What comes next

The most consequential date on the calendar is September 8, when Canada's retaliatory tariffs are due to take effect — the point at which the conflict moves from a one-sided US tariff to a genuine trade war with losses accruing on both sides of the border. Between now and then, watch for two things: whether Washington and Ottawa reopen any channel of talks before the retaliation becomes binding, and how the Bank of Canada and the Federal Reserve each factor a widening trade conflict into their next policy meetings. Neither side has signaled a near-term willingness to blink, which is itself the more important data point than any single tariff line.
