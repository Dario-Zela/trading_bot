JPMorgan says it 'simply doesn't know' how to forecast oil amid Trump-Iran tensions

An oil pumpjack in West Texas — JPMorgan says it can no longer model where crude is headed as the US-Iran conflict drags on. — Eric Kounce (TexasRaiser) / Wikimedia Commons, public domain
In one sentence
JPMorgan has abandoned its oil price forecast, saying the Iran conflict has broken the models it uses to predict crude's path.

Oil traders have grown used to banks disagreeing about where crude is headed. What they are not used to is a bank saying it no longer has a view at all.

JPMorgan's commodities desk told clients this week that it has abandoned its working forecast for oil prices, six months into the conflict between the United States and Iran, because the assumptions it built the forecast on no longer hold. "For the first time since the start of the Iran conflict, we don't have a baseline view," Natasha Kaneva, the bank's head of global commodities strategy, wrote in a note to clients. "We simply don't know how to model the endgame."

The redlines that didn't hold

When the fighting began, JPMorgan's analysts built their forecast around a simple premise: pain would force a deal. They identified three economic thresholds — crude above $100 a barrel, US pump prices near $5 a gallon, and the 10-year Treasury yield above 5% — that they expected would pressure the Trump administration into brokering an end to the disruption and reopening the Strait of Hormuz, the channel through which roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil passes.

All three lines have now been crossed. Brent crude is trading near $106 a barrel. The national average at the pump has climbed above $4.30 a gallon, with diesel setting a record at $6.31. The 10-year Treasury yield touched 5% again on Friday. None of it produced the deal JPMorgan's model assumed. "The exit strategy is less clear, not more," Kaneva wrote.

A market pricing in the unknown

The bank still publishes a number — it puts Brent's "fair value" for September at roughly $90 a barrel, based on supply and demand fundamentals alone. But that leaves a gap of about $16 between the model and the market, which JPMorgan reads as traders pricing in the possibility of losing another 4 million barrels a day of supply on top of the roughly 10 million barrels a day already disrupted. The International Energy Agency has pencilled in a supply contraction of 5.7 million barrels a day, the steepest since the pandemic, while combined crude and product stockpiles have drawn down by an estimated 555 million barrels as demand has run about 4.4 million barrels a day below year-ago levels.

Even so, Kaneva's note stopped short of forecasting a spike: "there is still enough dry powder to keep prices contained — for now," she wrote, pointing to spare capacity elsewhere in the market. It is a hedge inside a note that is otherwise an admission of not knowing — a bank keeping one foot in forecasting while conceding the ground under it has shifted.

Why a bank giving up matters

Oil forecasting has never been a precise science, but banks have historically been willing to put a number on it, however caveated. JPMorgan's retreat is notable less for what it says about the price of oil and more for what it says about the limits of the models themselves. Standard forecasting leans on historical correlation — how markets have behaved in comparable prior episodes. The Iran conflict has produced no comparable prior episode: a major bank's own economic-pain thresholds were breached without triggering the response the model predicted, which means the relationship between price and policy that the model relied on has broken down, not just the price level.

That has consequences beyond crude itself. Trading systems and hedging strategies built on historical correlations between oil, inflation expectations and rate policy are operating in conditions those correlations were never trained on. Diesel at a record alongside crude well off its own highs, and equity markets shrugging off near-5% yields even as Bitcoin pushes past $80,000, all point to a market where idiosyncratic geopolitical risk is overriding the usual technical signals.

What's next

The next scheduled catalyst is September 24, when Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping are due to meet in Washington — a meeting with no formal agenda item on Iran but one traders will watch for any signal on sanctions enforcement or Gulf shipping. Absent a diplomatic breakthrough there or elsewhere, JPMorgan's position is unlikely to change: not a forecast of higher or lower prices, but an acknowledgment that its model has nothing left to say.
