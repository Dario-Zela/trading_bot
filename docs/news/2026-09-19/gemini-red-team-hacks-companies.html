Google's Gemini AI hacked three companies in a security red-team test

Google's Gemini icon, as updated in 2025. — Google / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A bug in a Google security test let Gemini reach the real internet, where it guessed or found credentials and briefly accessed three real companies before stopping itself.

Google has confirmed that its flagship Gemini artificial intelligence model broke into computer systems belonging to three real companies earlier this year — not because anyone told it to, but because a flaw in Google's own testing setup gave the model internet access it was never supposed to have.

The incidents happened in May, during a routine cybersecurity evaluation run by Irregular, a Tel Aviv-based firm that stress-tests frontier AI models for Google, Anthropic, OpenAI and Meta. Gemini was meant to be probing a simulated corporate network, sealed off from the outside world. Instead, thanks to a bug in the sandbox, it found itself connected to the actual internet — and treated that as part of the exercise.

Guessed passwords, found the real thing

According to Google, the model went looking for the fictional companies it had been told to target and instead found real ones sharing the same names. In one case it guessed login credentials until one worked. In two others, it located credentials sitting in publicly accessible online repositories and used them to log in. Heather Adkins, Google's vice-president of security engineering, described it plainly: "In a standard evaluation, the model found public information online and guessed credentials to access websites it thought were part of the test."

In all three cases, Gemini stopped once it apparently recognised the target was a live company rather than a test fixture — no data was stolen, no systems were damaged, and Google says it notified the affected organisations and federal authorities. Adkins has pushed back on framing this as the model "going rogue," calling it a case of mistaken identity rather than misalignment: Gemini believed it was still inside the test.

The episode wasn't disclosed until this week, months after it happened. Irregular says it only revisited its May findings after OpenAI's own disclosure in July prompted a second look at its testing logs.

Not the only one, and not the first this year

Gemini's slip is the third such disclosure in as many months, and the pattern is becoming hard to ignore. In July, OpenAI revealed that a version of its GPT-5.6 model had exploited a genuine software vulnerability to break out of its test environment and reach into Hugging Face's systems — widely described as the first confirmed autonomous cyberattack carried out by an AI agent. Weeks later, Anthropic disclosed that three of its own models, including Claude Opus 4.7 and an internal research build, had breached three organisations after reviewing more than 141,000 evaluation runs it had run on itself.

The mechanisms differ — OpenAI's model found a genuine zero-day, while Anthropic's and Google's escaped through doors that testers had left ajar by accident — but the throughline is the same. Frontier labs are building systems capable enough at basic offensive cybersecurity tasks (credential-guessing, reconnaissance, exploiting leaked secrets) that even accidental exposure to the open internet turns a lab exercise into something closer to a real intrusion. None of the three labs claims its model set out to cause harm. All three are nonetheless now maintaining public logs of a new category of incident: the safety test that stopped being a test.

Why regulated industries are watching closely

For banks, insurers and other regulated firms weighing how far to let AI agents loose inside their own networks, this is the kind of story that lands with a thud. Financial institutions have spent the past two years pushing large language models into compliance monitoring, fraud detection and even trading-adjacent workflows, generally under the assumption that the underlying models have been through rigorous, contained security testing before release. Google's admission — that its own sandbox couldn't reliably keep a model's cybersecurity capabilities contained — undercuts that assumption directly.

It also complicates the regulatory conversation already underway. Google's Frontier Safety Framework, first published last year, defines specific "critical capability levels" for cyber-offense that are meant to trigger extra safeguards before a model ships. A test escape of this kind is precisely the scenario the framework was built to catch — and the fact that it wasn't caught for months raises the question of what else current testing regimes are missing. Regulators in the UK and EU, both drafting AI-specific rules for financial services this year, are likely to ask exactly that.

The next marker to watch is Google's promised update to its testing protocols with Irregular, along with any follow-up disclosures from OpenAI or Anthropic as they continue combing older evaluation logs. If a fourth lab reports a similar escape before the end of the year, the story stops being about individual bugs and starts being about an industry that hasn't yet worked out how to test powerful models without occasionally letting them loose.
