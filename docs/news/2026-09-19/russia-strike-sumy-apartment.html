Russian guided bomb kills two in strike on Sumy apartment block

In one sentence
A Russian guided bomb hit a nine-storey Sumy apartment block, killing two city council workers and injuring eight, including children.

A Russian guided bomb tore into a nine-storey apartment block in Sumy late on Friday, killing two city council employees and injuring at least eight people, including two children, in the latest strike on a city that has spent much of the war within artillery range of the Russian border.

The bomb hit between the third and fourth floors of the building in the city's Kovpakivskyi district, according to Sumy Mayor Artem Kobzar, tearing a gap across three storeys and setting the structure alight. Regional governor Oleh Hryhorov said rescue crews pulled four people from the rubble, including a seven-year-old boy, as search operations continued into the night. Three of the injured were listed in serious condition.

The two people killed — a 52-year-old man and a 45-year-old woman — worked for the executive committee of the Sumy City Council, Kobzar said. Sumy City Military Administration head Serhii Kryvosheienko said Russian aircraft dropped six or seven guided bombs on the city during the attack, damaging at least four residential buildings in total.

A familiar target

Sumy sits roughly 30 kilometres from the Russian border, close enough that guided bombs — cheap, GPS-corrected munitions dropped from aircraft that never need to cross into Ukrainian-controlled airspace to reach the city — have become a recurring feature of life there. The region absorbed a large Russian ground incursion in 2024 and has remained a target for glide-bomb and drone strikes since, including a January 2025 drone attack that killed four people in a residential building and a separate strike this year that left sixteen injured.

President Volodymyr Zelensky called Friday's strike "absolutely inhuman" and said the toll could still rise as crews searched for anyone left under the debris. He used the attack to press the case for further sanctions on Moscow, pointing to the Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act — the bill sponsored by Senator Lindsey Graham that had passed Congress and was awaiting the US president's signature. The same evening, Russian forces also struck a medical clinic in Pavlohrad and logistics infrastructure in Kyiv Oblast, part of a broader wave of attacks Ukrainian officials described as coordinated.

No sign of a shift

What makes Friday's strike notable is precisely how unremarkable it is within the pattern of this war: a guided bomb, a residential building, casualties among people who had nothing to do with the fighting. Ukrainian officials have logged strikes on Sumy's housing stock repeatedly over the past two years, and Friday's attack adds to that tally rather than departing from it. There is no indication so far of an expanded target set or a change in Russian tactics — just the continuation of a campaign that has made the city's apartment blocks a recurring casualty count.

The immediate task in Sumy is recovery: clearing rubble, treating the injured, and establishing whether anyone remains trapped in the four damaged buildings. The broader question — whether the newly passed US sanctions bill changes Moscow's calculus at all — will start to be answered once the president signs it and its provisions take effect.
