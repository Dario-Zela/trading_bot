Ofgem price cap to rise 4% in October despite Burnham's electricity VAT cut

Andy Burnham, who cut VAT on domestic electricity days into his premiership — only to see wholesale gas prices erode the saving. — UK House of Commons / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
UK energy bills rise ~£60 a year from October as Middle East-driven gas prices swallow PM Burnham's £45 VAT cut on electricity.

Households across Great Britain will pay more for energy this winter than last, regardless of a tax cut the prime minister designed specifically to stop that happening. Ofgem confirmed on 26 August that its quarterly price cap will rise 4% from 1 October, taking the typical dual-fuel household bill, for someone paying by direct debit, from £1,663 to £1,723 a year — an increase of roughly £60, or £5 a month. Andy Burnham's decision in July to strip VAT off domestic electricity was meant to cushion exactly this kind of shock. It is worth roughly £45 a year to the average household. The math does not clear.

What Ofgem actually changed

The price cap is not a cap on bills — it limits what suppliers can charge per unit of gas and electricity plus the standing charge, so actual bills still depend on how much a household uses. Ofgem said the increase was driven almost entirely by gas: gas costs within the cap are rising 8%, while the electricity component is broadly flat, largely because the VAT removal offsets what would otherwise have been a similar increase on that side of the bill. Wholesale gas prices are up 11% over the past three months. Around 35% of homes, some 11 million, are on fixed tariffs and will not feel October's move directly, though most of those deals were priced against the same rising wholesale backdrop.

"High international gas prices are continuing to drive energy costs in the UK," said Neil Kenward, Ofgem's director general for markets, in the regulator's announcement — a sentence notable mainly for how little agency it assigns to anyone in Whitehall.

A tax cut overtaken

Burnham's electricity VAT cut was one of his first acts as prime minister, announced on his second day in the job in July and funded in part by scrapping the previous government's digital ID scheme, budgeted at roughly £600m a year. It takes the VAT rate on domestic electricity from 5% to zero from 1 October to the end of the 2026/27 financial year in March — a genuine, if temporary, transfer from the exchequer to household bills, worth about £45 a year on average.

Set against Ofgem's £60 increase, the net effect is a household bill still rising by roughly £15 a year despite the tax cut — before accounting for the fact that most of the intended relief has already been absorbed by the very price rise it was designed to offset. Martin Lewis of MoneySavingExpert had flagged the risk before Ofgem's number was even out, warning in July that a predicted cap rise of around 3.1% would eat into the VAT saving and that a further increase in January "could negate all the benefit of the VAT cut" entirely. The actual October rise came in higher than his forecast. Ahmed Tabaqchali of the Atlantic Council put the broader point more bluntly: government tax relief "doesn't fix the fundamentals."

The fundamentals are in the Gulf

Those fundamentals trace back to the Strait of Hormuz. Israeli and US strikes on Iran began on 28 February, and Iranian retaliation against regional energy infrastructure and shipping has repeatedly disrupted a waterway that normally carries around a fifth of the world's oil and LNG. The International Energy Agency has estimated Gulf oil production cut by at least 10 million barrels a day at points during the conflict, roughly a tenth of global output, while LNG exports from Qatar and the UAE — significant suppliers to European markets — have been severely curtailed. UK wholesale gas briefly spiked to 151p a therm, a level last seen in February 2023, having risen 93% in a single week during one acute phase of the crisis, before easing back toward 148p.

Britain imports a large share of its gas, which leaves it exposed to exactly this kind of geopolitical repricing no matter what happens to VAT. "The consumer is going to be paying a 'risk premium' for energy supply," said Jack Burt, a Cambridge researcher studying energy storage. "As long as the United Kingdom relies on energy imports, we are going to be impacted by geopolitics." The Centre for Economics and Business Research has gone further, forecasting that UK households' real spending power will fall by £2,400 by the end of 2027 if current trends hold — a figure that dwarfs both October's £60 increase and Burnham's £45 offset.

What comes next

The next data point lands by 25 November, when Ofgem confirms the cap for January to March 2027. Cornwall Insight currently forecasts a further 9% rise, to £1,872 a year — £149 above the level set this week — driven again by gas, which makes up just over half of a typical dual-fuel bill. Bloomberg Economics has floated an even sharper scenario, a roughly 25% jump in January if Middle East tensions escalate further, which would swamp the VAT cut entirely and hand Burnham's government its first serious cost-of-living setback since taking office. Whether that scenario materializes depends less on anything decided in London than on how the Strait of Hormuz situation resolves over the next two months.
