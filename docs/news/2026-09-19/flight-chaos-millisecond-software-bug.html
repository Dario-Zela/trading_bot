Official report pins mass flight chaos on 'millisecond' software defect

NATS's Swanwick control centre, where the flight data system failure originated on 8 September — Peter Facey / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
A software glitch lasting roughly a millisecond corrupted UK flight data and cancelled over 2,000 flights, hitting hundreds of thousands of passengers.

It took less than the blink of an eye — literally a thousandth of a second — to ground a meaningful share of British aviation for the better part of a day. On 8 September, a defect buried in a small subsection of code at National Air Traffic Services corrupted flight data at the moment a controller manually requested an identification code for an aircraft. The system never recovered cleanly. Within hours, more than 2,000 flights in and out of the UK were cancelled, disruption rippled on for days, and hundreds of thousands of passengers were left stranded, rebooked or simply stuck.

NATS published its preliminary report on the incident this week, and the finding is uncomfortable precisely because of how small the trigger was. This wasn't a cyberattack, a staffing failure, or a piece of ageing hardware giving out. It was a sequencing error: a higher-priority message interrupted a routine code-allocation request, and when the system resumed processing, it didn't pick back up where it should have. The output that followed was corrupted, and that corruption propagated into the flight data feeding London's higher-altitude airspace.

In one sentence: A software glitch lasting roughly a millisecond corrupted UK flight data and triggered a cascade that cancelled over 2,000 flights and disrupted hundreds of thousands of passengers.

A fault too fast for anyone to catch

NATS chief executive Martin Rolfe described the cause plainly: "This was a software issue in a specific part of our flight data system, that we have traced to a small subsection of coding." The defect sat in the National Airspace System, the software that assigns individual transponder codes so controllers can distinguish aircraft on radar. Because the interruption and failed resumption happened in the space of a millisecond, there was no human window in which to intervene — the corrupted data was already out before anyone could notice something was wrong.

Once the fault was flagged, at 13:46 that Tuesday, NATS had little choice but to impose airspace restrictions to protect safety while the system was restarted and flight data reloaded — a process that took roughly six hours. Automated processing normally handles around 800 flight plans an hour; done manually, that drops to roughly 60. That ratio is the real reason a millisecond of corrupted code turned into days of disruption rather than hours: once controllers had to fall back to manual workarounds, the bottleneck wasn't radar or radios, it was how fast people could type.

The bill, in cancelled flights and stranded crews

Ryanair alone cancelled 260 flights, affecting roughly 48,000 passengers and costing the airline at least £3 million. British Airways scrapped close to 200. Heathrow, Gatwick, Manchester and Birmingham bore the brunt, and the backlog was still clearing through 15 airports a day later, as aircraft and crews sat out of position and pilots ran up against duty-hour limits. Michael O'Leary, Ryanair's chief executive, did not mince words, calling it "an absolute re-run" of NATS's August 2023 meltdown and publicly demanding Rolfe's removal: "It is time for this expensive, failing CEO to go, and allow UK NATS to be led by somebody competent and capable of delivering a resilient system and a backup system that actually works."

NATS disputes that framing. Officials say the 2023 failure originated in a different subsystem — the Flight Plan Reception Suite, which choked on an unusual flight plan with duplicate waypoint names — while September's fault sat in the core flight data system itself. Technically, that may be true. Practically, it is a distinction few stranded passengers are likely to find reassuring: twice in three years, a single, narrow software fault has taken a meaningful slice of UK airspace capacity offline for a day.

Redundancy that didn't redound

That repetition is what has turned a technical bug into a political problem. Transport minister Keir Mather, pressed in Parliament on why NATS lacked an effective backup for exactly this kind of failure, conceded the obvious: "the hon. Member is right to point to the fact that resilience is clearly not where it needs to be." He said systems needed to become "more adaptive and more reactive," without offering a timeline for how that happens.

The Civil Aviation Authority has opened an independent review into the outage and into NATS's broader resilience, due to report within six months, and Rolfe was required to hand ministers a technical account of the incident by 16 September. NATS, for its part, says a permanent fix is undergoing safety testing before deployment, and is preparing to ask the CAA to approve roughly £1 billion in systems investment through 2033 — money aimed less at any one bug than at the deeper problem the report exposes: critical national infrastructure that still depends on individual, human-scale patches of code holding up under conditions no one fully anticipated.

The next marker is the CAA's resilience findings, expected within six months, and whether NATS's investment case survives contact with a regulator — and an airline industry — that has now watched the same failure mode repeat twice in three years.
