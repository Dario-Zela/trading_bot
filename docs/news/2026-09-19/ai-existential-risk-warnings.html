King Charles and Microsoft warn of AI's 'existential' risks

King Charles III, who told AI industry leaders gathered at Dumfries House there was 'urgency' in addressing AI's existential dangers. — White House / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
King Charles and Microsoft's AI chief both warned of AI's existential dangers this week, but neither offered a policy fix.

King Charles spent Thursday hosting the people who build the technology he is worried about. At Dumfries House, his estate in Ayrshire, around 30 executives, officials and ethicists gathered for a closed-door summit on artificial intelligence — among them Nvidia's Jensen Huang, Google DeepMind's Demis Hassabis, OpenAI's Sarah Friar, Anthropic's Tino Cuéllar and the UK's AI minister, Kanishka Narayan.

The King did not mince words. "There seems urgency in adequately considering the existential dangers of such technologies falling into the wrong hands, and being used in potentially catastrophic ways," he told the room, adding that "the development of AI — its substance and its pace — are both intriguing and deeply concerning in equal measure."

It is the kind of line that makes headlines and moves nothing. The summit produced no binding commitments — delegates discussed whether the industry could agree on a shared set of guiding principles, and left it there. No regulatory timeline, no legislative proposal, no funding announcement. Buckingham Palace does not write UK tech policy; that remains the job of ministers, and Narayan's department has made no new commitments since the gathering broke up.

A rival species, from inside the industry

What gave the week its edge was a warning from someone with rather more skin in the game. Mustafa Suleyman, the DeepMind co-founder who now runs Microsoft AI, told the BBC's Today programme that letting AI systems act with too much independence risks "seeding a new silicon species which will no doubt compete with us for resources." His scenario: AI agents that set their own goals, earn money, own assets and run businesses without a human signing off at each step.

Suleyman was direct about where he thinks that risk is being courted. He singled out Anthropic's approach of training its Claude models to exercise judgment and reflect human-like values, calling it "mistaken and misguided" — while conceding the industry will only find out by testing it. His prescription is narrower than the King's grand language suggests: independent scrutiny of how AI systems are trained and evaluated, and better tools to monitor and constrain them, so that whatever gets built stays "subordinate to humanity."

The timing is not coincidental. Suleyman's remarks landed the same week as the Dumfries House summit, and days after Anthropic chief executive Dario Amodei published an essay urging AI labs to deliberately slow the pace of capability gains — a call that both Sam Altman at OpenAI and Elon Musk at xAI publicly endorsed. Even the companies racing hardest are now, in public at least, arguing for brakes. Whether that translates into anyone actually easing off is a separate question.

Grand language, thin substance

Strip away the royal setting and the rival-species framing, and what is left is a familiar pattern: senior figures inside and around the AI industry flagging existential-scale risks in public, while the concrete asks stay modest — more transparency, more scrutiny, a shared statement of principles nobody has yet agreed to. Suleyman's proposals amount to audits and monitoring tools, not a moratorium. The King's palace has convening power but no legislative one. And the dispute between Microsoft and Anthropic over how human-like an AI should be allowed to seem is, at bottom, a design disagreement dressed up in the language of species-level danger.

That gap between the rhetoric and the remedy is exactly where policy attention now needs to go. The UK's AI Safety Institute and the government's promised AI bill remain the venues where any of this could turn into rules with teeth. Until then, a monarch's warning and a tech executive's essay are signals of establishment anxiety, not the governance framework itself.

The next marker to watch is whether Dumfries House produces anything beyond talk — a published set of principles, a follow-up meeting date, or a UK regulatory move tied to the AI bill the government has been promising. Absent that, this week's warnings join the growing pile of statements about AI's dangers that have yet to become policy.
