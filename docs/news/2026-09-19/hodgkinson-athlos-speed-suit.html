Keely Hodgkinson wins Athlos 800m in new speed suit

Keely Hodgkinson racing the 800m at the 2023 European Indoor Championships in Istanbul. — Erik van Leeuwen / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Keely Hodgkinson won Athlos London's 800m in a bespoke Nike speed suit — but ran slower in it than she had a week earlier in ordinary kit.

Keely Hodgkinson closed her season the way she has closed most of them lately — winning — but this time the story was as much about what she was wearing as how she ran.

The Olympic 800m champion won the final event of the 2026 Athlos series in London on Friday night, clocking 1:56.40 to take the meeting title in a full-length, hooded Nike suit unlike anything seen on a track before. Black from ankle to scalp, with a ponytail slit cut into the hood and no visible seams or zips, it looked more like something from a velodrome final than a middle-distance race. Nike says it is a genuine one-off, built around Hodgkinson's own biomechanics over roughly a year of fittings, wind-tunnel sessions and track testing in South Africa.

"It's a mixture of fashion, speed and science all coming together, for maybe the future of athletics potentially," Hodgkinson said afterwards.

A suit six months in the making

Nike's account of the project, laid out by innovation design director Hannah Amis, reads like a Formula 1 development log rather than a kit launch. The hood "streamlines the hairline and manages the chaotic aerodynamics of hair in motion" via 3D-printed vortex generators — small ridges that Nike calls its fastest yet at reducing drag. An articulated knee and a "4D mobility gusset" were added for range of motion, with cut-outs at the back and underarms for cooling. Hodgkinson herself insisted on keeping her signature ponytail visible, even though, by Nike's own logic on hair and drag, that costs her a fraction of the aerodynamic gain the rest of the suit is designed to deliver — a small concession of speed for identity that says something about how these projects actually get built: through negotiation with the athlete, not pure wind-tunnel arithmetic.

She wore it in competition for the first time at Athlos, having trained in it through South African heat that reportedly touched 40°C. "This has been like a year in the making with all the adjustments and fittings," she said. "I'm honoured they let me put this on show — it's something I'll remember forever."

The number that complicates the story

Here is the inconvenient detail for anyone tempted to credit the suit with the win: 1:56.40 was slower than the 1:56.26 Hodgkinson ran eight days earlier at the World Athletics Ultimate Championship in Budapest — in ordinary racing kit, and in a race she lost, to Switzerland's Audrey Werro. Her two most dangerous rivals this year, Werro and the Netherlands' Femke Broeders-Bol, who beat or pushed her in Budapest, were not in the Athlos field at all. Against a thinner line-up — Valentina Rosamilia was more than two seconds back in second — a meeting-record time and a comfortable margin tell you more about the depth of the field than about drag coefficients.

None of which makes the suit a gimmick, exactly. Aerodynamic gains in middle-distance running are real but small, typically measured in hundredths of a second per lap rather than the visible margins swimming or cycling have found with similar tech. The honest reading of Athlos is that Nike got a striking debut and a highlight reel, and Hodgkinson got to close an injury-interrupted season on a win, but the suit itself remains unproven as a genuine time-shaving device under full competitive pressure.

Why it matters beyond one race

The pattern is familiar from swimming's polyurethane-suit era and cycling's aero arms race: a marquee athlete debuts bespoke equipment at a showcase event, the visual is arresting, and the inference — better kit, faster athlete — gets drawn before the data supports it. If suits like this do eventually prove decisive, the competitive edge tilts further toward athletes and federations with manufacturer backing deep enough to fund a year of individualised wind-tunnel work, rather than toward training and talent alone. For Nike, regardless of the aerodynamic verdict, a one-of-one suit built around Britain's biggest female track star ahead of a quiet post-Olympic year is a marketing asset in its own right, at a time when the company has been fending off boardroom pressure over its competitive standing against Adidas and On.

The next real test is whether Hodgkinson, or anyone else, races in a version of this suit against Werro and Broeders-Bol with something other than a meeting title at stake — most likely at next year's World Championships in Tokyo, where a head-to-head against full-strength opposition would settle whether the vortex generators earn their keep or just their column inches.
