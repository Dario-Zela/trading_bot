Fed hikes to 3.75%-4.00%, still eyeing another move this year

Kevin Warsh, whose September 16 press conference did little to settle markets on how far the Fed's hiking cycle will go. — Federal Reserve / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The Fed raised rates a quarter point to 3.75%-4.00%, its first hike since 2023, and signaled at least one more by year-end.

The Federal Reserve raised its benchmark interest rate a quarter point on Wednesday, to a target range of 3.75%-4.00%, ending an 18-month pause and delivering the central bank's first hike since 2023. The vote was unanimous, 12-0. It was also the first rate move of Kevin Warsh's chairmanship, and it landed the way he had been telegraphing it would.

The case, in the Fed's telling, is straightforward: core PCE inflation ran at 3.3% in July, more than a point above the 2% target, and hasn't meaningfully budged in months. Warsh had already flagged the direction of travel at Jackson Hole in August, saying the summer's softer inflation prints didn't tell him underlying price pressure had eased. Wednesday's move followed through on that.

The dots move with it

What unsettled markets more than the hike itself was the guidance attached to it. The Fed's updated projections put the median year-end 2026 fed funds rate at 4.1%, up from 3.8% in June — arithmetic that implies one more quarter-point increase before the year is out. Sixteen of eighteen FOMC participants now see at least one further hike; four pencilled in two. The 2027 median rose in step, to 4.1%, effectively erasing the easing path investors had been pricing for next year.

Warsh's press conference added to the unease without adding much clarity. Asked how far above neutral the current rate now sits, he waved off the question, calling neutral-rate framing "useful academically" but denying it had "any operational effect" on the committee's decisions — an answer that markets read as cover for further tightening rather than reassurance that this was a one-and-done move. His phrase that the Fed had withdrawn "a dose of accommodation" from the economy did more to unsettle traders than any single dot on the chart, and by Friday morning futures markets had priced a 55% chance of another hike at the October 28 meeting and 90% odds of one by December 9.

A market still finding its footing

Equities took the news in stages rather than all at once. The S&P 500 fell 0.45% the day of the decision as the Dow dropped more than 600 points and bank stocks had their worst session since February; the following day both major indices rebounded sharply, the S&P up 1.14% and the Nasdaq up 1.69%. By Friday's close the week had settled into something closer to a shrug: the S&P 500 ended at 7,650.50, up 0.17% on the day but down 0.1% for the week; the Nasdaq gained 0.7% over the same stretch. Treasury yields, meanwhile, stayed elevated rather than retreating — the kind of response that suggests investors are still repricing for a tightening cycle that isn't finished, not celebrating one that is.

That is the shape of the story: a Fed that broke an 18-month hold with unanimous conviction, a chairman unwilling to rule out — or clearly commit to — where the rate stops rising, and a market too divided on the answer to move decisively in either direction. The next data points that matter are the September jobs report and the next core PCE reading; either could tip the odds on October 28 well before the Fed has to.
