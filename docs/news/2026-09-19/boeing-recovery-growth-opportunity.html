Boeing's recovery seen creating multi-year growth opportunity, analysts say

In one sentence
Zacks and Wall Street see a multi-year Boeing earnings recovery in the 737's climb to 47 jets a month — but August's 787 delivery dip shows the ramp isn't guaranteed.

Boeing delivered 51 commercial jets in August, a slight step back from July's 53 and below the 57 it handed over a year earlier. The dip was driven almost entirely by the 787 Dreamliner line, where handovers fell to four aircraft from nine in July and 13 in June. A company spokesperson said the drop was not tied to any production, supply-chain or certification problem — but the numbers landed just as Wall Street was building a bull case around the opposite trend: a manufacturer finally executing on a multi-year ramp.

The headline number analysts are watching is the 737 MAX production rate. The Federal Aviation Administration lifted its post-Alaska Airlines door-plug production cap in March, and by late May Boeing said it had cleared the FAA's "capstone review" to run the line at 47 aircraft a month, up from the 38-a-month ceiling imposed in January 2024 and the 42-a-month rate approved last October. Chief executive Kelly Ortberg has set 52 a month as the next rate break, with an internal ambition of 63 eventually. Through August, Boeing had delivered 418 aircraft for the year, including 324 737s — comfortably ahead of last year's pace.

What Zacks is telling clients

Sheraz Mian, Zacks Investment Research's director of research, has pointed to that trajectory as the basis for what he calls a multi-year earnings recovery rather than a one-quarter bounce. Zacks' consensus estimates now show Boeing's 2026 earnings per share improving roughly 91% year over year, with a further jump of more than 500% penciled in for 2027 as the 737 and 787 lines both scale and the company works through a backlog that still runs into the thousands of aircraft. Zacks currently rates the stock a Hold (Rank #3), reflecting mixed Value and Growth scores even as momentum indicators point up — a reminder that the case rests on execution still to come, not results already banked.

Sell-side desks have leaned more bullish. Jefferies' Sheila Kahyaoglu reaffirmed a Buy rating on September 14 with a $295 price target, citing the pace of deliveries through August. Redburn Atlantic has gone further, raising Boeing to "strong buy" with a $275 target. Across 28 analysts tracked by S&P Global, the consensus is Buy, with an average target near $275 — implying roughly 30% upside from current levels.

The catch: nothing here is locked in

What none of these targets or estimates account for is a further slip in the ramp itself, and August's soft 787 number is exactly the kind of wobble that could compound. Boeing folded Spirit AeroSystems — maker of the 737 fuselage and other structures for the 767, 777 and 787 — back into the company in December after two decades of outsourcing, a deal meant to fix the quality lapses that repeatedly slowed the MAX line. Integrating Spirit's plants in Wichita, Tulsa, Dallas and Belfast while simultaneously pushing rate is not a small task, and industry analysts flag engine availability and supplier capacity — not Boeing's own assembly lines — as the more likely constraint on single-aisle output through the rest of 2026. Boeing still says it expects to deliver 90 to 100 787s for the full year, which would require a sharp rebound from August's four.

The distinction matters for anyone trading the suppliers rather than Boeing stock itself. Engine makers, fuselage and structures suppliers, and leasing companies are all priced, to varying degrees, on the assumption that 47-a-month becomes 52-a-month on schedule. A stall at Spirit's reintegrated plants, or a renewed engine bottleneck, would ripple through that chain faster than it would show up in Boeing's own delivery count, since suppliers carry less margin for absorbing a pause.

The next hard data point is Boeing's third-quarter earnings call, expected around October 27, when the company will have to explain whether August's 787 dip was noise or the start of a pattern — and whether the 737 line is still tracking toward 52 a month on the timeline Ortberg has laid out.
