Report warns sensitive UK police data vulnerable to US, foreign access

In one sentence
A report finds sensitive data from 40+ UK police forces sits on US-linked cloud platforms with no policy stopping it, exposing the data to CLOUD Act access.

Sensitive data from more than 40 UK police forces — criminal records, victim statements, internal emails, and biometric files — is sitting on Microsoft's Azure cloud platform despite an internal security assessment warning that the material could be accessed by foreign governments, according to a Guardian investigation into police IT contracts and disclosure documents.

Some of the files exceed the government's "official" security classification, according to a police document reviewed by the paper, raising the possibility that material properly classed as "secret" or "top secret" is being routed through infrastructure the UK does not control.

The gap between policy and practice

The exposure runs wider than email and casework. The Ident1 national fingerprint database — more than 8.4 million records — has been integrated with Amazon Web Services through the Police Digital Service's Xchange platform, while Police Scotland has moved biometric data onto systems run by Microsoft and Axon. In each case, the underlying legal problem is the same: the US CLOUD Act lets American authorities compel US-headquartered companies to hand over data those firms hold, wherever in the world it sits, without a UK court's involvement.

Microsoft has acknowledged the limits of its own assurances. In a 2023 disclosure to Police Scotland, the company said policing data "can go outside the UK" and that it "cannot guarantee data sovereignty." Microsoft maintains it does not give governments direct or unrestricted access to customer data and says it has never handed over UK government data in response to a US or foreign request — but that is a statement of practice, not a legal barrier, since the CLOUD Act itself imposes no such restriction.

The concern is not new. In 2017, senior police officer Ian Dyson chaired a meeting that catalogued 15 distinct risks of moving force data onto Microsoft's global cloud, covering everything from Office 365 to the Azure infrastructure beneath it. Fraser Sampson, the former UK biometrics and surveillance camera commissioner, later put the underlying problem bluntly: "It doesn't really matter, in some respects, where it's housed — it's the degree to which you've got some sovereignty," adding that "the police rap sheet on data protection and databases isn't great."

No policy to stop it

What the report underscores is an absence rather than a single failure: there is no formal UK government policy barring police forces from placing sensitive operational data on US-linked cloud infrastructure, even as data sovereignty is treated as a settled principle elsewhere in UK law and in sectors like health and defence. Individual forces and watchdogs — Scotland's biometrics commissioner among them — have pressed providers for specifics and been told, in effect, that sovereignty cannot be guaranteed. Nothing has stopped procurement from continuing anyway.

That leaves cloud providers exposed to reputational and regulatory risk if a data protection or human rights challenge materialises, and leaves ministers facing a decision they have so far avoided: whether to mandate UK-sovereign hosting for policing data, at a cost likely to run into hundreds of millions of pounds, or to formalise the current arrangement and accept the exposure it carries. The next pressure point is likely to be Holyrood, where Police Scotland's cloud contracts have already drawn parliamentary scrutiny, and any move by the Information Commissioner's Office to issue formal guidance on law enforcement cloud hosting would set the template UK-wide.
