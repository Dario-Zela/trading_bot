Taiwan's central bank expected to hold rate at 2% for 10th straight quarter

Yang Chin-long, Governor of the Central Bank of the Republic of China (Taiwan), who has held the discount rate at 2% for ten straight quarters. — Central Bank of the Republic of China (Taiwan), via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Taiwan's central bank held its rate at 2% for a tenth straight quarter, betting cooling inflation outweighs an AI-driven growth forecast of 11.48%.

Taiwan's central bank left its benchmark discount rate unchanged at 2 percent on Thursday, extending the longest run of policy stability the island has seen since 2019 — even as its AI-fuelled economy posts the kind of growth numbers most finance ministries only dream about.

The hold, confirmed after the board's quarterly meeting in Taipei, marks the tenth straight quarter the rate has sat at 2 percent, unchanged since a hike in March 2023. All 29 economists surveyed by LSEG before the meeting had expected exactly this outcome. What made the decision notable wasn't the number itself but the tension underneath it: inflation has been running above the central bank's 2 percent alert threshold for months, and growth has been accelerating, yet the board chose to sit still anyway.

A hold that looks hawkish

The numbers explain the caution. The central bank now projects full-year consumer price inflation at 2.03 percent, with core inflation at 2.16 percent — both just above target, but a touch cooler than analysts had been braced for after headline CPI topped 2 percent for four consecutive months through August. That softening, flagged by Bloomberg ahead of the meeting, gave the board room to hold without looking complacent. Two directors still dissented in favour of a hike, according to people familiar with the board's deliberations, underscoring that the pause was not unanimous comfort but a majority judgment call.

Governor Yang Chin-long pointed to "global risks, particularly energy threats from Middle East tensions," as reason enough to keep borrowing costs steady until price pressures cool further. The bank expects inflation to drop back below 2 percent in 2027. Yang has also signalled that the bank is "moving toward tightening" through market operations — trimming liquidity without touching the headline rate — a middle path that lets policymakers lean against inflation without derailing exporters riding the AI wave.

Growth the hold doesn't need to chase

That AI wave is doing plenty of the economy's work already. The central bank raised its full-year growth forecast to 11.48 percent, powered by chip export orders that show little sign of easing, before penciling in a slowdown to 5.82 percent next year as those export gains normalize. Semiconductor demand tied to AI infrastructure has been the single biggest swing factor in Taiwan's numbers all year, and it is precisely because growth needs no help from cheaper credit that the central bank can afford to prioritise price stability over stimulus.

The board also loosened property rules alongside the rate hold, raising the loan-to-value ceiling on second-home mortgages from 60 percent to 70 percent and scrapping a 2021 construction-deadline requirement for developers sitting on land-purchase loans — a sign officials see more risk in an overcooled housing market than in an overheating one.

What it means beyond Taipei

For traders, the hold reinforces Taiwan's position as an outlier in a region where the Bank of Korea and other central banks have been tightening. Yang has described the approach as going "our own way," independent of the US Federal Reserve's path. A steady discount rate, paired with quieter liquidity tightening, tends to support the Taiwan dollar without the volatility a headline hike would bring, and it keeps the rate gap with the Fed's benchmark — now 3.75–4.00 percent after this week's move — from widening further in ways that would pressure capital flows.

The next data point to watch is Taiwan's September CPI reading, due in early October, which will show whether the deceleration the board is banking on is real or a one-month blip. If inflation reaccelerates, the two dissenting board members may not stay a minority for long.
