MI5 admits giving false evidence in neo-Nazi informant case

Thames House on Millbank in London, headquarters of MI5, the UK's domestic security service. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
MI5 admitted to England's High Court that it gave false evidence to protect a violent neo-Nazi informant, and judges are weighing contempt proceedings.

MI5 told three of England's most senior judges on Friday that it accepts, "without reservation," a devastating official finding: that its officers gave false evidence to courts — including the Court of Appeal — in a legal battle over a violent neo-Nazi informant the agency had run for years.

The admission closes, at least formally, a four-year saga that began with a domestic abuse case and ended with Britain's domestic security service conceding it misled the judiciary itself. The informant, known only as Agent X for legal reasons, was a self-identified neo-Nazi whom MI5 recruited as a covert human intelligence source inside far-right networks. His former partner, referred to in court as Beth, says he used his status as a spy to terrorise her — coercive control, threats, and an assault in which he attacked her with a machete.

In one sentence: MI5 has admitted to England's High Court that it gave false evidence to protect a violent neo-Nazi informant, and judges are now weighing whether to hold the agency or its officers in contempt of court.

A story MI5 tried to bury

The BBC first investigated Agent X in 2022, seeking to reveal his identity so that Beth and others could be protected from him. The government fought the broadcast in court, and while it failed to stop the story running, it succeeded in winning X permanent legal anonymity — the courts accepted the argument that naming a far-right extremist who had worked as an informant could put his life at risk.

That anonymity order became the mechanism through which the deeper problem surfaced. MI5's official position, repeated across multiple proceedings including the Investigatory Powers Tribunal and the Court of Appeal, was that it maintained a strict "neither confirm nor deny" policy about whether X had ever been one of its sources. That policy is close to sacred within the intelligence services: confirming or denying a source's status, case by case, would make every past or present informant identifiable by elimination, and MI5 has long argued in court that breaching it endangers lives.

The policy turned out to be fiction in this case. A recording surfaced of an MI5 officer discussing X's status directly with a BBC journalist in 2020 — years before the agency swore in court filings that NCND had been rigorously observed. Jude Bunting KC, representing the BBC, told the High Court the relevant MI5 officer, referred to in proceedings as Person B, "seems to have deliberately lied."

The Goldring findings

The reckoning followed a familiar British pattern: journalism forces a limited concession, litigation forces a fuller one, and only a formal inquiry forces the whole picture into view. After the High Court ordered an independent investigation in July 2025, and Prime Minister Keir Starmer directed the Investigatory Powers Commissioner's Office to examine the case that September, Sir John Goldring — then deputy investigatory powers commissioner — led the inquiry that produced Friday's reckoning.

Goldring's report, according to Sir James Eadie KC, representing the Attorney General, found "serious and systemic failures in MI5's conduct." Specifically, it concluded that one senior officer, referred to as Officer 2, lied repeatedly about the agency's handling of X's status, while another, Officer 3, misled his own MI5 colleagues about what he had been told. Record-keeping around press contacts was found to be inadequate across the board — a failure that let false claims about NCND compliance go unchallenged inside the agency for years.

"Everyone from the Director General onwards acknowledges the seriousness of what has occurred." — Sir James Eadie KC, representing the Attorney General

Timothy Otty, MI5's own counsel, confirmed on Friday that the agency accepts the findings in full, including those concerning named individual officers and the systemic conclusion that its internal checks failed to catch the false evidence before it reached the courts.

Beth's settlement, and what remains open

MI5 has separately settled Beth's human rights claim, paying her a confidential sum in damages and issuing what it called an unreserved private apology — without admitting legal liability for the abuse itself. Her lawyer, Kate Ellis, joint head of litigation at the Centre for Women's Justice, said a full hearing of Beth's allegations "would, in my view, have been highly embarrassing for MI5, who have already been heavily criticised for misleading the courts." Beth's own statement was pointed: she said she was grateful for the compensation but that, despite the apology, she felt MI5 was "still protecting this violent misogynistic predator."

What the settlement does not resolve is the institutional question now before a panel of three of the country's most senior judges — Lady Chief Justice Baroness Sue Carr, President of the King's Bench Division Dame Victoria Sharp, and Mr Justice Chamberlain. They are considering whether to open contempt of court proceedings, either against individual MI5 officers or against the security service as an institution. A finding of contempt against a state intelligence agency, rather than an individual defendant, would be close to unprecedented in modern British legal history.

Why the wider question won't go away

What makes this more than a single agency's embarrassment is the process that exposed it. MI5's false evidence was not caught by internal audit, by IPCO's routine oversight, or by the tribunal system built to supervise covert intelligence work. It was caught because a BBC journalist happened to be recording a phone call, and because Beth and her lawyers refused to let the case drop across three separate courts over several years. Had that recording not existed, MI5's account of events — asserted formally, under its institutional authority, before judges — would likely stand unchallenged today.

That is the uncomfortable inference sitting underneath Friday's admission: the safeguards designed to prevent exactly this kind of institutional falsehood did not work, and what worked instead was accident and persistence. Whether Agent X is an isolated case of an agency protecting a source it should have dropped years earlier, or a symptom of how routinely NCND claims go unverified by the courts that rely on them, is not something Friday's hearing answered. The judges' ruling on contempt — expected in the coming months — will be the first indication of how seriously the judiciary intends to test that question itself.
