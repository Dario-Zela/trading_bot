Bitcoin tops $80,000 as Treasury yields reclaim 5%

In one sentence
Bitcoin topped $80,000 as Treasury yields reclaimed 5% and stocks slipped, but the moves likely reflect separate causes, not a new safe-haven role for crypto.

Bitcoin pushed past $80,000 on Friday, and for a few hours the move looked like it might mean something more than the usual crypto churn. It arrived in the same session that the 10-year Treasury yield climbed back above 5% and the S&P 500 slipped, the kind of pairing that gets traders reaching for words like "decoupling" and "safe haven." The more careful reading is that two separate stories happened to land on the same day.

The bond side is the more legible of the two. The 10-year note yield rose roughly seven basis points to reclaim 5%, while the 30-year climbed to 5.34%, near its highest level in almost two decades. Traders have been pricing in the possibility that higher energy costs feed back into inflation, complicating the Federal Reserve's path just after this month's hike to a 3.75%–4.00% target range. Kansas City Fed President Jeffrey Schmid added to the hawkish tone, defending further tightening against inflation that has stayed above 3% on a broad basis. The S&P 500 eased 0.2% to 7,621.16 and the Dow gave back 222 points, or 0.4%, to 51,555.73 — a modest, unsurprising reaction to yields at multi-year highs.

A rally with its own momentum

Bitcoin's move looks less like a reaction to bonds and more like a continuation of a rally that was already underway. Spot bitcoin ETFs had just flipped back into net inflows after a rough stretch, and crypto-adjacent equities went along for the ride: Strategy Inc. jumped 12.9% to $149.25, Coinbase climbed 10.6% to $192.32 on trading volumes and a new SEC tokenization exemption, and Bitmine Immersion Technologies added 7.4%. None of that has an obvious causal link to the Treasury market. It reads more like sector-specific enthusiasm — regulatory news, ETF flows, leveraged proxies moving together — than a rotation out of bonds and into crypto as an inflation hedge.

The data on correlation backs that up. Bitcoin's 90-day correlation with the 10-year yield sits at roughly -0.17, a weak relationship, compared with -0.41 for gold. If Bitcoin were genuinely stepping into gold's role as the asset investors buy when they lose faith in government paper, its price should move against yields far more consistently than it does. Instead it still tracks risk sentiment more than it tracks rates — just not perfectly, and not on this particular Friday.

Why the coincidence still matters

That distinction is not just academic. Algorithmic strategies and portfolio hedges increasingly treat asset correlations as inputs, and a market that mistakes coincidence for a structural shift can end up mispricing risk in both directions — either overhedging bitcoin exposure against rate moves that don't actually predict it, or underhedging on the assumption that crypto will keep behaving like a risk asset when a real macro shock hits. Even skeptical coverage of Friday's action stopped short of calling the pattern noise entirely; the honest position is that one session of divergence, however striking the round numbers look, does not establish which asset is leading and which is coincidental.

The next real test arrives with the following Fed meeting and the inflation prints between now and then. If yields keep climbing on genuine inflation concern and bitcoin holds its ground rather than selling off with equities, that would start to look like evidence rather than coincidence. Until then, $80,000 and 5% are two numbers that happened on the same day, not yet a story about what bitcoin has become.
