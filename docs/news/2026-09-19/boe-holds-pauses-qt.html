BoE holds at 3.75%, pauses gilt sales as three MPC members push for hike to 4%

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, which held its Bank Rate at 3.75% while overhauling its gilt-sale programme. — David Iliff / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
The BoE held rates at 3.75% on a 6-3 vote but paused gilt sales until April 2027, sending 10-year yields down 9bp and easing 30-year yields to 5.75%.

The Bank of England left its Bank Rate unchanged at 3.75% on Thursday, but it was the small print on gilt sales, not the headline number, that moved markets. The Monetary Policy Committee voted 6-3 to hold, and in the same breath told bond traders it would not resume active sales of long-dated gilts before April 2027 — a pause in quantitative tightening (QT) that investors read as the clearest signal yet that the tightening cycle is winding down, rate hold or not.

Ten-year gilt yields fell 9 basis points on the announcement. Thirty-year yields, which had touched 5.89% earlier this month — their highest since 1998, on fears about the November budget — eased back to around 5.75%. For a market that has spent much of 2026 pricing in ever-heavier gilt supply, a central bank stepping back from the sell side, even temporarily, was worth more than a quarter-point of rate guidance.

The QT overhaul

Quantitative tightening is the mirror image of the money-printing the Bank did during the pandemic: having bought gilts to hold rates down, it has since been running that portfolio off, either letting bonds mature or actively selling them, to drain the liquidity back out of the system. That process has been a persistent source of downward pressure on gilt prices — extra supply hitting a market already nervous about Britain's borrowing needs.

Governor Andrew Bailey used Thursday's decision to rewrite the plan almost from scratch. The Bank set out a path to reduce its Asset Purchase Facility holdings to zero, but stretched the timeline out to 2034 and changed the mechanics along the way. It will retain £222 billion of gilts maturing before 2035 to run off naturally rather than sell, and hold a further £120 billion of the longest-dated bonds to maturity to help back banknote issuance. For the remaining £146 billion, maturing between 2035 and 2049, the Bank is in talks with the Treasury and the Debt Management Office about selling gilts directly to government at market prices instead of running its own auctions — sidestepping the auction mechanism that has repeatedly rattled the long end of the curve this year.

"Today we provided clarity over the future of our quantitative tightening policy," Bailey said, adding that the Bank would "withhold a substantial part of the stock of gilts held for monetary policy purposes from sale while the remainder will be unwound over the next eight years." In practice, that cuts the pace of QT to roughly £46 billion a year, down from £70 billion, with £20 billion of that continuing as sales alongside maturing debt rather than the current mix.

A committee pulling in different directions

The rate vote itself was closer than the QT overhaul suggested a unified Bank speaks with. Three of the nine MPC members — external member Catherine Mann, deputy governor for monetary policy Huw Pill, and external member Megan Greene — voted for an immediate quarter-point rise to 4%, arguing that energy-driven inflation has not been beaten back far enough to justify standing pat. The Bank's own forecasts show inflation topping 4% in early 2027, well above target, which gives the hawkish minority a credible case even as the majority held its ground.

It was the same 6-3 split recorded at the July meeting, suggesting this is now a settled fault line on the committee rather than a one-off wobble. The dissenters' argument is straightforward: with inflation still climbing and energy costs doing much of the pushing, cutting the Bank slack on rates risks letting price growth become entrenched. The majority's counter, implicit in the QT decision, is that the economy is already absorbing enough tightening through the balance-sheet channel — and that hammering the gilt market with both a rate rise and continued heavy sales at once would be needlessly restrictive.

Reading the signal

Taken together, the two decisions tell a coherent story even though they point in slightly different directions on paper. Holding rates with three dissents for higher borrowing costs looks hawkish. Pausing gilt sales for a year and a half, and restructuring the entire QT programme around minimising market disruption, looks like a central bank preparing the ground for an eventual easing cycle rather than digging in for more tightening. Gilt traders picked the second interpretation, and the fall in yields — sharper at the front end than a simple rate hold would justify — reflects that.

The practical effect is to remove a known source of selling pressure from a gilt market that has spent 2026 anxious about fiscal supply, just as Chancellor Rachel Reeves heads into a difficult November budget. It buys the Treasury some breathing room on financing costs at the long end, even as the Bank's own inflation forecast argues for caution rather than complacency.

The next test comes with the November budget, where the government's borrowing plans will either validate the market's calmer mood on gilts or reignite the supply fears that pushed 30-year yields near 6% earlier this month. Beyond that, watch whether the hawkish trio's dissent grows or shrinks at the November MPC meeting — a fourth vote for a hike would signal the committee's patience with above-target inflation is running out, QT pause or not.
