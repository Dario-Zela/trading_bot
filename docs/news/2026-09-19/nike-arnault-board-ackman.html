Nike adds LVMH's Alexandre Arnault to board after Ackman pressure campaign

Alexandre Arnault, deputy CEO of LVMH's Moët Hennessy and Nike's newest board member. — Sedentaire / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Nike added LVMH executive Alexandre Arnault to its board as the stock craters and Bill Ackman — no longer a shareholder — called it a step in the right direction.

Nike named Alexandre Arnault, the 34-year-old deputy chief executive of LVMH's Moët Hennessy division, to its board of directors this week, expanding the panel to twelve members and handing the struggling sportswear giant a director whose entire career has been built inside the luxury conglomerate his father controls. The appointment, effective September 15, drew an immediate and pointed endorsement from Bill Ackman, the activist investor whose own bruising two-year bet on Nike's turnaround has made him one of the company's most-watched outside critics.

"Great development," Ackman wrote on X, adding that he was "a fan" of Arnault and, more bluntly, that "Nike needs help." The market's reaction was calmer than the quote: shares rose roughly 1.5% in the following session, a modest bump for a stock that has lost close to 40% of its value this year alone.

A luxury résumé for a mass-market crisis

Arnault's credentials read like a checklist for exactly the kind of repositioning Nike's critics have been demanding. He ran RIMOWA, the German luggage maker, from 2016 to 2020 after LVMH acquired it, engineering a brand relaunch credited with roughly doubling its revenue. He then spent four years as executive vice president of product and communications at Tiffany & Co., steering the jeweler's own image refresh following LVMH's $15.8 billion acquisition of it. Since February 2025 he has been deputy CEO of Moët Hennessy, LVMH's wine and spirits arm. He also sits on LVMH's own board and is a trustee of the Museum of Modern Art in New York — a résumé built entirely around premium positioning, not the mass-athletic retail that has been Nike's business since Phil Knight sold shoes from the back of a car.

He joins a board that already includes Knight and Apple chief executive Tim Cook, who has served as Nike's lead independent director since 2005. Arnault received a sign-on grant of restricted shares worth $200,000 and will serve until the 2027 annual meeting. Nike has not signaled a change in product strategy tied to the appointment, but the symbolism is not subtle: a company whose core business is built on volume and accessibility has just added a director whose entire track record is about scarcity, heritage pricing and brand elevation.

The activist who already lost the bet

What makes Ackman's applause notable is that he is no longer a Nike shareholder. Pershing Square built a stake in the company starting in mid-2024, growing it past $1.4 billion by year-end as a public bet on a turnaround under new chief executive Elliott Hill. In early 2025 the fund converted that equity position into deep in-the-money call options — a structure that amplified potential gains but also masked the position from routine 13F disclosures. In February of that year Ackman said on X that he intended to discuss the business directly with Hill. The thesis did not play out: by November 2025, Pershing Square had exited the options entirely, taking a loss estimated at roughly 30% on the position, or more than $600 million.

That history complicates the framing of this week's news as a straightforward activist victory. Ackman has no formal leverage over Nike's board and made no public demand tied to Arnault specifically. What he retains is reputational standing as an investor who spent 18 months publicly pressing the company to fix itself, lost badly, and is now watching from outside as Nike makes a personnel move that at least gestures toward the kind of change he wanted. His welcome reads less like a victory lap than relief that someone else might succeed where his money didn't.

A company in genuine distress

The backdrop explains why any credible signal of change draws attention. Nike's market capitalization has fallen from roughly $264 billion five years ago to about $57 billion today, a decline of nearly 78%. On September 21 — two days after this appointment lands — S&P Dow Jones Indices will remove Nike from the S&P 100 after nearly 18 years in the index, replacing it with four technology names: Dell, Palo Alto Networks, Arista Networks and SanDisk. Nike keeps its place in the broader S&P 500, but the demotion from the top-100 club is a hard marker of how far the company has fallen relative to its former peer set.

Hill's "Win Now" plan, launched when he took over as CEO in September 2024, has delivered mixed results at best. North America grew revenue 3% in the most recent quarter on a 10% jump in wholesale shipments, and running has posted five straight quarters of double-digit growth as Nike rebuilds relationships with retailers it had spent years trying to cut out. But Nike Sportswear and Jordan streetwear — together roughly half of total revenue — posted double-digit declines in the same quarter, with management warning both categories will stay negative through fiscal 2027. China, once Nike's highest-margin growth engine, saw sales fall 17%, and the company does not expect a China recovery before fiscal 2028. Hill himself has acknowledged the turnaround is taking "longer" than he expected.

What the appointment actually signals

One board seat does not rewrite a company's product strategy, and Arnault has no operating role at Nike. But boards shape strategy at the margins that matter — capital allocation, brand positioning debates, executive succession — and a director steeped in scarcity-driven luxury economics is a different voice in that room than the retail and consumer-goods executives who have historically populated Nike's board. For a company whose core problem is that its once-premium sneaker culture has been diluted by years of oversupply and discounting, having someone in the room who built his career on the opposite instinct is at minimum a statement of intent.

The next real test of whether that intent translates into results comes October 1, when Nike reports fiscal first-quarter 2027 earnings — the first results since Arnault's appointment and since the company's exit from the S&P 100. Traders will be watching Sportswear and Jordan streetwear numbers most closely; those are the categories management itself has flagged as the drag on the turnaround, and the ones where a luxury sensibility, if it has any influence at all, would show up first.
