Badenoch to challenge government after Tory council's 94% tax hike

Kemi Badenoch, who says the Conservatives have instructed lawyers to challenge government funding cuts blamed for Wandsworth's 94% council tax rise. — Roger Harris / UK House of Commons, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
Badenoch has instructed lawyers to challenge Labour's council funding formula after it left Tory-run Wandsworth facing a 94% council tax rise.

A council tax bill nearly doubling. A Conservative-run authority pointing the finger at a Labour government. And now the Tory leader knocking on the door of a King's Counsel to see if any of it can be stopped in court.

Kemi Badenoch has told the BBC's Newsnight that the Conservative Party has "instructed lawyers" to explore a legal challenge against the government over funding cuts she says are forcing Wandsworth Council, one of the party's flagship London boroughs, to raise council tax by 94% — an increase of £958 a year on a typical Band D property.

An £84m hole

The root of the dispute is a rewrite of the formula used to distribute central government grants to English councils. Under the changes, which redistribute funding away from a cluster of London boroughs — Wandsworth, Westminster, Richmond, Kensington and Chelsea, and Windsor and Maidenhead among them — Wandsworth stands to lose roughly £84m a year in grant income over the next three years.

Councils normally face a 5% cap on how much they can raise tax in a single year without triggering a local referendum. That cap has been lifted for the boroughs hit hardest by the formula change, which is what has opened the door to a rise as steep as 94%. Wandsworth says the resulting gap in its budget works out to about £1,434 per average household, only a little over a third of which is directly attributable to the funding cut — the rest reflects inflation and spending commitments left over from the borough's own previous administration.

Badenoch's move

Badenoch called the scale of the rise "outrageous," while taking care to note that Wandsworth's Conservative councillors are reacting to the cut rather than choosing it. "Families have budgets," she said, arguing that sudden spikes of this size do real damage to household finances. She went further on the government's motives, suggesting ministers were "taking money from areas that don't vote Labour and giving it to places that do vote Labour."

The barrister the party has brought in for the legal review is understood not to be Lord Wolfson KC, the Conservatives' usual outside counsel — a detail that suggests the party wants a specialist read on the funding formula itself, an unglamorous corner of local government law that rarely finds itself the subject of political attention, let alone litigation.

The government's answer

A government spokesperson pushed back, noting that local authorities set their own tax levels and should prioritise taxpayers, and pointing to more than £78bn made available for council finances, the bulk of it unringfenced. Ministers describe the formula change as "fixing an outdated funding system so funding goes where need is greatest," while insisting transitional protections are in place for affected councils.

That framing — needs-based redistribution versus a politically targeted squeeze — is the real fight here, and it is one that will be relitigated, in Parliament if not in court, long before Wandsworth's residents see a final bill. The council says its formal budget, and the tax rise itself, won't be locked in until it is agreed in March 2027, leaving eighteen months for the argument to run.

What's next

Watch for whether the Conservatives' lawyers actually file anything — a legal challenge to a funding formula is a high bar to clear, since central government has wide discretion over how it allocates grants. In the meantime, the political fight continues: other affected councils, several also Conservative-run, are likely to make similar noise as their own budget-setting seasons approach, and Wandsworth's March 2027 vote will be the first hard test of whether the 94% figure survives contact with actual ratepayers.
