Nikkei outpaces S&P 500 as yen slides after BOJ hike

The Tokyo Stock Exchange, where the Nikkei 225 has outpaced Wall Street by a wide margin in 2026. — Kakidai / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Nikkei has outpaced the S&P 500 by roughly 20 points this year as a divided BOJ's rate hike still left US-Japan rates about 2.5 points apart, weakening the yen further.

Japan's stock market has spent 2026 doing something it rarely does: beating Wall Street by a wide margin. The Nikkei 225 is up roughly 30% for the year, more than double the S&P 500's 10.6% price gain through September 16. The gap has persisted, and in the past week it has widened further — for a reason that would normally work the other way around.

On September 18, the Bank of Japan raised its policy rate by 25 basis points to 1.25%, the highest level in 31 years. A rate hike is supposed to lift a currency. Instead, the yen fell, sliding to around 156.9 per dollar, its weakest in roughly two weeks. The Nikkei rose 1.5% on the news. Higher rates, a weaker currency, a stronger stock market — the sequence only makes sense once the vote count and the guidance are factored in.

A hike that read as a hold

The BOJ's board split 7-2, with two members dissenting in favor of no change. Governor Kazuo Ueda declined to commit to a follow-up move, telling reporters afterward that "there could be various possibilities" and that policymakers "shouldn't rule anything out" — language traders read as leaving the door open rather than signaling intent. Strategists at TD Securities and elsewhere called the framing hawkish on paper but insufficiently hawkish for a market that had spent the prior two weeks buying yen in anticipation of a firmer signal. USD/JPY had touched 155.28 on September 3 as hike bets built; by the time the decision landed, that positioning unwound fast.

The divided vote matters because it caps expectations for the next move. A 7-2 board is a board that will need more convincing before it hikes again, which is exactly the kind of signal that keeps a currency cheap.

The gap that didn't close

The more basic arithmetic is the Federal Reserve. Two days before the BOJ acted, the Fed raised its own target range a quarter point to 3.75%-4.00%, a unanimous, hawkish decision accompanied by a dot plot showing one more hike penciled in for 2026 and only gradual easing after that. So even with the BOJ's move, the gap between the two policy rates sits close to 2.5 percentage points — barely narrower than before, and set to widen again if the Fed follows through in its next meeting while Japan's board stays cautious.

That differential is the engine behind the weak yen, and the weak yen is a large part of the engine behind the strong Nikkei: a big share of Japan's blue-chip exporters — automakers, machinery makers, chip equipment suppliers — book a large share of revenue overseas, and every yen of depreciation inflates those earnings when they're translated back home. It is a mechanical relationship, not a mysterious one, but it depends entirely on the rate gap staying wide.

What would break it

That is also the trade's vulnerability. The rally is a function of a policy divergence rather than a durable shift in how capital is allocated to Japan. If the Fed's "one more hike" projection turns into a pause, or if a less divided BOJ board delivers a more committal signal at a future meeting, the differential compresses and the currency tailwind for Japanese equities fades with it. Carry trades built on that gap — borrowing cheap yen to fund dollar assets — are similarly exposed; they have absorbed the news so far, but they are a crowded position, and crowded positions unwind quickly when the rate math changes.

The S&P 500, for its own part, isn't struggling — a 10.6% year-to-date gain is a solidly above-average year, and the index sits only about 1.9% below its August 13 record close. Japan's outperformance is not a story of American weakness. It is a story of a currency move amplifying Japanese equity returns in a way that has no obvious floor and no obvious permanence.

The next test comes at the BOJ's following policy meeting, where investors will be watching whether the board's split narrows or widens, and at the Fed's next decision, where confirmation of that pencilled-in 2026 hike would keep the rate gap — and the yen trade riding on it — intact for a while longer.
