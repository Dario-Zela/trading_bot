Trump signs sweeping Russia sanctions as Moscow seizes Western firms' assets

In one sentence
Trump signed a law letting him hit Russia's oil buyers with 100% tariffs days before Xi's visit, as Moscow seized Nestlé and Auchan's Russian assets in retaliation.

President Trump signed a sweeping new Russia sanctions law on Friday, handing himself the power to impose tariffs of up to 100% on the countries that keep Moscow's oil money flowing — and doing it, deliberately, days before Chinese leader Xi Jinping arrives in Washington for a state visit. A few hundred miles east, the Kremlin answered in kind: a decree transferring the Russian operations of Nestlé and the French retailer Auchan to a shell company run by an interior ministry general.

Taken together, the two moves mark an escalation on both sides of a war now well into its fifth year — one where the weapons are balance sheets rather than battalions.

A bill named for a dying wish

The law Trump signed is formally the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026, and the name carries weight beyond politics. Graham, the South Carolina Republican who had pushed for tougher Russia sanctions for years, died in July shortly after returning from a trip to Ukraine. The bill became his last legislative legacy, passed by the Senate 86-11 in August and by the House 262-159 on September 16, before landing on Trump's desk two days later.

"The best way to honor Lindsey's memory will be to implement the provisions of this law fully and swiftly," Trump wrote in a statement accompanying the signing. "And the best reward for everyone helping us put pressure on Russia to end its war will be peace."

The law sanctions Vladimir Putin directly, along with Russian officials, oligarchs, banks and the "shadow fleet" of tankers that has let Moscow keep selling oil around Western price caps. But its most consequential lever is a tariff mechanism: the president must impose duties of up to 100% on goods from countries ranking among the top purchasers of Russian crude or gas by volume. China alone has bought roughly half of Russia's crude exports since December 2022; India has taken about 37%, with Turkey a distant third. A House amendment widened the list of potential targets to ten countries, including India, China, Turkey and the UAE — a broader net than the Senate's original five.

Crucially, the law leaves the timing and targeting almost entirely to Trump's discretion. That is what makes the signing date pointed rather than incidental. Xi is due in Washington within days for a state visit, and a law that could nominally flatten trade with Beijing under triple-digit tariffs is now sitting, unused, on the president's desk as a bargaining chip rather than a lit fuse. It is a sharp turn for an administration that has spent much of the year courting Beijing's cooperation on trade rather than confronting it — the bill converts China from a subject Trump has tried to accommodate into a lever he can pull, or withhold pulling, in the same week he sits down with its leader.

Moscow answers with expropriation

While Washington was finishing the bill, Putin signed his own decree — issued late Thursday, hours before Trump's signature — placing the Russian assets of Nestlé, Auchan, the former Leroy Merlin chain (now rebranded Lemana Pro) and the French logistics group FM Logistic under "temporary administration." All four now sit with a company called LEV Management, created only at the end of 2025 and headed by a Russian interior ministry general, with no prior business activity of its own.

The scale is not trivial. Nestlé runs six factories in Russia — coffee, pet care, infant formula — employing around 7,000 people, and generated roughly 2 billion Swiss francs ($2.4 billion) in Russian sales in the last year it disclosed figures. Auchan's Russian arm operates 241 stores, including 62 hypermarkets, with over 33,000 employees.

Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov told reporters the targeted firms came from "unfriendly" countries "currently involved in the most active manner in military actions against our country" — Moscow's now-standard framing for any Western state supplying arms to Kyiv. Nestlé said it was "committed to taking all necessary steps to protect its rights and ensure continuity of business operations in the interests of all stakeholders, particularly its employees." Auchan's Russian unit said it had asked authorities for "clarifications" and would comment once it received them.

The distinction matters: this is not the wave of voluntary exits that swept Western multinationals out of Russia in 2022 and 2023, when companies like McDonald's and Renault negotiated sales, often at a fraction of book value, and left on something resembling their own terms. Nestlé and Auchan chose to stay. Their punishment for staying is now a forced handover to a state-run shell entity — a signal, as one Ukrainian outlet put it, to every remaining Western company that continued presence in Russia carries no protection at all.

Two escalations, one bargaining table

The juxtaposition is not coincidental so much as structural. Sanctions bite by making Russia's revenue harder to collect and its currency harder to spend abroad; asset seizures are the Kremlin's most direct way to make the West's remaining economic presence in Russia costly in return, converting private capital into leverage of its own. Each escalation narrows the room for the other side to de-escalate without looking as though it blinked.

For markets, the immediate question is less about Russia than about the tariff threat's second-order target. A 100% duty applied to Chinese or Indian goods would be a shock to global trade flows on a scale well beyond anything imposed on Russia directly — which is precisely why Trump signed a law that lets him threaten it without yet doing it. Energy traders are left pricing a genuine tail risk into Russian crude flows to Asia, without knowing whether Washington intends to collect on the threat or trade it away at the negotiating table with Xi.

The next marker to watch is the Xi-Trump summit itself: whether Beijing offers any concession on Russian energy purchases in exchange for tariff relief, or whether it calls the bluff and keeps buying at current volumes, betting Trump won't risk a trade rupture with his largest trading partner over Ukraine. Thirty days after signing, the law also requires the administration to act — meaning Washington has a hard deadline to either use the tariff weapon or explain why it hasn't.
