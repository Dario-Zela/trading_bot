US and Denmark reach deal on Greenland, defusing Trump annexation threats

Pituffik Space Base (formerly Thule Air Base) in northwestern Greenland, the hub of the US military presence at the center of the new security agreement — U.S. Army Corps of Engineers / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The US and Denmark will sign a security pact next week granting Washington expanded — but not sovereign — control over Greenland, ending Trump's annexation threats.

The last time Washington and Copenhagen were on speaking terms about Greenland, the American president was refusing to rule out taking the island by force. On Friday, the two governments announced they would instead sign a security agreement — expected in New York next week, timed to coincide with the UN General Assembly — that leaves Danish sovereignty intact while giving the United States sweeping new military and veto rights over the Arctic territory.

The announcement caps a fight that began in January 2025, when Donald Trump threatened to "tariff Denmark at a very high level" if it would not cede Greenland, and escalated through a year of tariff threats, a special envoy appointment, and repeated refusals by Trump to take military force off the table. It ends, for now, not with annexation but with a document neither side has fully published — a resolution that looks less like a territorial realignment and more like both governments finding a face-saving way to step back from a fight that had become useful to no one.

From threats to a treaty text

The crisis had genuine escalatory momentum. Trump used his March 2025 State of the Union address to tell Greenlanders the United States would get the island "one way or the other," while insisting he respected their "right to determine your own future." In May, in an NBC interview, he again declined to rule out military force. By December he had named Louisiana Governor Jeff Landry as a special envoy to Greenland, and by mid-January 2026 he was threatening a 10 percent tariff on Denmark and seven other European countries — due to rise to 25 percent by June — unless Denmark handed over the territory.

The threat evaporated almost as fast as it arrived. On January 21, at Davos, Trump reversed course, saying he and NATO Secretary-General Mark Rutte had sketched "the framework of a future deal." Eight months of negotiation later, that framework has become an actual agreement — though its precise legal terms remain unpublished, and both governments are for now working from summaries given to reporters rather than a released text.

What the deal reportedly contains

According to US officials briefed to CBS News and NBC News, the agreement:

  • Grants the United States permanent access to Greenland and overflight rights, with room to increase the number of American bases
  • Bars any non-NATO country from establishing bases or troop presence on the island
  • Gives Washington an effective veto over foreign — chiefly Russian and Chinese — investment deemed a security risk
  • Remains binding "forever," a State Department official told NBC, including if Greenland eventually becomes fully independent of Denmark

Trump framed the outcome as total victory, posting that the US would "immediately begin the process of developing a large Military presence in the appropriate part of Greenland," and that the deal came at "NO COST" to Washington, calling it an "Infinite Life" agreement. Secretary of State Marco Rubio struck a similar note, saying Greenland "will forever be part of the strategic defense area of North America" and would exclude "any adversary" from it.

Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen offered a notably different emphasis. Her office said the agreement "strengthens our common security in the Arctic and the North Atlantic area," while stressing it does not transfer sovereignty and explicitly recognizes "the right of the Greenlandic people to self-determination." Greenland's premier, Jens-Frederik Nielsen, said the agreement "recognizes Greenland's interests and our place in international cooperation" — a careful phrase from a leader whose government spent much of the past eighteen months publicly resisting American pressure.

Theater, pivot, or both

The gap between the American and Danish framings is the story. Washington is describing an open-ended expansion of US military reach in the Arctic with no territorial cost — a deal it can claim as a coup. Copenhagen is describing a security pact within existing NATO structures that changes little about who owns Greenland or who governs it. Both descriptions can be true simultaneously, because the actual terms — troop numbers, base locations, investment-screening thresholds, what happens to Pituffik Space Base's current footprint — have not been made public, and both parliaments in Nuuk and Copenhagen still have to ratify whatever gets signed.

That ambiguity is itself informative. A year of tariff threats, a floated special envoy, and repeated refusals to disavow force produced, in the end, an agreement that formalizes a US military presence Washington largely already had under a 1951 defense treaty and a NATO umbrella that made non-NATO bases unlikely in any case. What changed most visibly is the temperature of the relationship, not the map. For a story that began with talk of purchase or seizure, arriving instead at a bilateral security pact with an unreleased text looks like the sort of outcome both sides can sell as consistent with what they wanted all along — Trump as expanded reach, Frederiksen as sovereignty preserved.

What comes next

The signing is expected Tuesday in New York, alongside the UN General Assembly gathering that will put Trump, Frederiksen and dozens of other heads of government in the same city. After that, the real test moves to Nuuk and Copenhagen, where Greenland's and Denmark's parliaments must ratify the pact — a process that will force the release of actual treaty language and settle whether this was a genuine recalibration of Arctic strategy or, as the absence of hard specifics so far suggests, a carefully managed exit from a standoff that had run its course.
