Warren Buffett steps down after six decades at Berkshire Hathaway's helm

In one sentence
Buffett hands Berkshire's chairmanship to son Howard, leaving CEO Greg Abel with sole control of a $365bn cash pile.

Warren Buffett has stepped down as chairman of Berkshire Hathaway, closing out 56 years at the head of the conglomerate's board and, with it, roughly six decades of personal command over one of the largest pools of capital in American markets. Buffett, 96, becomes chairman emeritus effective immediately. His son, Howard Buffett, 71, takes the chairman's seat after 33 years on the board. Greg Abel, who became chief executive in January, now runs a Berkshire in which no Buffett holds an operating role for the first time since 1970.

"In one sentence: Buffett hands Berkshire's chairmanship to his son Howard, leaving CEO Greg Abel with full control of a $365 billion cash pile and no Buffett left in an operating seat."

A transition telegraphed a year in advance

The chairman handover was the second, quieter half of a succession plan Buffett announced at Berkshire's annual meeting in Omaha in May 2025, when he told shareholders he would step aside as CEO at year's end. Abel, who had already run Berkshire's sprawling non-insurance businesses since 2018 and led Berkshire Hathaway Energy before that, was confirmed as the successor and took over as CEO on January 1. Buffett stayed on as chairman through the interim, a bridge that let markets and the board test Abel's decision-making with Buffett still formally in charge.

That bridge is now gone. In a letter to shareholders released alongside the announcement, Buffett offered the explanation he has used before for why the timing was his to choose and no one else's: "Father Time always wins. He has, however, been generous with me." He added that one of his great-grandchildren had just turned one and was "moving a bit faster than I am these days" — Buffett's preferred register for a decision that is, in substance, about age and mortality rather than performance.

Abel's ledger, and what it bought him

What makes this transition unusual is how much evidence shareholders already have. Abel has been making capital-allocation calls unsupervised for nine months, and Buffett used the letter to grade him: "My expectations for him were sky high from the start, and he has exceeded them." The record backs the applause. Second-quarter operating earnings rose 16% year-over-year to nearly $13 billion. Abel resumed share buybacks, spending roughly $4.8 billion, after Buffett had gone quiet on repurchases for over a year. He put $10 billion into a new stake in Alphabet and agreed to buy homebuilder Taylor Morrison in a deal valuing it at $8.5 billion — the kind of decisive, sector-specific bet that departs from Buffett's more concentrated, insurance-and-consumer-brand style.

Buffett framed the vote of confidence in economic terms rather than sentimental ones: "The decision to keep every share is an economic decision because I think the prospects of Berkshire will be better under Greg's management than mine." Berkshire's cash reserves, which have swelled for years as Buffett found few investments he judged cheap enough, stood at roughly $365 billion — a war chest now entirely Abel's to deploy, without a Buffett veto sitting one floor up.

Howard's job is not Abel's job

Howard Buffett's new role is deliberately narrow. He will not manage operations or make investment calls; Abel does both. Howard's mandate, as his father put it, is to "guard its culture and values — both worth more than anything on our balance sheet" — a description Buffett sharpened with an unusual analogy, calling his son "a policy the shareholders own and hope never to claim against." Abel echoed the framing in his own statement: "The culture Warren built and the values he championed will remain at the heart of Berkshire, and Howard will be their guardian."

The elder Buffett's own assessment of his successor as chief executive is notably direct for a man known for understatement: "Warren's impact on Berkshire and its owners is without parallel in the history of American business," Abel said of him. Howard brings a different résumé to the chairman's role than Abel brought to the CEO's — 17 years on Coca-Cola's board before retiring from it in 2016, decades running his own philanthropic foundation — credentials suited to institutional stewardship rather than portfolio management, which is precisely the split Berkshire's structure now enforces.

A market that already priced this in

The most telling data point may be how little the stock moved. Berkshire's Class B shares closed at $509.20 the day before the announcement and were roughly flat in early trading the next morning, dipping about 0.3% on the day. That muted reaction is itself informative: the market had already done its repricing back in May 2025 and again in January, when Abel actually took the CEO title. A chairman handover to a family member with an explicitly custodial mandate changes little about who allocates capital.

What the stock's behavior this year does reveal is a harder question for Abel than any single afternoon's trading. Berkshire shares are up only about 1% year-to-date, against an 11.5% gain for the S&P 500 — a gap partly attributable to rising oil prices and a market rotation toward higher-growth names that Berkshire's insurance-and-industrials mix doesn't chase, but one that also reflects shareholders waiting to see whether Abel can deploy that $365 billion cash pile with the same discipline Buffett showed across half a century of cycles.

The next real test of the Abel era will not be a governance announcement but a results season: Berkshire's third-quarter earnings, due in early November, will be the first full quarter reported under a board with no Buffett in an executive chair since the company took its modern form. Investors will be watching capital deployment — further buybacks, further large purchases like the Alphabet stake — for signs of whether Abel intends to keep sitting on Berkshire's cash or start spending it down.
