AstraZeneca wins FDA priority review for rare bone disease drug

In one sentence
The FDA granted priority review to AstraZeneca's efzimfotase alfa for the rare bone disease hypophosphatasia — routine orphan-drug procedure, not a market-moving event.

AstraZeneca's rare-disease unit has cleared a routine but meaningful regulatory hurdle: the US Food and Drug Administration has granted priority review to efzimfotase alfa, an experimental enzyme-replacement therapy for hypophosphatasia, a rare inherited bone disease. The designation, announced by Alexion, AstraZeneca Rare Disease on September 18, doesn't guarantee approval — but it does put a firm date on the calendar, with the FDA expected to rule by the first half of 2027 under the Prescription Drug User Fee Act timeline.

Hypophosphatasia, known as HPP, is caused by a deficiency in the enzyme alkaline phosphatase, which the body needs to mineralise bone properly. Left undertreated, it produces soft, fragile bones, muscle weakness, chronic pain and, in severe paediatric cases, respiratory failure. It is rare enough — estimated at roughly one in 100,000 to one in 300,000 births for the more severe forms — that drugs for it qualify for the FDA's priority-review track, which the agency reserves for treatments it judges likely to offer a meaningful improvement over existing options. That is a routine classification for orphan-disease filings, not a signal of unusual promise, and it does not by itself accelerate the review clock beyond the standard priority timeline.[^1]

A would-be successor, not a new franchise

Efzimfotase alfa is designed to succeed AstraZeneca's existing HPP drug, Strensiq (asfotase alfa), which generated roughly $1.7 billion in sales in 2025.[^2] The pitch for the newer molecule rests on convenience: it is dosed by injection once every two weeks, compared with Strensiq's three-to-six-times-weekly regimen, and the company says trial data show five times fewer injection-site reactions.[^3] AstraZeneca has told investors it expects peak sales in the $3 billion to $5 billion range if the drug wins broad approval — a projection that assumes the therapy can expand beyond Strensiq's current, narrower patient population.[^2]

The clinical picture behind that forecast is mixed. In the Mulberry trial, which enrolled treatment-naive children aged two to twelve, patients on efzimfotase alfa showed clear improvement on a standard radiographic bone-healing scale at 25 weeks, versus no change on placebo. In the Chestnut trial, patients who switched from Strensiq maintained stable bone health on the new drug. But a separate study, Hickory, tested the therapy in patients aged 12 and older using a six-minute walk test as the primary measure of functional benefit — and failed to beat placebo, a setback that complicates AstraZeneca's ambitions to extend the drug beyond younger children.[^2]

Why this one won't move the share price

Rare-disease approvals of this kind rarely register with markets, even when the peak-sales numbers sound large in isolation. HPP's total addressable patient population is small by pharmaceutical standards, and priority review itself is close to the default pathway for orphan-drug applications rather than a mark of unusual regulatory favour. AstraZeneca is a roughly £180 billion company with a broad oncology and biopharmaceuticals portfolio; a single rare-bone-disease filing, however lucrative if it clears the finish line, sits well below the threshold of news that reshapes the investment case.[^4] The filing is best read as normal pipeline maintenance — the unglamorous, ongoing work of a large pharmaceutical company cycling drugs through the FDA — rather than a competitive threat to any rival or a catalyst traders need to price in now.

The date to watch is the PDUFA action deadline in the first half of 2027, when the FDA will issue its final decision on efzimfotase alfa. Between now and then, expect little incremental newsflow beyond routine regulatory updates.
