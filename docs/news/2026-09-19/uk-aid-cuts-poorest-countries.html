Poorest countries hit hardest by UK aid cuts under Burnham government

Prime Minister Andy Burnham, whose government has continued the aid cuts begun under his predecessor. — Alecsandra Dragoi / No 10 Downing Street (OGL v3.0)
In one sentence
New figures show UK aid to the poorest countries fell 7% last year while funding to middle-income nations rose 13%, under cuts Burnham inherited from Starmer.

New figures released this week show that the poorest countries bore a disproportionate share of the cuts to Britain's overseas aid budget last year — even as funding for wealthier, middle-income nations rose. It is the first hard evidence of how Andy Burnham's government has managed, or failed to manage, the aid retrenchment he inherited from Sir Keir Starmer.

According to data compiled by Bond, the umbrella group for UK development charities, funding to least-developed and low-income countries fell by £118m — a 7% cut — in 2025, while aid to low- and middle-income countries rose by £115m, or 13%. Upper-middle-income countries saw a smaller increase too. The overall aid budget fell by £987m to 0.44% of gross national income, down from 0.5% the year before, en route to the 0.3% floor set for 2027.

"The world's least developed countries suffered deep cuts, while UK aid funding for programmes in wealthier middle- and upper-middle income countries actually increased," said Gideon Rabinowitz, Bond's director of policy.

A pattern, not an accident

The unevenness is the striking part. When Starmer announced in February 2025 that aid would fall from 0.5% to 0.3% of GNI to help pay for higher defence spending, ministers described the reduction in broadly proportional terms — a smaller pie, sliced the same way. The Independent Commission for Aid Impact has since found otherwise: cuts were applied unevenly rather than according to assessed effectiveness, with bilateral programmes in Africa and South Asia absorbing the heaviest reductions.

The FCDO's own annual report, published in July, sketches the trajectory to 2028/29: a 49% fall in funding to least-developed countries, a 34% cut to fragile and conflict-affected states, and a combined £950m reduction across both categories — even though the government says it wants 70% of country spending to go to fragile states. Malawi and Mozambique face reductions of around 90% from pre-cut levels. Somalia's direct support is set to fall by 49%, South Sudan's by 46%, despite two-thirds of its population needing humanitarian assistance next year. The Democratic Republic of Congo loses £34m even as it battles an Ebola outbreak.

Burnham's inheritance

Burnham became prime minister on 20 July, installing Ed Miliband as foreign secretary and, with him, stewardship of the aid budget. Neither has moved to reverse the trajectory set by his predecessor, and Bond is now pressing the new government to use the autumn spending review to do so. Education spending fell 34% in the same period, water and sanitation 19%, and humanitarian aid 9% — even as administrative costs at the FCDO rose 11% to £790m. Sudan, Ukraine and Palestine were shielded by explicit government directive, which only sharpens the contrast for countries without a political ring-fence.

Adrian Lovett of the ONE Campaign, which has tracked the cuts since Starmer's announcement, put it bluntly: "Slashing bilateral aid to Africa, where need is greatest, will have a devastating impact. These choices will leave millions without access to basic healthcare, education and urgent humanitarian support, and risk a resurgence of deadly diseases we've spent decades trying to fight."

Why the shape matters more than the size

For markets, a shrinking aid budget is a rounding error against gilt issuance or the defence settlement it is funding. But the shape of the cuts is the more telling signal: a government facing fiscal constraints chose to protect middle-income partnerships — often more commercially and diplomatically useful — while letting support for the most fragile states erode fastest. That is a recalibration of what British aid is for, not simply a smaller version of what it used to be.

The next test comes with the autumn budget, when the Treasury will confirm whether the 0.3% floor holds for 2027 as scheduled, or whether Burnham finds room to soften it. Bond and other charities are lobbying hard for the latter; so far, nothing in the government's public statements suggests a change of course.
