Macron warns of intensifying Russian hybrid attacks; UK opposition presses Burnham for urgency

French President Emmanuel Macron, who warned this week that Russian hybrid attacks against Europe are intensifying and ordered infrastructure protections. — Nebojša Tejić / STA, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Macron ordered infrastructure protections after warning Russian hybrid attacks are intensifying; UK opposition says Burnham isn't matching that urgency.

Emmanuel Macron does not usually summon France's opposition leaders into the Élysée Palace's crisis room. He did it on Thursday, flanking himself with the heads of the DGSE and DGSI — France's foreign and domestic intelligence services — to deliver a message that was less about any single incident than about trajectory: the Russian hybrid threat, he said, "has intensified" over recent weeks, and it will not go unanswered.

"In recent weeks, the threat — particularly the Russian hybrid threat — facing Europeans and France has intensified."

What Macron actually said

Stripped of diplomatic hedging, the announcement was concrete enough to matter. Macron has ordered his government to draw up a plan protecting critical infrastructure and defence-industry sites against drone incursions and cyberattacks. He pointed to last month's drone attack on Leipzig airport, blamed on Russia, and to Polish premier Donald Tusk's warnings that Moscow may be preparing drone and missile strikes against NATO's eastern flank. The stated goal of the campaign, in Macron's telling, is not damage for its own sake but coercion: "to intimidate us and break our effort to support Ukraine." His response — "we are not intimidated" — was aimed as much at Warsaw and Vilnius as at Moscow.

That matters for how the story should be read. This is not a vague gesture at rising tension; it is a head of state converting an intelligence briefing into an infrastructure-hardening order, in a country that hosts significant NATO logistics and defence manufacturing. The market-relevant question is whether this marks a step change in the tempo of incidents against Europe, or whether France is simply the latest capital to say publicly what allied intelligence services have believed privately for a year. Danish and Polish leaders have used similar language since late 2025; Lithuania has already scrambled jets against drones crossing from Belarus. Macron's contribution is scale and specificity, not novelty.

The Burnham gap

Across the Channel, the same warning has produced a domestic argument rather than a policy announcement. Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch used Macron's press conference as a cudgel against Prime Minister Andy Burnham, arguing the government isn't matching the "growing urgency" shown by NATO allies and noting that Britain's own intelligence assessment reportedly puts a possible attack on NATO territory as soon as 2030 — with smaller incursions, in her telling, already under way. Reform UK's Nigel Farage went further, urging Burnham to copy Macron directly by briefing opposition parties on the intelligence behind the drone threat: "Given how seriously the French Government is taking Russian drone threats, it is fair to ask, are we doing enough?"

No. 10 has not published a comparable infrastructure plan, nor confirmed the cross-party intelligence briefing Farage is asking for. That silence is doing more political work than a rebuttal would. Badenoch and Farage rarely find common cause on anything; here, two opposition leaders with almost nothing else in common are making the identical demand, which suggests the gap between rhetoric and visible action in Westminster is wide enough to unite critics who agree on little else.

What doesn't reprice yet

For markets, the honest answer is: not much, not yet. European equities and energy futures have shrugged off a year of hybrid-warfare headlines because the incidents — drone sightings, undersea cable damage, cyber intrusions — have so far stayed below the threshold of sustained physical disruption. Macron's announcement doesn't change that threshold; it signals that a G7 government now believes the pattern is worsening enough to warrant emergency intelligence briefings and infrastructure hardening, which is a different thing from a confirmed attack on gas pipelines or power grids. Defence contractors and infrastructure-security names have inched up on this kind of news before without it holding; the asymmetry is that a single successful strike on European energy or grid infrastructure would reprice risk premia fast, while a dozen warnings like this one tend to fade from screens within a week.

The next real catalyst isn't another speech — it's whether France's infrastructure-protection plan produces anything visible (new security spending, a specific incident attributed publicly to Russian state actors) and whether Westminster answers Farage and Badenoch with either a briefing or continued silence. Both would tell traders more about how seriously Europe's governments now rate this risk than Thursday's warning did on its own.
