Trump nominates SEC veteran Jay Clayton as director of national intelligence

Jay Clayton, pictured as SEC chairman, is now nominated to lead the Office of the Director of National Intelligence. — U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission / Public domain
In one sentence
Trump taps former SEC chair Jay Clayton to lead national intelligence, replacing the controversial acting appointee Bill Pulte and breaking a FISA stalemate.

President Donald Trump has nominated Jay Clayton — the former chair of the Securities and Exchange Commission who currently serves as U.S. attorney for the Southern District of New York — to lead the Office of the Director of National Intelligence. The announcement, made Thursday, comes after a week of bipartisan uproar over Trump's earlier decision to install Bill Pulte, the director of the Federal Housing Finance Agency, as acting intelligence chief.

Pulte had no intelligence or national security experience. More pointedly, he had used his FHFA position to dig up mortgage records against figures the White House regards as adversaries — including a Federal Reserve board member and a sitting senator. That conduct drove Democrats to a hardball tactic: blocking reauthorisation of Section 702 of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act, the legal basis for key signals-intelligence collection against foreign targets. The authority was set to lapse for the first time since its creation, and without Democratic votes, congressional leaders could not move a short-term extension. Clayton's nomination reads, in part, as a direct response to that standoff.

A Financial Regulator Heads for Langley

Clayton's path to intelligence oversight is a circuitous one. He spent more than two decades at Sullivan & Cromwell, advising corporations on mergers, securities offerings, and regulatory proceedings — navigating deals including Barclays' acquisition of Lehman Brothers during the 2008 crisis. Trump tapped him to chair the SEC in 2017; he served until the end of 2020. After a brief return to private life, Trump brought him back in April 2025 as acting U.S. attorney in Manhattan, a position he has held on an indefinite basis since judges formalised the appointment that August.

His credentials are formidable in finance and law, modest in the traditional intelligence sense. The law that governs the DNI role calls for "extensive" national security experience. Clayton does not have it — at least not in the way the statute envisages. What he does have is credibility among lawmakers on both sides of the aisle. Senator Mark Warner said he had "great respect" for Clayton; Representative Jim Himes, the ranking Democrat on the House Intelligence Committee, called his "intelligence, temperament and deep commitment to public service" fitting for the role. The Senate Intelligence Committee scheduled a confirmation hearing for June 17.

For market observers, the nomination's sharpest edge is what it reveals about personnel priorities in Trump's second term. Clayton's move to DNI vacates the SDNY's top post — a perch with direct implications for financial fraud and securities enforcement in New York. Separately, the SEC is itself in an unsettled moment: Paul Atkins, confirmed as chairman in April 2025 to complete Gary Gensler's term, technically reached the end of that term on June 5. Whether Trump renominates Atkins or reaches elsewhere is unresolved.

The FISA question is the more immediate clock. Even with Clayton's nomination on the table, Section 702 authority remains expired for now — its renewal still requires votes, and Democrats have indicated they want confirmation, not just a nomination, before they move. That vote is the first real test of how far Clayton's bipartisan goodwill extends.
