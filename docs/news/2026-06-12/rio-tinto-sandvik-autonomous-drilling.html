Rio Tinto and Sandvik move toward autonomous surface drilling in new integration pact

A surface drill rig in Western Australia's Pilbara region — the terrain where Rio Tinto's autonomous drilling operations have run since 2008 and where Sandvik field trials are planned. — Calistemon / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Rio Tinto and Sandvik are jointly developing autonomous surface drilling integration, but the June 1 announcement covers testing phases, not a fleet deployment or investment commitment.

Rio Tinto has operated autonomous drill rigs in Western Australia's Pilbara region since 2008. On June 1, it announced a collaboration with Swedish equipment maker Sandvik to extend that track record to a new hardware platform — Sandvik's i-series surface blasthole rigs — but the terms of the deal reveal little about when, or whether, any of this reaches full deployment.

In one sentence: Rio Tinto and Sandvik are jointly developing autonomous surface drilling, but the announcement covers integration testing, not a fleet order.

The agreement pairs Sandvik's AutoMine® automation software with Rio Tinto's proprietary Autonomous Drilling System, known internally as ADS™. The stated objective is multi-rig, multi-site remote operation of Sandvik hardware managed through Rio Tinto's Perth Operations Centre — the same facility that already monitors haul trucks, production drills and autonomous trains from more than 1,500 kilometres away.

What the announcement does not include: a fleet purchase order, a disclosed investment figure, a deployment timeline, or a count of rigs involved. The first step is development and testing at Sandvik's test facility in Finland. Site-based trials at Rio Tinto's Western Australian operations follow, with performance assessed against specific production targets. That sequence describes early-stage integration work — the kind that typically precedes a commercial contract, not the contract itself.

A fleet built over nearly two decades

Rio Tinto first trialled the ADS at its West Angelas mine in 2008 and expanded to 20 autonomous drills across two Pilbara sites by the end of 2018. By mid-2026, it operates 40 autonomous drills across seven mine sites. Its most technologically advanced operation, Gudai-Darri — opened in 2022 at a cost of $2.6 billion — runs three Caterpillar MD6310 autonomous production drills alongside 23 autonomous haul trucks, all monitored remotely from Perth.

The Sandvik pact is notable in part because Gudai-Darri's drilling automation was built around Caterpillar equipment. Connecting a different vendor's rigs to the ADS requires non-trivial integration — software bridging between Sandvik's Intelligent Control System Architecture and Rio Tinto's own control stack, hardware-level compatibility testing, and validation that multi-rig autonomous behaviour holds up in demanding Pilbara conditions. That is the work the Finland testing phase is meant to begin.

The case for it is clear enough. Autonomous drilling's productivity advantage has been well-documented in Rio Tinto's own estate: the company's Iron Ore Company of Canada trial of an autonomous electric blasthole drill in Labrador — its first deployment in a subarctic climate — recorded a 20 per cent improvement in average penetration rate compared to manually operated rigs during the first quarter of 2026. A single operator can monitor and control up to eight rigs from a centralised console, reducing the exposure of workers to blast zones and cutting the dead time that comes with shift changes.

Sandvik's president of surface drilling, Petri Virrankoski, framed the rationale in terms of interoperability: "This joint development reflects Sandvik's commitment to improving safety and productivity through open, interoperable automation. Building on AutoMine and our i-series platform, we are proud to extend our long-standing relationship with Rio Tinto and support the continued development of autonomous drilling for demanding Pilbara conditions."

No Rio Tinto executive statement accompanied the announcement — a notable omission for a deal being presented as a meaningful step forward. The absence of numbers, timelines and deployment specifics is consistent with a vendor collaboration in its early technical phase rather than an agreed-upon programme with committed capital. Whether the field trials produce a commercial result is the question this announcement leaves open.

The next indicator to watch for: performance results from Rio Tinto's Western Australian trial sites. If autonomous Sandvik rigs hit the production targets set for the field programme, a formal equipment order would be the logical follow-on. Until that announcement arrives, this remains a promising handshake.
