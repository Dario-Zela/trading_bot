Trump opens federally protected Pacific Ocean zones to commercial fishing

Waters of the Papahānaumokuākea Marine National Monument in the north-western Hawaiian Islands, now reopened to U.S. commercial fishing fleets under Thursday's presidential proclamation. — NOAA Photo Library / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Trump signed a proclamation reopening ~500,000 sq miles across three Pacific marine monuments to commercial fishing, reversing Bush- and Obama-era protections.

A presidential proclamation signed at the White House on Thursday removes commercial fishing restrictions from roughly half a million square miles of protected Pacific Ocean waters, rolling back conservation measures that have been in place across three marine national monuments for as long as two decades.

The proclamation affects the Papahānaumokuākea Marine National Monument north-west of Hawaii — the largest of the three, spanning waters first protected by George W. Bush in 2006 and dramatically expanded by Barack Obama in 2016 — as well as the Mariana Trench Marine National Monument near Guam and Rose Atoll Marine National Monument near American Samoa. Both of the latter were established under Bush in 2009. Together the three cover nearly 500,000 square miles. Only U.S.-flagged vessels may fish commercially in the reopened zones; some restrictions within 50 nautical miles of certain Hawaiian islands and 12 nautical miles of Rose Atoll remain in place.

Speaking at the signing, Trump framed the closures as an economic wound. "With today's action we are officially reopening nearly half a million square miles of water around the northwestern Hawaiian islands," he said. "It ruined a big industry, by the way." Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick called it "restoring U.S. seafood competitiveness," arguing that fishing restrictions forced American fleets into international waters where they compete against less-regulated foreign operations.

Science against the current

Conservation groups dispute that framing on both ecological and economic grounds. Earthjustice attorney David Henkin argued that "commercial fishing in our protected marine monuments would not only be disastrous for the environment, but also does nothing for the fishing industry" — noting that U.S. fleets already reach annual tuna catch limits without accessing monument waters. Oceana's Ben Enticknap put the scientific case more directly: the closed zones function as breeding and feeding grounds that replenish adjacent fisheries. Since Papahānaumokuākea's fishing ban was expanded in 2016, tuna abundance in surrounding waters has measurably increased, a trend researchers cite as the spillover effect that monument protections are designed to produce.

The monuments shelter ecosystems with no equivalent elsewhere — hydrothermal vents, ancient coral, endangered Hawaiian monk seals, and thousands of species found nowhere else on Earth. The Center for Biological Diversity called Thursday's action "a reckless attack on the world's greatest ocean sanctuaries."

Thursday's proclamation is the latest in a series: Trump reopened Atlantic protected fishing areas in February 2026 and targeted the Pacific Islands Heritage Marine National Monument in April 2025, only for a federal court to strike down that order in August 2025 on legal grounds. Whether Thursday's broader action will face a similar challenge — and whether that earlier ruling offers a template for litigation — is the question conservation attorneys are now working through. The courts, not the administration, may have the last word.
