SpaceX IPO draws retail cash from chip stocks ahead of Friday debut

The Nasdaq MarketSite in Times Square, where SpaceX begins trading today under the ticker SPCX. — Wikimedia Commons / CC BY 4.0
In one sentence
Retail traders drained $70B-plus in chip-stock positions to fund SpaceX's $135 IPO debut — the largest in history — raising questions about what's lost on the sell side.

Three semiconductor names that led the AI-driven rally all spring got hit in the same window this week: Micron Technology fell 4.7% on Wednesday, Qualcomm shed 6.9%, and Broadcom dropped 5.1%. The proximate cause wasn't an earnings miss or a macro surprise. It was a rocket company.

SpaceX began trading on Nasdaq today under the ticker SPCX, priced at $135 a share — a fixed, take-it-or-leave-it figure set by Elon Musk rather than shaped through the conventional bookbuilding process. The offering raises $75 billion and implies a valuation of roughly $1.75 trillion, making it the largest IPO in stock market history. In the days before that debut, retail traders were liquidating positions in some of the most-owned names in their portfolios to get there.

The Drawdown in Chip Land

The data is unambiguous, if the interpretation is still contested. Vanda Research tracked non-professional traders selling individual stocks for three consecutive days through Wednesday — the first such streak since March 2020, when the pandemic triggered a different kind of forced liquidation. Monday alone generated what Vanda called the largest retail cash pull from single-name equities since November 2023.

"Evidence so far is that retail may be saving some dry powder for these upcoming IPOs," said Viraj Patel of Vanda Research. Greg Boutle of BNP Paribas was more pointed about the chip names specifically, describing Micron's drop as consistent with "selling flows in recent winners and levered products" being unwound to fund SpaceX participation.

Micron, Qualcomm, and Broadcom aren't random targets. They were the core holdings of the retail AI trade in the first half of 2026, their valuations built in part on the assumption that the semiconductor supercycle had further to run. Advanced Micro Devices and Super Micro Computer also showed weakness in the same window. The selling wasn't a market-wide flush; it was concentrated in the names retail had been building since January.

The concern, articulated by Douglas Beath at Wells Fargo, is about the residual effect: "Selling existing stocks, tech and non-tech, could lead to issues and volatility down the line through the rest of the year." He suggested the setup "could cause some indigestion," though he stopped short of predicting sustained declines. One structural backdrop amplifies the risk: U.S. household equity holdings now sit at 35% of total financial assets, an all-time high — meaning the marginal retail seller has more concentrated exposure to swing than at almost any point on record.

What SpaceX Is Selling — And What It Isn't

The $75 billion SpaceX attracted in retail orders didn't arrive on blind faith. The company did something structurally unusual: reserving up to 30% of its IPO for individual investors, compared with a sector norm of 5 to 10%. Fidelity lowered its account minimum to $2,000 to widen participation. Schwab, Robinhood, SoFi, and Morgan Stanley's E-Trade joined as distribution platforms. Prediction markets on Polymarket gave a 65% probability that SPCX closes above $2 trillion in market cap on its first day.

The enthusiasm runs ahead of the fundamentals, at least by conventional metrics. The combined SpaceX-xAI entity — merged in February via an all-stock deal that valued the combination at $1.25 trillion — posted $18.67 billion in revenue in 2025 against a net loss of $4.94 billion. That loss marks a sharp reversal from 2024, when SpaceX on a standalone basis earned roughly $791 million. The culprit is xAI, which contributed $3.2 billion in revenue while burning approximately $14 billion in cash — consuming, by itself, more cash than the rest of SpaceX's operations generate. Starlink's economics remain strong, with adjusted EBITDA up 86% and subscriber counts doubled in 2025, but those numbers are now buried under a $14 billion AI infrastructure commitment.

Retail buyers are, in a real sense, exchanging proven earners in Micron and Broadcom for a bet on whether Starship, Starlink, and Grok can eventually justify a $1.75 trillion entry price. That isn't necessarily wrong — it is, however, a different risk profile from the one they held last Tuesday.

The ETF Wave Arrives with a Warning

Brokerages aren't the only vehicles being positioned around SPCX. At least 25 ETFs tied to SpaceX have registered with the SEC ahead of the listing, with roughly a dozen structured as 2x leveraged products — both bull and bear versions. Defiance's SPCU, Tradr's SPCM and SPCG, and Leverage Shares' SPCH and SSPC are among those either launched or imminent.

Jared Blikre, markets editor at Yahoo Finance, flagged the standard risk in plain terms: "If you're looking for one of those 2X up or 2X bear products, you've got to be careful because these are meant for short-term investors." Daily resets, compounding decay on volatile underlyings, and the mechanics of synthetic leverage mean the ETFs can diverge substantially from the underlying stock's performance over any window longer than a single session. For retail buyers who held chipmakers for months and are now redirecting those proceeds, the leveraged wrapper introduces an entirely new layer of risk on top of an already concentrated IPO bet.

The broader question the week has raised is whether the rotation will persist past the IPO's first-day pop or reverse as enthusiasm normalizes. Chip stocks sold to fund SpaceX allocations don't automatically flow back if SPCX disappoints. And with OpenAI and Anthropic both expected to test public markets in the coming months, the same dynamic — retail liquidating existing tech positions to fund new listings — could repeat. The next catalyst to watch is SPCX's close today: if it finishes well above $135, expect the leveraged ETFs to open with significant inflows. If it struggles to hold its offer price, the chip stocks sold to get here may start looking like the better trade.
