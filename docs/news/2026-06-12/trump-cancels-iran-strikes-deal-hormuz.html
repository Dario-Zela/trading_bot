IRAN-HORMUZ · By C. Tanaka · 2026-06-12 · 5 min read

Trump cancels Iran strikes as deal text is approved; Hormuz blockade to lift by weekend

The Strait of Hormuz separating Iran from the Musandam Peninsula — effectively closed to commercial shipping since February 28, when US and Israeli strikes killed Supreme Leader Khamenei. — NASA/MODIS via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Trump cancels planned Iran strikes after deal text approved; Hormuz blockade stays in place until a signing — possibly this weekend in Europe with Vance — that Iran has yet to formally confirm.

For three and a half months, the Strait of Hormuz — the narrow passage through which roughly one-fifth of the world's seaborne oil trade normally flows — has been effectively closed to commercial shipping. The disruption began on February 28 when United States and Israeli strikes killed Iran's Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei; Tehran responded by closing the strait and mining sea lanes, triggering what the International Energy Agency has called the largest supply disruption in the history of the global oil market. On Thursday evening, something shifted.

President Donald Trump posted on Truth Social that he had cancelled scheduled strikes and bombings against Iran, citing discussions that had been "brought to the highest level of Iranian leadership and approved." Speaking to reporters at the White House, he went further: the Strait of Hormuz would reopen the moment a deal was signed, and that signing, he said, could come as soon as this weekend in Europe, with Vice President J.D. Vance in attendance. The naval blockade of Iranian ports, in place since April 13, would remain until the transaction was finalised.

The text that landed

The mechanism behind Trump's announcement is a memorandum of understanding whose draft has been shuttled between Washington and Tehran for weeks by Qatari mediators. Al Arabiya reported Thursday that a Qatari delegation had returned from Tehran carrying Iran's approval of the final draft — the most concrete diplomatic movement in the negotiations to date.

The MOU's terms, as described by officials from mediating countries and confirmed in broad outline by multiple outlets, are structured around a 60-day window. The Strait of Hormuz would reopen immediately, without tolls, with traffic expected to recover to pre-war volumes within 30 days. Iran would receive temporary sanctions waivers allowing it to resume selling oil for the duration. In exchange, the ceasefire would extend through Lebanon, and nuclear negotiations would begin — covering Iran's stockpile of roughly 440 kilograms of uranium enriched to 60 percent (well short of the 90 percent threshold for weapons-grade material) and a broader framework for its nuclear programme. Tehran would pledge not to pursue nuclear weapons; the harder question of enrichment itself would be deferred to a second, more detailed accord.

Iran's Fars News Agency — affiliated with the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps — signalled that, given US acceptance of Iran's proposed text, approval from the Iranian side was likely. Iran's Foreign Ministry told state television a different story: reports of a finalised agreement were "merely speculation," and no final decision had been reached. That gap between the mediating countries' read and Tehran's public posture is recognisable as late-stage negotiating behaviour, but it is a real gap nonetheless.

Parties not at the table

A discordant note came from Jerusalem. A senior Israeli official told Channel 12 that Israel had not been made aware of any finalised US–Iran agreement — appearing to contradict Trump's claim on Truth Social that parties including Israel, Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Qatar, Turkey, Pakistan, and others had all approved the deal in "both concept and great detail." Netanyahu's office subsequently framed their call with Trump more cautiously, describing a conversation about "the emerging memorandum of understanding with Iran regarding entry into negotiations" — language that stops well short of endorsement. Israel is not a signatory to any ceasefire and was not present at the negotiating table; its position matters because any resumption of Israeli strikes on Iran would almost certainly unravel whatever Washington and Tehran have agreed.

Trump simultaneously threatened to take Kharg Island — which handles roughly 90 percent of Iran's crude exports — if negotiations faltered, a warning that serves dual purpose: signalling resolve to Tehran and providing reassurance to Israel that Washington has not blinked.

What the market had already priced in

Oil's reaction on Thursday reveals a market that had anticipated de-escalation — but not yet in full. Brent crude settled at $89.15, down 4.2 percent; WTI fell 3.9 percent to $86.51. European natural gas futures dropped as much as 4 percent alongside the moves. Those are meaningful single-session declines, but they follow a substantial retreat that had already been under way: Brent touched $138 per barrel on April 7, its highest level in four years, before sliding as ceasefire signals accumulated through May and June.

The cumulative supply loss since Hormuz closed runs to some 880 million barrels. Around 600 tankers remain trapped inside the Persian Gulf; another 240 sit waiting on the ocean side. The strait's normal throughput is roughly 20 million barrels per day — the largest single maritime chokepoint in the global energy system. Saudi Aramco's chief executive, Amin Nasser, warned in May that oil market normalisation might not arrive until 2027 if the obstruction persisted.

Markets have been here before. Trump signalled on multiple occasions since March that a deal was days away, only for it to stall. Thursday's announcement carries more weight than those earlier statements — the Qatari delegation's return trip from Tehran is a concrete diplomatic movement, not a social media post — but the distance between "text agreed" and "deal signed" has repeatedly proven longer than anticipated. The 4 percent oil drop suggests traders believe Thursday was different; it also reflects roughly 35 percent of the price premium remaining, implying real scepticism about the weekend timeline.

The weekend as deadline

The specific catalyst to watch is a formal signing. If Vance boards a plane to Europe and a ceremony takes place on schedule, the Hormuz reopening becomes contractually obligated — and oil markets, which have retreated from $138 to the upper eighties over ten weeks, would face a more nuanced set of questions: how much further supply normalisation is already assumed in spot prices, how fast the 600 trapped tankers can clear, and whether the nuclear talks that open in the 60-day window can hold.

Iran's Supreme Leadership — whoever formally holds that authority after Khamenei's death — must still provide written final approval. That sign-off is the variable Trump cannot deliver unilaterally, regardless of what the Qatari delegation carried back from Tehran. If it arrives before Sunday, the machinery for the largest oil-supply restoration in decades is in place. If it does not, the naval blockade holds, and the oil market's tentative $50-per-barrel retreat from its crisis peak faces its own test.
