Boeing services unit hits record $33bn backlog as recurring revenue rebuilds

A Singapore Airlines Boeing 777-300ER — the carrier signed Boeing's largest-ever landing gear exchange deal in February 2026 — Wikimedia Commons / Konstantin von Wedelstaedt
In one sentence
Boeing Global Services posted $5.4bn in Q1 revenue, an 18% margin, and a record $33bn backlog — promising, but one quarter short of a re-rating.

Boeing's aftermarket division reported a record $33 billion backlog at the end of the first quarter, its highest ever, as the company continues pushing its services unit toward the front of its recovery story.

Boeing Global Services booked $8 billion in new orders in Q1 2026, pushing its backlog to a record $33 billion — a book-to-bill ratio of 1.6x that signals demand running well ahead of current output.

Revenue for the segment reached $5.4 billion in Q1 2026, a 6% rise on the prior-year period. Strip out the impact of Boeing's recently divested Digital Aviation Solutions unit, and the organic growth rate comes closer to 13%. Operating earnings were $971 million at an 18.1% margin — down modestly from 18.6% a year earlier, a decline the company attributes directly to that divestiture rather than underlying deterioration.

For a company that spent much of 2024 working through a crippling machinists' strike and serial certification delays, a services business generating near-20% operating margins is a meaningful counterweight. Boeing's commercial airplane and defense segments have long dragged on the blended margin profile; Global Services does not.

Contracts and what they signal

The quarter's headline win — and a useful illustration of what the backlog actually represents — was the largest Landing Gear Exchange Program agreement in Boeing's history, signed with Singapore Airlines Group at February's Singapore Airshow. The deal covers more than 75 aircraft across Singapore Airlines' and low-cost subsidiary Scoot's combined 737 MAX and 787 fleets. Under exchange-program structures, Boeing supplies overhauled gear from managed inventory rather than waiting for each airline to pull its own units offline; the economics favor the carrier on downtime and favor Boeing on parts throughput and long-term contract lock-in.

William Ampofo, Boeing Global Services' senior vice president for parts and supply chain, described the logic simply: "This agreement helps deliver parts faster and closer to operations — reducing downtime and supporting consistent, reliable service."

The division also received initial FAA and EASA qualification for 777-9 flight-simulator training devices during the quarter, extending its footprint into the training segment as the widebody's entry into service draws nearer.

One quarter, not a trend line

The structural argument for services is straightforward: once an aircraft enters a customer's fleet, the manufacturer has a captive addressable market for maintenance, parts, training, and digital tools across a 25-30 year operational life. Airbus and RTX have made exactly this pivot, and their aftermarket businesses now carry meaningfully richer valuation multiples than the hardware side.

Boeing trades at roughly 1.6 times forward sales, against an industry average closer to 2.5 times. That discount reflects the company's broader baggage — unresolved certification timelines, a debt load that remains uncomfortable, and the ongoing question of whether production-rate recoveries on the 737 and 787 will hold. A single strong quarter in services does not resolve any of those overhangs.

CEO Kelly Ortberg, in the Q1 earnings release, framed the results as momentum rather than arrival: "We're building on our momentum with a strong start to the year and growing record-breaking backlog across our business."

What closes the valuation gap, if anything does, is sustained sequential growth in services revenue coupled with proof that the commercial programs can deliver at scale. The Q2 earnings call — due in late July — will be the next substantive read on whether the $33 billion backlog is translating into the cash flow traders need to see before re-rating the stock.
