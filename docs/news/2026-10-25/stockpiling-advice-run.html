Stockpiling advice prompts rush on tinned food, loo roll and power banks

In one sentence
Britain's three-day household supplies advice has drawn warnings of panic buying, but no sales data yet shows how big any rush on tinned food, loo roll or power banks is.

Britain's government has told households to keep three days of food and water in the cupboard. Retailers have warned the advice could prompt the very panic buying it says it wants to avoid. Reports of a rush on tinned food, loo roll and power banks have followed. The sources checked for this piece give no sales figures to show how large any rush is.

What the advice says

The guidance went up on a government website on Thursday 23 October. It asks households to hold three days of bottled water and non-perishable food, with nine litres of water per person cited in coverage of the checklist. Tinned meat, vegetables and dried fruit are named. Other items on the list are a torch and batteries, a battery or wind-up radio, a charged power bank, blankets, hand sanitiser, some cash and copies of important documents. Loo roll does not appear in the coverage of the official checklist that was reviewed.

The Security Minister, Dan Jarvis, set out the campaign in a written statement to Parliament. He said it brings Britain "into line with many of our European peers" that use a three-day message. Defence Secretary Wes Streeting had already said, on 14 October, that he wanted people to be "alert not alarmed". Reports say Andy Burnham, the Prime Minister, backed the guidance and cited storms, cyber risks and a recent major security flaw. Bloomberg reported that he had earlier delayed a campaign planned under Keir Starmer because it might seem too pessimistic. The sources reviewed carry no direct Burnham quote.

Calm or spooked?

The government has taken care over its tone. Ministers describe the steps as basic preparation, and officials say households need not make large purchases. The framing also puts power cuts, flooding and cyber attacks alongside the language of conflict and hostile states. Mr Streeting did refer to the latter, warning of disruption from "hostile state actors or terrorists".

Retailers are not reassured. Jim Bligh of the British Retail Consortium said: "Panic buying is quite easy to generate, particularly if you tell people to buy a particular type of product." His point is that a named shopping list, however modest, works as a signal. Power banks and tinned goods are on this one.

The precedent is March 2020, when loo roll, pasta and cleaning products sold out and the big supermarkets joined the BRC in urging shoppers to buy only what they needed. Grocery data from that spring showed sales up by more than half in a single week at some chains. Nothing comparable has been published for this week. No retailer statement or scanner-data release was found, and the claim of a visible run rests on the reported headline rather than figures.

What to watch

The test is in the weekly grocery data. Retail analysts' till figures for the week to 25 October, and statements from the large supermarkets on stock and any purchase limits, would show whether the run is real and whether it lasts beyond the first few days. The campaign is described as running for three years, with water and non-perishable food as its first stage. How officials respond if shelves empty will say a good deal about how far they can ask the public to prepare without unsettling it. Cornwall, where storms have cut power for some households, is the place where the advice is easiest to defend.
