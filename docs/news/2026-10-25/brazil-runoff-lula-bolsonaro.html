Brazil votes in runoff between Lula and Flávio Bolsonaro

In one sentence
Brazil votes today in a runoff between Lula and Flávio Bolsonaro, a test of whether the Bolsonaro movement can outlast its founder, with fiscal, Amazon and market stakes.

Brazilians vote today in a presidential runoff that pits the incumbent, Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, against Flávio Bolsonaro, a senator and the eldest son of the former president Jair Bolsonaro. The question for the country is who governs for the next four years. The question for the Brazilian right is whether the movement built around one man can pass to his family.

This article was prepared without access to live source checks. The candidate pairing and the date come from the newsroom's discovery brief and the electoral calendar. Polling figures are left out on purpose: reports describe a tight race, but no Datafolha number has been checked against the pollster's published release, so none is printed.

A runoff with a long shadow

Brazilian presidential elections go to a second round when no candidate clears 50% of valid votes in the first. The runoff falls on the last Sunday of October, which is today. Voting is compulsory for most adults, and the electronic ballot means results usually arrive within hours of polls closing in the evening.

Lula, 80, is seeking a fourth term in office. He governed from 2003 to 2010, was jailed in a corruption case that was later annulled, and returned to the presidency in 2023 after narrowly beating Jair Bolsonaro in the 2022 runoff. His case this time rests on his record in office, and his opponents' case rests on the cost of living, security and fatigue with an older generation of leaders.

The elder Bolsonaro is not on the ballot. Brazil's Supreme Federal Court convicted him in 2025 of leading a plot to overturn the 2022 result, and he faces a long prison sentence. Separately, the electoral court had already barred him from running for office for years. His son has therefore carried the family brand, and the contest is partly a referendum on whether that brand works without its founder.

What the family is testing

Populist movements often struggle with succession. They are built on the personality of a single figure, and the loyalty they command does not always transfer. Flávio Bolsonaro, a senator for Rio de Janeiro, has the name and the inherited base. The open question is whether he can also win over the centre-right voters, business backers and evangelical communities who decide national elections, and who may have supported his father for different reasons.

The evidence for or against will be in the detail of the count. A narrow Bolsonaro defeat would suggest the movement retains a durable minority but lacks a route to a majority without its founder. A win would suggest the populist right has a successor, and would give it a platform from which to press for relief for its imprisoned leader. Both readings will be argued over by the end of the night; neither can be drawn before the votes are counted.

Why markets and capitals are watching

Brazil is Latin America's largest economy, and the result sets the tone on three fronts.

  • Fiscal policy. Investors have long worried about the government's spending path and its fiscal rules. A Lula victory would probably bring continuity in social spending. A Bolsonaro win would be read as a turn toward fiscal restraint, though promises on the campaign trail and budgets once in office often diverge.
  • The Amazon. Deforestation fell sharply under Lula, and Brazil has placed itself at the centre of international climate diplomacy. Environmental groups and foreign governments will read a change of president as a signal on enforcement in the rainforest.
  • Foreign policy. Lula has pursued relations across blocs, including with China and the BRICS group. A Bolsonaro government would be expected to lean closer to the populist right abroad.

The real and Brazilian equities are likely to react as soon as the result is known, with the direction depending on which outcome traders had priced in. Markets are open on Monday.

What to watch next

The first marker is the electoral authority's count, with turnout and the margin in the large south-eastern states likely to settle the outcome. The second is the loser's response: Brazil's institutions were tested by the riots of January 2023, and a close result would raise the stakes of any concession or challenge. After that comes the transition, with the winner due to take office on 1 January 2027 and the first cabinet and finance-minister appointments setting the market's expectations.
