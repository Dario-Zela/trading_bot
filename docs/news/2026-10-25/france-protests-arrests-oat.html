Almost 1,000 arrested in France as living-cost protests continue

In one sentence
Almost 1,000 more arrests in France's cost-of-living protests come as the 10-year OAT yield tops 5% and its spread over Bunds hits the widest since 2011.

Nearly 1,000 people have been arrested in France after another day of mass demonstrations over inflation and the cost of living, according to BBC and AFP reports. The street unrest is now being read in the bond market. French borrowing costs have climbed to levels not seen in more than two decades, ahead of a presidential election next year.

The street

The latest arrests follow a much larger wave a week earlier. On 17 October, authorities counted about 550,000 marchers nationwide, including 110,000 in Paris; organisers claimed around 800,000. Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez later said more than 1,800 people had been arrested, with Paris police reporting 855 detentions in the capital region alone. He said more than 500 police, gendarmes and firefighters were injured. In Limoges, the 19th-century city hall was badly damaged by fire.

The government has chosen to emphasise the violence rather than the grievance. Nuñez and Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu have blamed "ultra-left" groups for the worst of it, and Lecornu has said the government will not be "intimidated", framing the unrest as a possible warm-up for the 2027 campaign. That framing may prove politically useful, but it does not answer the underlying demand, which is about prices, wages and conditions in public services, schools among them.

The market

The 10-year OAT yield crossed 5% in recent weeks, a level last seen in 2002, according to Trading Economics. It reported 5.05% on 22 October. The premium over German Bunds has been quoted at around 170 basis points, the widest since the euro-zone debt crisis of 2011. Early October coverage had put it nearer 150 basis points, so the gap has widened further as the protests have continued.

It would be too simple to say protesters have moved the market. Bond investors are reacting to a minority government struggling to pass its 2027 budget, uncertainty over whether any consolidation plan survives, and a presidential race in which candidates are already competing on spending cuts. Marine Le Pen, for instance, was expected to outline cuts of €25 billion a year. Higher yields across the euro area, with markets pricing a further ECB rate rise, add to the pressure. The protests matter because they raise the political price of the austerity that investors want to see. A government that cannot pass a tight budget, and cannot easily offer relief to angry households either, has little room to manoeuvre.

A Moody's review of France's rating was reported as due last Friday, with downgrade risk flagged. This article could not confirm its outcome.

What to watch

Three things will show whether unrest turns into fiscal risk. The first is whether the 2027 budget clears parliament, and in what form, since concessions to protesters would widen the deficit. The second is any rating action, which would test forced-selling and collateral rules for French paper. The third is whether the Bund spread stabilises or keeps widening if arrests and clashes continue into November. Without a visible easing on the streets or in the budget talks, the premium investors charge to hold French debt looks more likely to rise than fall.
