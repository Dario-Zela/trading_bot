Gunmen kill at least 10 villagers in northeast Nigeria

In one sentence
Gunmen on motorcycles raided Binnari village in Taraba state, killing at least 10 by police count and 20 by residents' count, and looting shops; no group is named.

Gunmen riding motorcycles attacked a village in Taraba state, Nigeria, on Friday, killing at least 10 people by the police count and at least 20 by the count of survivors. They looted shops before leaving. Amnesty International put the toll at 40, with more bodies found on Saturday. The BBC headline placed the attack in the northeast. Reports from Al Jazeera and AFP place Binnari in Taraba, a state usually described as central-eastern, so the geography is loose. The death toll is still in dispute.

What is known

The attack began shortly after Friday prayers in Binnari. A community leader, Malam Abubakar Saifullahi, said about 50 motorcycles carried two men each. Residents scattered.

"People were running in different directions to escape the attack. Nobody knew where it was safe to go," resident Isiyaku Musa told AFP. Another resident, Sa'idu Nuhu, said: "After the attackers left, we counted more than 20 bodies, and some people were still missing."

Police confirmed 10 bodies recovered as of Friday evening and said the attackers looted food from shops. Saifullahi said they also took cash, foodstuffs and other provisions, and seized motorcycles that fleeing residents had abandoned. Counts of the dead in rural attacks usually rise as people return and search the bush, and the gap between 10 and 40 fits that pattern. Which figure is closest will depend on later police and Amnesty tallies.

No group has claimed the attack, and none of the reports I found names a perpetrator.

A familiar pattern, with caveats

The raid resembles the banditry that has spread across Nigeria's northwest and centre. Armed gangs there raid villages, kidnap for ransom and impose "taxes" on farmers and miners, often in remote areas where police and military are stretched thin. Taraba faces this kind of non-ideological gang violence. None of the reporting I found attributes Friday's attack to the bandits. Large motorcycle-borne raids and looting of food are consistent with their methods but do not establish who was responsible.

It is also separate from the jihadist insurgency in the far northeast. Boko Haram began its uprising in 2009 and remains active in Borno, Yobe and Adamawa. Earlier this month, suspected Boko Haram fighters killed at least 15 farmers and firewood gatherers near Marte in Borno, according to the Washington Post. Placing Binnari under a "northeast" label risks blending two different security problems with different perpetrators and different remedies.

Response

The official response reported so far is thin. The military said on Saturday that it killed 15 "terrorists" in an air strike in Borno state. That is a different state, and nothing in the reports links it to Binnari.

Amnesty International Nigeria was blunt. "Consistent failure of the authorities to bring the suspected perpetrators to justice is clearly emboldening impunity," it said, adding that authorities "continue to leave rural communities in some parts of the north at the mercy of gunmen."

What to watch

The first test is whether the police and army give a fuller account of the toll and name a suspected group. After that comes whether anyone is arrested, which is the point Amnesty is pressing. The pattern of the past year, with attacks of this size recurring and few prosecutions announced, suggests a low bar for continuity and a high one for change.
