Rosebank approval becomes a test of climate pledges against $117 oil

In one sentence
Rosebank is not approved: with Brent near $117, Energy Secretary Miatta Fahnbulleh is reportedly leaning towards refusal, testing whether the Scope 3 process holds against energy-security pressure.

Rosebank has not been approved. The largest undeveloped oilfield in UK waters is awaiting a fresh consent decision, and the reporting points the other way: Energy Secretary Miatta Fahnbulleh is said to be minded to refuse it. The test the field poses is still real. Brent crude has climbed to around $117 a barrel, and the question is whether a government that has pledged to reach net zero by 2050 will let that price override its own legal process.

Where the decision stands

Rosebank lies in about 1,100 metres of water west of Shetland. Equinor operates it, with Ithaca Energy among its partners. Equinor has put lifetime output at roughly 500 million barrels of oil equivalent.

In January 2025 the Court of Session in Edinburgh ruled the original consent unlawful. The judge held that the environmental assessment had to cover the climate impact of burning the extracted oil, the so-called Scope 3 emissions. That ruling followed a challenge brought by Greenpeace and Uplift. Drilling-related work could continue during the reassessment, but no oil can be produced without new approval.

Equinor resubmitted its environmental statement in October 2025, this time including downstream emissions, and a public consultation followed. The decision now rests with Ms Fahnbulleh. The Department for Energy Security and Net Zero has called reports of a likely refusal "speculation", and no ruling has been announced. One report says a decision is expected before the end of 2026. That timing comes from a single source and should be treated as indicative.

Jackdaw as the comparison

The clearest signal so far came on Wednesday, when the government approved Jackdaw, a smaller gas field that was quashed in the same January 2025 ruling. Ms Fahnbulleh drew the distinction herself: "One is a gasfield that caters for the domestic market, while the other is an oilfield that is mainly for export." The government says Jackdaw will support gas supply for up to 11 years and add about £2.9bn to the economy, though the minister told LBC it will not lower energy bills. Officials note Rosebank's emissions would be around seven times Jackdaw's.

That reasoning matters because it shows the decision is being framed as a case-by-case test rather than a general stance on North Sea output. Ms Fahnbulleh has said each application is judged on its merits. Prime Minister Andy Burnham has backed a "pragmatic" approach to the North Sea while keeping the 2050 target.

What $117 oil changes, and what it doesn't

Brent touched about $117.4 on 23 October, according to market reports, its highest since the spring. The move is attributed to Middle East tension: renewed Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia and Washington's deliberations over further strikes on Iran. The price series comes from secondary sources, and some earlier reports from this autumn showed Brent near $100 or below, so the level is volatile.

The pressure to approve is open. Donald Trump has urged Britain to "go FULL BLAST" on North Sea drilling. Kemi Badenoch, the Conservative leader, says Labour has "lost the confidence to make tough decisions" and wants the ban on new oil and gas licences repealed. Energy-security arguments are at their strongest when the price is high.

The economic case is weaker than the rhetoric suggests, however. Rosebank would not produce for years, so it cannot ease a price spike now. Its oil would be sold on the international market, and Ms Fahnbulleh's own export argument cuts against treating it as domestic security. A well-sited field also depends on prices years ahead rather than today's spike. Nothing in the reporting so far suggests the Scope 3 assessment, the legal requirement that produced this reassessment, has been set aside.

What to watch

The decision itself is the catalyst. Approval would suggest high prices are winning out in individual consents, with implications for other North Sea projects awaiting a ruling. Refusal, alongside Jackdaw's approval, would suggest the government is separating gas for domestic supply from export-oriented oil, and that the Scope 3 test is doing real work. Either outcome is likely to be challenged in court, and Labour MPs have warned of a backlash if Rosebank goes ahead.
