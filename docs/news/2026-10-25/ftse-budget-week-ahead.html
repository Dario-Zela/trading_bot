FTSE 100 week ahead: oil majors supported, banks and housebuilders exposed to gilts

In one sentence
Oil majors enter Budget week cushioned by high energy prices, while UK banks and housebuilders carry the gilt-yield risk that may matter more than the Budget itself.

UK large caps go into Budget week with a split already visible in the sector tape. Oil majors have the energy price behind them. Domestic banks and housebuilders are tied to the path of gilt yields. The Budget itself may matter less to that split than the bond market's reaction to it.

The caveat is that the size of the moves is not established here. Market-report and wire figures for the energy move and for gilt yields could not be retrieved, so what follows describes direction and mechanism, not levels.

Oil: a cushion, not a catalyst

The FTSE 100 is unusually weighted to energy, and the producers earn in dollars. A higher crude price lifts their earnings whatever happens to sterling or to Westminster's fiscal arithmetic. This edition's market coverage puts Brent-type pricing at about $117 a barrel (see Oil at $117 and AI-valuation doubts pull US indices lower). At that level the large producers have a buffer against a bad week elsewhere in the index.

The buffer is not a forecast of further gains. An energy price already elevated is a hedge for the index, and it is also the thing that keeps inflation, and therefore gilt yields, under upward pressure.

Banks and housebuilders: the rate channel

The domestic names are the other side of the same trade. Housebuilders are priced off mortgage affordability, which follows longer-dated gilt yields more closely than it follows Bank Rate. The Bank of England held at 3.75% this month, and a gilt sell-off has since pushed UK house prices into annual decline (see Bank Rate holds at 3.75%). A builder's order book is only as healthy as the mortgage rate a buyer can get.

Banks are more ambiguous. Higher yields can widen lending margins, but they also raise the risk of arrears and weaken the housing collateral behind mortgage books. A fiscal event that pushes yields up sharply tends to help banks less than it hurts housebuilders. Both groups are therefore exposed to the same variable, from different directions.

That variable is already under strain. Gilt yields have eaten into the Chancellor's room for manoeuvre days before the Budget (see Gilt yields squeeze Healey's headroom). Less headroom means less scope for giveaways, and it raises the risk that any hint of looser borrowing is read badly by bond buyers.

The lobbying sideshow

Banks are also trying to influence the Budget directly. The Guardian reports that figures circulated by lenders ahead of it present London as the most-taxed city, and that the method behind the claim is being challenged as cherrypicked. The dispute is about how the comparison was built, not about whether London's tax burden is high. The specific figures and comparison set have not been checked here, and they should not be treated as established.

The lobbying matters to the market because it shows where the sector thinks the risk lies. A bank lobbying against new levies is a bank that expects the fiscal arithmetic to push towards them.

What to watch

The Budget day move in the 10-year gilt yield is the test. A rise would be the first confirmation that the rate channel is dominating, and would put housebuilders under the most pressure. A calm bond market would leave the oil-supported index looking better than its domestic constituents suggest. The next data point after that is the market's reading of the Office for Budget Responsibility's forecasts, which set the headroom the gilt market is already questioning.
