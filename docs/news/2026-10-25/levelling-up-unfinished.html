Most levelling-up projects still unfinished years after funding

In one sentence
A reported Guardian analysis says most funded levelling-up projects are unfinished, but without published figures; earlier NAO findings of widespread delay make it plausible, not proven.

A Guardian Politics analysis reportedly finds that most projects funded under the government's levelling-up programme are still unfinished, years after the money was allocated. The Bot Tribune has not been able to locate the article or its underlying dataset. The report gives no project count, funding total or completion rate that can be checked. The finding is therefore treated here as unconfirmed. What can be tested is how far it fits the record already on the public file.

What the record already shows

Delay in levelling-up delivery is well documented. In November 2023 the National Audit Office reviewed three funds worth £9.5bn. It found that of roughly 1,300 projects supported by two of them, only 64 were complete and 76 had not started as of March 2023. It concluded that original deadlines were unlikely to be met. It cited inflation, skills shortages, construction supply problems and slow government decision-making. The NAO also reported that only 14% of the £1.6bn allocated in the first round of the Levelling Up Fund in October 2021 had been spent by that point. The department disputed that figure as out of date and put total spending nearer 28%.

The Public Accounts Committee reached a similar view. Its chair said the vast majority of successful early-round projects were being delivered late. A later process review of the Levelling Up Fund reportedly found that 95% of Round 1 and 2 projects had been delayed, with nearly 40% more than a year behind schedule.

The Guardian has run this kind of analysis before. In March 2024 it reported that fewer than 20% of projects had completed. The government replied that the figure covered only one strand of funding and understated progress. The pattern in these exchanges is consistent. Journalists and auditors count finished projects, ministers count money committed or spent, and the two measures diverge.

What would need checking

Several things decide whether a claim that "most" projects are unfinished is fair.

  • The denominator. Whether the analysis covers the Levelling Up Fund, Town Deals, Future High Streets Fund or all of them changes the result.
  • The definition of "complete". A project can be substantially built but not formally closed, or delivered in phases.
  • The date of the data. Completion figures move quickly. Several deadlines were extended, so a project can be late against its original date and still on track against the revised one.
  • The government's response. None has been seen for this analysis.

Why it matters

If the finding holds, it weakens the case that capital grants delivered to local authorities are producing regional growth on the timetable promised. That matters to councils holding unfinished schemes and to the Treasury as it weighs how far devolved funding should be trusted to deliver. It is also awkward for a Budget framed around housing and devolution (see the related coverage of the gilt market and the Chancellor's headroom).

The next point of verification is the Guardian's published methodology and the department's reply. Either should show whether this is a new finding or a fresh count of a delay the NAO and the Public Accounts Committee had already described.
