Ukraine: Kyiv says Starlink access is stable again

In one sentence
Kyiv says military satellite connectivity is restored after a disruption, but who imposed the limits is disputed and some restrictions may remain.

Ukraine's government says its military connectivity over the Starlink family of satellite services is stable again, after restrictions that disrupted front-line communications earlier this month. The restoration is welcome in Kyiv. It also leaves unanswered the question of who imposed the limits, and that question is why the episode matters beyond Ukraine.

The account below rests on Kyiv's statements, reporting by Al Jazeera, Meduza, Kyiv Post and others, and the BBC's report of the restoration. Some details are contested, and those are marked as such.

What happened

On Tuesday 13 October, Ukrainian defence officials said they had lost access to key satellite communications, disrupting military missions. The loss came after a Russian strike of more than 100 drones and five ballistic missiles that Kyiv said came with no warning, according to Al Jazeera's timeline.

By Wednesday, a Defence Ministry source said the connection had been restored. The ministry told Ukrinform that Starshield, the military-grade sister service to Starlink, had "resumed working on the move." Fedorov, a former defence minister, said service was returning at speeds of up to 160 km/h and that restrictions had not yet been fully lifted. He said removing the speed limits entirely was the next task. Kyiv Post's ministry sources did not say what had restored connectivity or whether any speed limit remained. The "stable" description therefore appears to cover mobile use, and it is not clear that every restriction is gone.

The speed limit points to the stakes. Serhiy Beskrestnov, a Ukrainian defence adviser, said a cap of roughly 100 mph (160 km/h) was placed on terminals. Anything moving faster, such as a drone or missile, would lose its guidance link. That claim comes from one adviser and has not been independently confirmed.

Who pulled the lever

The sources do not agree, and no party has accepted responsibility.

  • SpaceX: Beskrestnov attributes the cap to the company. Elon Musk denied involvement.
  • Washington: Musk said Starshield is "owned and controlled by the US government," with limits set by the US military and ultimately the president. Secretary of State Marco Rubio denied that the White House ordered a slowdown and said service was "back to where it was before." A senior US official told Axios the administration was not involved.
  • Kyiv: President Zelenskyy rejected a technical explanation. "It was not a technical problem; it was the will," he said. He told the Financial Times that US officials had warned Washington would block intelligence unless attacks on Russian energy infrastructure stopped. Ukrainian officials suspect a link to American pressure over strikes on Russian oil facilities. The Defence Ministry says it is in contact with partners to establish the causes.

The suspicion has context. Washington has pressed Kyiv to stop hitting Russian refineries, with President Trump tying the strikes to fuel prices before the November midterms. Kyiv has since resumed such strikes. Those facts show motive was available. They do not show who acted, and no party has been shown to have imposed the restriction.

Why a single network matters

Starlink, run by SpaceX, has about 11,000 low-orbit satellites and serves civilian users, including in remote areas. Starshield, launched in 2022, is the government-oriented version with stronger encryption. Ukraine uses it heavily to guide drones. Musk has said Starlink is for civilian or defensive use and that Starshield is not permitted for offensive operations. That makes the terms of use a lever, and one Ukraine does not hold.

There is a precedent for the lever being used. Last year Starlink cut off Russian troops who had been covertly using the service. The same switch that removes an adversary can also constrain a customer.

The dependency is the point. Whether the cap came from a company, from Washington or from a mix of the two, the decision sat outside Kyiv's control. A military whose drones and field communications ride on one privately operated network is exposed to that network's policies and to whoever can influence them. Other governments weighing reliance on commercial constellations have reason to read the episode closely.

What to watch

The immediate test is whether Fedorov's stated goal, removing the speed limits entirely, is met, and whether the Defence Ministry's inquiry names a party. A second test is whether Kyiv's continued strikes on Russian oil infrastructure bring a repeat. Until the cause is established, Ukraine's connectivity is restored but not secured.
