Gulf money reaches Manchester land and British kitchens

In one sentence
A Guardian report says Emirati-linked land ownership in Manchester rose 39% in a decade, but without base figures it is unclear how large a shift that is.

Land owned by Emirati-linked companies in Manchester has risen by 39% over ten years, according to a Guardian report. That is the kind of figure that sounds decisive and is hard to weigh without a denominator. The Guardian's headline gives the percentage but not the starting base, and no second outlet has reported the figure. It is best read as a claim to be tested, not a settled measurement.

What the number does and does not say

A 39% rise could mean a small holding became a slightly larger one, or that a dominant landowner got bigger. The summary available gives no hectares, parcel counts or valuations, and no breakdown of which firms are counted as "Emirati-linked" or how that link is defined. Land Registry title data and Companies House filings would settle most of this. Ownership chains that run through offshore holding vehicles can obscure the ultimate owner, which makes any "linked to" category a judgment call.

The wider record gives context but not confirmation. Abu Dhabi-linked capital has been in Manchester for well over a decade, most visibly through the City Football Group's owners and the east Manchester regeneration around the Etihad campus. A 2022 University of Sheffield report said nine sites were sold to the sheikh at a fraction of their value, on leases of 999 years, and noted that Jersey-domiciled subsidiaries made it impossible to know how much Abu Dhabi was making from the ventures. Manchester City Council rejects the criticism, saying it secured "the best overall deal" when investors were scarce after the 2007–08 financial crisis. A 2018 report by The Meteor also identified Emarati Holdings, a Guernsey-registered company with 102 land titles, as the fourth-largest overseas owner of titles in the city. That report said its owner was a Kuwait-registered investment group, which shows that "Gulf-linked" covers more than one state.

None of that verifies the 39% figure. It does show why the question matters. Regeneration depends on who holds the sites that plans assume will be built on.

Why ownership matters for regeneration

If the figures hold, the point is concentration, not foreign money as such. Councils negotiate with whoever controls the land, and a single large owner can set the pace, tenure mix and price of housing on a district. Long leases and offshore structures reduce what the public can see of the terms. Housing delivery in the city is already under pressure from higher borrowing costs, with the gilt sell-off feeding through to mortgage rates and house prices.

The kitchen side of the story

The companion thread is about people more than property. The Guardian also reports that Gulf states are luring top British chefs, with the pitch reportedly resting on no unions and low taxes. The detail of that claim could not be checked, and nothing found addresses union rights in Gulf hospitality. The cost pressure is easier to document. Jason Atherton has told Arabian Business that UK hospitality is as hard as it has ever been, and he is leaning towards expansion in Dubai, Saudi Arabia and Abu Dhabi. British chefs have been opening in Dubai for years, so the trend is not new. What would be new is evidence that cost pressure at home is speeding it up.

The two threads share a direction of travel. Capital and talent are both being drawn towards the Gulf, and Gulf capital is also arriving in British cities. They are different flows with different drivers, and the evidence does not yet show that they are connected.

What to watch

The test is the data. A breakdown of Manchester's land titles by ultimate beneficial owner, with hectares and a ten-year baseline, would show whether 39% is a large or a small change. The council's response, and any disclosure on the terms of long leases, would show whether the regeneration question has substance.
