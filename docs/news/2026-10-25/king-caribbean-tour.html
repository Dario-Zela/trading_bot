King's Caribbean tour tests monarchy amid reparations and republican pressure

In one sentence
The King's Caribbean tour, with no apology or reparations on offer, will show whether republican and reparations demands are becoming policy in the realms.

King Charles III sets out on Tuesday for a Caribbean tour that Buckingham Palace has already told the region will not include a formal apology for slavery. The visit takes in Antigua and Barbuda, The Bahamas and Guyana between 27 October and 4 November, according to published schedules. Some outlets list the stops in a different order. Antigua is also due to host the Commonwealth Heads of Government Meeting, which runs from 1 to 4 November. The Palace says the King will not shy away from "the darkest days of our past", but that the trip will mostly look to each host country's present and future.

The Caribbean is where the monarchy's colonial history and its Commonwealth future are argued most openly. The tour therefore tests a narrower question than whether the King is welcome. The question is whether republican and reparations pressure is turning into policy.

The pressure behind the itinerary

Barbados showed the direction of travel when it became a republic on 30 November 2021. The King, then Prince of Wales, attended the ceremony. Dame Sandra Mason became the island's first president.

Reparations are the second strand. The Caribbean Community (CARICOM) set up a Reparations Commission in 2013. Its 10-point plan for reparatory justice includes a formal apology and the cancellation of debt. Charles has said before that the "appalling atrocity of slavery" stains Britain's history. In March 2022, on a visit to Jamaica, his son William described slavery as "abhorrent" and expressed "deepest sorrow". Neither statement amounted to the apology campaigners want.

The Palace now says an apology is a matter for the government. The King acts on ministers' advice, and the UK government has said it will not pay financial reparations. That position keeps the monarchy out of a fight it cannot settle. It also leaves the demand with no one in London who will answer it.

What each stop tests

The three stops test different things. Antigua and Barbuda and The Bahamas are both realms in which Charles remains head of state. In The Bahamas, the National Reparations Committee has said the King should not arrive "empty-handed" and that "the time is now for the republic". Last month Jamaica handed Charles a petition seeking legal guidance on Britain's reparations obligations. Jamaica is not on this itinerary, but the petition suggests the campaign is now using legal channels as well as protest.

Guyana is a different case. It is already a republic, and the King will make a state visit there for the 60th anniversary of its independence from Britain. That is a courtesy to a Commonwealth member that chose a different constitutional path. It also shows the Commonwealth surviving the loss of the Crown as head of state.

The evidence so far points to a gap between rhetoric and policy. Campaigners are loud and the petitions are formal, but the reporting reviewed here shows no new republic referendum scheduled in either realm on the itinerary. That could change if the CHOGM discussions give reparations a formal platform.

What to watch

Two things will show whether the pressure is hardening. One is how Bahamian and Antiguan ministers receive the King. Warm welcomes with no mention of constitutional change would suggest the pressure is mostly rhetorical. Pointed speeches, or a commitment to consult voters, would suggest the opposite. The other is the CHOGM communiqué from 1 to 4 November, and whether it gives reparations language that goes beyond what London has accepted.
