Oil at $117 and AI-valuation doubts pull US indices lower despite strong earnings

In one sentence
US indices are reported lower as AI valuation doubts and very expensive crude meet strong earnings, but the $117 oil figure is unconfirmed and may be an April print.

US equities are being read this week as a story about costs and sentiment, not about company results. The framing circulating in market commentary is that crude oil at $117 a barrel and doubts about AI valuations are dragging the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq 100 lower, even as corporate earnings remain strong. The desk could not confirm every piece of that framing, and the gaps are part of the story.

What is established, and what is not

The starting claim is that the S&P 500 and Nasdaq 100 are both lower, with the Nasdaq 100 carrying the heavier burden of AI-related worry. The claim comes without index levels, percentage moves or a named benchmark for the oil price. Those are the numbers a market report needs, and none was available to verify against a primary data source.

The $117 figure needs particular caution. The only $117 reference the desk found is from late April, when Brent futures spiked above $126 on fears of escalating hostilities and then slipped back below $117 in volatile trade. By early October, reports had Brent settling near $100.60 and US crude near $89.44. Whether crude has returned to $117 in the past fortnight is not established here. It is also unclear which contract the figure refers to. Brent and US crude have been trading at a spread of roughly $10 a barrel.

The backdrop is not in dispute. The S&P 500 closed at 7,818.95 on 6 October and the Nasdaq Composite at a record 27,599.79, according to one account of that session. Any decline now is therefore from a high base. The S&P 500 was up more than 14% for the year at that point.

Earnings are not the problem

The premise that earnings are strong holds up. Goldman Sachs has pointed to year-on-year S&P 500 profit growth of about 30% in both the first and second quarters of 2026, and it argues that fears of an earnings bubble are misplaced. Its 12-month index target is about 8,700. For the third quarter, analysts surveyed by LSEG expected growth of about 30.6%, while another tally put it near 29.5%.

The brief names no individual reporters, beat rates or guidance figures, and the desk will not supply them without a source. The point stands without them. When profits are growing at that pace and the index still falls, the explanation has to lie in what investors are willing to pay for those profits, and in what energy and rates are doing to that price.

The valuation question

The concern about AI is less about whether the technology is earning money than about how concentrated the gains have been. Breadth has been narrow even on record days: on 6 October the Russell 2000 fell 0.6% while the Nasdaq hit its high. JPMorgan has warned that some of this year's strongest performers have been lifted by capital flows, which leaves them exposed to fast pullbacks. Ameriprise noted in July that nearly 60% of the stocks in the S&P 500 information technology index were down 20% or more from their recent highs, so the index headline has masked a weaker underlying picture for some time.

The second thread in the brief is a defensive rotation. A Benzinga item reportedly says the income-focused Schwab US Dividend Equity ETF (SCHD) is beating the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF (VOO) and the Invesco QQQ Trust (QQQ). The desk could not locate that article. It did find an Invezz analysis from 29 June describing SCHD as an "anti-AI" fund, with a year-to-date total return of about 19% against 15.2% for QQQ and 7.53% for VOO, and net inflows of over $12 billion that year. That is four months old, and it shows that the outperformance predates this week's moves. Evidence of a fresh rotation, in the form of current returns or flow data, was not found.

Why oil matters here

If crude is anywhere near $117, the mechanism is straightforward. Higher energy costs feed into inflation expectations, which push up the rates at which future earnings are discounted. Long-dated growth stocks, whose value rests on profits far in the future, are the most sensitive to that. A market priced for AI-led growth is therefore exposed twice: to the cost of energy for data centres and to the cost of capital. One report put the 10-year Treasury yield at 5.29–5.35%, but it is the only source for that figure and it is not relied on here.

What to watch

Three data points would settle the matter. The first is the actual Brent and WTI settlement prices, which would show whether $117 is current or a stale April print. The second is the next US inflation report and any Federal Reserve commentary on energy-driven price pressures. The third is the remaining large-cap technology earnings this season, where guidance on AI spending will test whether the valuation worries are a mood or a message. Until the oil price is pinned down, the claim that macro costs are driving the tape rests on the premise rather than on the evidence.
