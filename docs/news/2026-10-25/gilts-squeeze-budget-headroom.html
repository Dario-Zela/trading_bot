Gilt yields squeeze Healey's headroom days before a Budget built around housing and devolution

In one sentence
With 30-year gilts near 5.9% and the 10-year at an 18-year high, outside estimates put Healey's headroom at roughly £11-14bn, down from the OBR's £23.6bn, before the 28 October Budget.

The Chancellor, John Healey, goes into Wednesday's Budget with less room than the Treasury's own forecasts implied seven months ago. The reason is the gilt market. Long-dated government borrowing costs have risen sharply, and each rise makes the debt-interest bill larger and the margin against the fiscal rules smaller. The Office for Budget Responsibility (OBR) put that margin, known as headroom, at £23.6bn in March. Outside estimates now put the cushion at roughly £11bn to £14bn. That is not an official figure, and the OBR's pre-Budget forecast will settle it.

What the market has done

The 30-year gilt yield reached 5.89% on 1 September, its highest since March 1998. On one day that month the 10-year yield rose to 5.268%, its highest since June 2008. The triage reading for the 30-year is about 5.9%. This piece did not find a fresh late-October quote, so the levels above are the latest confirmed ones.

Headroom is the gap between the Chancellor's forecast borrowing and the limit set by her fiscal rules. The forecast depends on market interest rates. When yields climb, the OBR marks up the expected cost of servicing existing and new debt, and the gap narrows with no policy change at all.

Several forecasters have tried to size the effect:

  • EY puts headroom at about £11.3bn. It identifies gilt yields as the largest single drag, at around £10bn, and expects a limited Budget that raises at least £12bn.
  • Pantheon Macroeconomics estimates headroom at about £13bn.
  • Deutsche Bank's Sanjay Raja estimates £13.8bn if current yields were used in the calculations. He suggests the government would want to keep at least £10bn as a buffer to reassure markets.

The spread is narrow for work done independently, and all of it points the same way. The £10-12bn erosion in the triage summary sits inside that range. It remains an external estimate, not an OBR publication.

A Budget with housing and devolution in it

The Budget is due on 28 October. The Treasury has pre-briefed a £1bn line for social and affordable housing and a devolution package aimed at regional growth, according to BBC Politics and Guardian Politics.

The £1bn is small against the £10bn or so that yields have taken out of headroom. Set against a £23.6bn cushion, it would have been a modest, affordable gesture. Set against a cushion around half that size, every pound of new spending has to be matched by revenue elsewhere or by a smaller buffer. The devolution package matters for a similar reason: its fiscal cost depends on how much of it is new money and how much is existing budgets redirected, and the pre-briefing does not settle that.

Housing carries a second link to the gilt market. Mortgage pricing follows swap and gilt rates, so the same rise that squeezes the Treasury also squeezes the households the housing announcement is meant to help. The Bank of England's decision to hold Bank Rate at 3.75% has not insulated either from long-end moves, which are set by investors rather than by the Monetary Policy Committee.

The income tax pledge and what bends

The Chancellor is weighing four ways to raise money without raising income tax, according to BBC Politics. The options reported include other tax rises and changes to thresholds. The stated aim is to rebuild headroom while keeping the pledge intact.

The arithmetic explains why thresholds feature. Freezing income tax and national insurance thresholds raises revenue without a headline rate change, because wage growth pulls more income into higher bands. It is the least visible way to collect several billion pounds. It is also the route that most obviously lands on households the pledge was meant to protect, which is the tension in the choice.

Market commentators have been blunt about the scale. Handelsbanken said higher yields would "add to the likelihood that fresh tax increases will be announced on 28 October". City AM's coverage carries the headline "Large tax hikes on the way". The Chancellor can pre-empt this by showing a credible path. She cannot dictate how the market responds to it.

Who sets the room

Budget-setting has long assumed the Treasury and the OBR determine the available room, with markets as a background condition. This autumn that order has reversed. The forecast is taken from market rates in a window shortly before publication, so a bad week for gilts goes straight into the headroom number. The OBR has not said publicly what window it will use for this Budget.

The reverse risk is also real. A Budget read as too loose could push yields higher still, and the headroom figure with them. Commentators have invoked the 2022 gilt crisis as a warning. That comparison overstates the position, since yields have drifted up rather than gapped, but it explains why a buffer of at least £10bn is being discussed as a target.

What to watch

Three things will settle the picture. The first is the OBR's headroom figure, published with the Budget on 28 October, which will show whether outside estimates of £11-14bn were right. The second is how much of the housing and devolution packages is new money. The third is the gilt reaction on the day, particularly at the 30-year point, where the 5.9% level is the reference. If the market takes the Budget as credible, the headroom the OBR reports will be the room the Chancellor actually has. If it does not, the number will shrink again before the next forecast.
