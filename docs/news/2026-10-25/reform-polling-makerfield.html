Is Reform's poll lead eroding after Makerfield?

In one sentence
Reform's poll lead has already gone: averages put Labour about five points ahead and Reform level with the Conservatives near 22%, and Makerfield was a symptom rather than the cause.

The premise of the question needs adjusting. Reform UK is no longer clearly ahead in national polling, and the Makerfield by-election was less of a rout for the party than the framing suggests. The more useful question is how far Reform has slipped since Andy Burnham took over as Labour leader, and whether the slide is lasting.

What Makerfield did and did not show

The by-election was held on 18 June 2026 and Burnham, then Greater Manchester mayor, won it with 24,937 votes, or 54.8%. Reform's Robert Kenyon took 15,696 votes, or 34.5%, and Restore Britain's Rebecca Shepherd was third on 6.8%. Reports of the result put the swing from Reform to Labour at about 3.4 points. That is a rare swing towards a governing party, but it is a modest one.

By several accounts Reform's 15,696 votes was its highest total in any Westminster by-election, and its share was second only to its Runcorn and Helsby result in 2025. Whether it was the party's "worst night since the general election" depends on the measure. It lost, and it was beaten heavily. But it lost a seat that Burnham, a figure with a large personal following, was always favoured to win, and on an unusually high turnout of about 58.7%. One contest in a seat with that kind of candidate says little about the national mood.

What the national polls say

The polling average is a better guide, and it points to a three-way contest with Labour in front rather than a Reform lead:

  • YouGov, 19 October: Labour 27%, Conservatives 23%, Reform 22%.
  • Opinium, 9 October: Labour 29%, Reform 23%, Conservatives 20%.
  • PollCheck's seven-poll average, 22 October: Labour 27.7%, Conservatives 22.3%, Reform 21.9%.
  • BritPolls' October average: Labour 27.2%, Conservatives 22.4%, Reform 21.8%.

The YouGov and Opinium figures come second-hand, through an aggregator and a search summary, and should be checked against the pollsters' own tables. Some aggregator pages also label Reform as the leader in their headings while their own tables show Labour first, so those labels should be treated with caution.

The averages put Reform at about 22%, level with the Conservatives and some five to six points behind Labour. That is a long way from the position the party held through 2025, when it led most polls. On these numbers, the lead has not merely eroded. It has gone.

Cause and effect

The by-election did not cause that change. The polling shift appears to follow Keir Starmer's resignation and Burnham's arrival as Labour leader, with Makerfield a symptom rather than a trigger. An incoming leader often enjoys a honeymoon, and honeymoons fade. The current Labour lead of five to six points may prove temporary, and a fragmented right-of-centre vote can flatter the party in front.

The point matters beyond the polls. A Labour lead built on 27–29% is not a majority-winning position by itself. But with Reform and the Conservatives splitting a similar share of the vote between them, the arithmetic of a general election looks very different from the one Labour faced a year ago, and a more credible route to a majority opens up.

What to watch

The next test is whether Labour's lead holds once the Budget lands. Gilt yields are already squeezing the chancellor's headroom, and a difficult fiscal statement could end the honeymoon quickly. If Reform rebounds to the mid-20s in the polls that follow, Makerfield will look like a footnote. If it stays near 22%, the erosion is real.
