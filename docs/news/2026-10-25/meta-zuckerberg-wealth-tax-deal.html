Meta deal to help Zuckerberg fund a possible California billionaire tax raises governance questions

In one sentence
Meta has created non-voting shares Zuckerberg can sell to cover a possible California wealth tax, in exchange for an eight-year residency pledge, raising related-party questions.

Meta Platforms has given its founder a way to pay a wealth tax that California voters have not yet approved. It has done so without handing over cash, but not without cost to other shareholders. In filings published this week, the board created a new class of non-voting shares that Mark Zuckerberg may sell "for any reason", including to meet a California billionaire-tax bill. In return he has committed to remain a California resident for eight years.

The arrangement is narrower than the shorthand "Meta helps fund Zuckerberg's tax" suggests. The company is not writing a cheque. The governance question lies elsewhere: a board is using the company's capital structure to solve its controlling shareholder's personal tax problem.

What the filings say

The tax is Proposition 40, a one-time 5% levy on net worth above $1bn for people who were California residents on 1 January 2026. It goes to voters on 3 November. Forbes puts Zuckerberg's net worth above $220bn, which would imply a bill of more than $11bn, though no figure for his own liability has been published.

Per the AP's reading of the filings, the new shares represent 10% of Zuckerberg's overall economic stake, and he remains the majority owner. The reports do not give a share count or class designation. Meta says he receives no additional pay. He has drawn a $1 annual salary as chief executive since 2013.

The company's stated rationale is notable. It said that without the change he would effectively be "paying to serve as the Company's chief executive officer" while living in California. Chief financial officer Susan Li framed the new class differently, saying it would "expand our funding tools and give us flexibility to invest" in AI-driven growth.

Reports say Zuckerberg had told the board he was weighing a sale of his Palo Alto home and a move out of state if the measure passed. The residency pledge, which allows an exception for "material unforeseen circumstances", is what the board received in exchange.

Why shareholders may care

The arrangement raises three questions that the filings, as reported, do not fully answer.

  • Dilution and economics. A non-voting class that the founder can sell freely creates a liquid supply of stock. Whether it is issued at no cost to the founder, or in exchange for existing voting shares, determines who pays. The reports do not say.
  • Related-party process. The arrangement benefits a controlling holder. The reports do not describe whether independent directors or minority holders had a say, or how a fairness judgment was reached.
  • Precedent. The board is treating a personal tax exposure as a retention problem. Other founder-controlled companies in California may now face the same request.

The retention argument has some force: a chief executive who relocates is a real operating risk. But the same logic would justify nearly any personal concession to a founder who holds voting control, which is why it merits scrutiny rather than deference.

Politics and what comes next

The deal has drawn comment from both sides of the campaign. Governor Gavin Newsom is among opponents who warn the tax would drive wealthy residents out. The SEIU United Healthcare Workers West union argues the arrangement undercuts that claim, since the state's most prominent exit threat has been bought off. Opposition spending is heavy: the Building a Better California committee has raised more than $333m, about 30% from Sergey Brin, against $32.7m for the Yes on 40 campaign.

Two dates matter. The 3 November vote will determine whether the arrangement is ever used. If the measure fails, the new class remains a standing option for the founder to sell stock. Meta's next proxy statement and quarterly filing should then show the share count, the terms of issue, and any board committee approval.
