Bank Rate holds at 3.75% as gilt sell-off pushes UK house prices into annual decline

In one sentence
Bank Rate stands at 3.75%, but gilt yields near 5.4% and futures pricing of up to three hikes mean the debate is now about tightening; house prices fell in August, then flattened.

The Bank of England has not moved Bank Rate since its last decision, on 17 September, when it held at 3.75%. The argument around that rate has changed, though. Through the first half of the year the question was how soon the Bank could cut. Now gilt yields and rate futures are asking whether an oil-driven inflation shock forces it to raise rates. Housing is the first place the higher cost of money shows up. The Lloyds index recorded an annual fall in prices in August, but it has since flattened.

A hold with a hawkish edge

The Bank's own account of the September decision is cautious. It says higher global energy costs have had a limited effect on price and wage setting in the UK so far. Governor Andrew Bailey went further than that in his remarks: "the longer this volatility persists, the bigger the impact it will have on inflation." A prolonged period of high energy prices, he said, could make a rate rise more likely. Reports also name Sarah Breeden and Clare Lombardelli as members who have signalled growing support for a hike. No vote split is published on the Bank's decision page, so how many members actually favoured tightening in September is not established here.

Market pricing has moved well past the committee's public language. Trading Economics reported on 28 September that markets saw better than an 80% chance of a hike at the next meeting, on 5 November. By 23 October it described pricing for at least three 25-basis-point increases by April 2027, with the first possibly in November. These are secondary-source readings of rate pricing, not a direct pull from swaps data. They should be read as the direction and rough scale of the bet. Even so, three hikes would take Bank Rate from 3.75% to 4.5%, and a bet of that size is a substantial one. The oil driver is the disruption around the Strait of Hormuz and the unsettled US–Iran talks. Oil is trading at about $117, as covered in our markets report.

Gilts well above the policy rate

The 10-year gilt yield was above 5.4% on 28 September, a level not seen in almost two decades. It touched 5.53% on 8 October, the highest since July 2007, according to press and market reports. It was around 5.33% on 23 October, after finishing the previous day just under 5.4%. Against a Bank Rate of 3.75%, that is a spread of roughly 160 to 170 basis points. One report put the 30-year yield at 6% at the height of a renewed global bond sell-off, its first such print since 1998. That figure rests on a single source and has not been checked against Debt Management Office or Bank data.

A curve that sits this far above the policy rate says investors expect short rates to rise, or want more compensation for holding government debt, or both. The pattern fits the oil story, but the data cannot separate the two explanations. The practical effect is the same for the Treasury, which is why the sell-off is squeezing the chancellor's room before the Budget (gilts squeeze Budget headroom). It also matters for banks and housebuilders (FTSE week ahead).

Housing: a fall, then a pause

Lloyds Banking Group's index showed average UK prices down 0.4% year on year in August, to £298,468. That was the first annual decline since November 2023. Prices fell 0.2% on the month in the first estimate, later revised to 0.3%. Lloyds described a subdued market in which buyers and sellers were cautious and borrowing costs were elevated. Reuters tied some of that caution to higher borrowing costs after the US–Iran conflict.

The link from gilts to house prices is plausible. Fixed-rate mortgages are priced off swap rates, which move with the same expectations that drive gilts. But none of the coverage reviewed here sets the gilt move against the change in average mortgage rates over the same period, so the causal claim is an inference, not a measured result.

The September reading, published after the August fall, undercuts any suggestion of a spiral. Prices were unchanged on the year (0.0%) and flat on the month, at an average of £298,441. Lloyds reported that new buyer enquiries reached their highest since February. Nationwide's measure showed annual growth of 0.8%. The regional split is wide. Northern Ireland is up 7.4% on the year and Scotland 3.4%, while Greater London is down 2.2% and the South East 2.1%. In August Lloyds had expected prices to stay roughly flat for the rest of 2026. September fitted that forecast. A rate rise, or even sustained pricing of one, would test it.

What to watch

The next catalyst is the Monetary Policy Committee's 5 November decision. The vote split will show whether the committee has moved with the market or is holding out against it. Lloyds publishes its October index on 6 November, the day after, and it will be the first reading to capture the autumn run in swap rates. If the Bank holds again while futures price three hikes, the gap between the policy rate and the curve will have widened further. If it raises, mortgage lenders will reprice again, and the flat September figure will become harder to sustain.
