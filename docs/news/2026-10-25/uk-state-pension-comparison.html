How the UK state pension compares with other countries

In one sentence
The UK state pension ranks low internationally against earnings, yet the triple lock is a fast-rising cost, leaving the Budget caught between generosity and borrowing.

Britain's state pension is among the least generous in the rich world when measured against what people earn, yet it is also one of the fastest-growing items in the public accounts. That tension sits behind the argument over the triple lock, which is back in focus as the Budget approaches and gilt yields squeeze the Treasury's room for manoeuvre.

What the triple lock does

Each April the new state pension rises by the highest of three measures: CPI inflation, average earnings growth, or 2.5%. The full new state pension was £230.25 a week in 2025/26, about £11,973 a year. This April's uprating, which followed earnings growth, took it to roughly £241 a week. A full rate requires 35 qualifying years of National Insurance contributions, and at least 10 are needed for any payment.

The mechanism guarantees that pensions never lose ground to prices or wages, and in years when earnings outrun inflation they gain on both. Compounded over a decade, that ratchet is why the bill grows faster than the economy it is paid from.

Generous or stingy?

The answer depends on what is measured. Compared with other countries, the UK's headline flat-rate payment looks modest. The OECD's Pensions at a Glance 2025 report, as covered in the trade press, put the UK at the bottom of the club for the net replacement rate — pension income as a share of pre-retirement pay — from mandatory provision for average earners. One report cites a figure of 29% against an OECD average of 63%. Mark Pearson of the OECD was quoted as saying the UK has the lowest such rate of any member. Those numbers should be read with care: the figures vary with the earner profile and the cohort modelled, and an earlier House of Commons Library briefing, using a different basis, put the UK at 54.4% against an OECD average of 61.4%.

The gap with continental Europe reflects design. Many peers, including France, Italy and Spain, run earnings-related public schemes that pay a large share of final salary. The UK pays a flat-rate pension and relies on workplace and private saving, which the OECD's mandatory-provision measure largely leaves out, to do the rest. The UK's low ranking is therefore partly a statement about how much it expects people to save privately, not only about how much the state pays.

The fiscal side

The other half of the comparison is cost. A flat payment that is uprated by the highest of three indices will rise as a share of average earnings whenever inflation or the 2.5% floor beats wage growth, narrowing the gap with peers over time. The cost also compounds: every extra point of uprating is paid to millions of recipients, every year, with no end date.

That is the dilemma ministers face. Weakening the lock would lower borrowing, but it would cut the incomes of a group that votes heavily and sits near the bottom of the international table. Keeping it leaves a rising commitment at a time when higher gilt yields make each pound borrowed dearer. For savers and pension-fund investors, a more generous state floor would reduce the pressure on private saving; a squeezed one would raise it.

What to watch

The Budget is the next test. Any move to change the lock's formula, or to alter the way the state pension age or tax thresholds interact with it, would show how far the Treasury is willing to go. The autumn earnings and CPI figures that set the next uprating will then show how much the commitment is likely to cost next April.
