Indian workers film themselves at work to train AI systems

In one sentence
Data firms in India pay thousands of people about $2 an hour to film their daily tasks, producing footage that trains robots to do the same work.

A growing corner of India's data-labelling industry pays people to film themselves doing ordinary work. The footage is sold on as training material for automation, chiefly robots meant to do the manual tasks the workers are filmed performing. The BBC reported the trend. Reporting by AFP, from June, supplies more detail on pay and the firms involved.

What the work looks like

The set-up is simple. A worker straps a smartphone to their forehead, or wears a head camera or smart glasses, and records themselves doing a task from a first-person view. Clients have asked for folding clothes, making coffee, cooking particular dishes and assembling sandwiches. Robot developers want that footage because it shows how human hands handle real objects.

AFP described Nagireddy Sriramyachandra, 25, a homemaker in Chennai who films herself slicing mangoes for an app that passes the video to the data firm Objectways. Pay is just over $2 an hour of footage, or about 250 rupees. "Who else will give you 250 rupees an hour just for doing housework?" she said. Rani N., 21, an engineering graduate, films about 90 four-minute videos a day and calls the job "tolerable." Some workers film in mock furnished apartments built as studios. Others film in real workplaces: Objectways supplies head cameras to eight people at a textile factory in Karur.

Scale and buyers

The industry is made up of subcontractors. Objectways has offices in India and the US and says its clients include Fortune 500 multinationals. Qanat Consulting Services, an Objectways subcontractor in Andhra Pradesh, has about 2,000 contributors and supplies roughly a dozen larger data firms. Bengaluru's Humyn Labs also collects video and conversation data. The end buyers are mostly unnamed in the coverage, so who is paying for the footage remains unclear. No reliable total for the number of workers has been published; AFP said only that "thousands" are doing this work.

The tension in the arrangement is obvious, and some participants see it. Ponni, 55, who makes flower garlands at a roadside stall in Bengaluru and has been paid to wear a phone on her forehead for about a decade, said workers will "face a problem" in future if they do similar work. Ravi Shankar, who runs Objectways, took the opposite view: "Some jobs are supposed to be taken over, so humans can go and do better things." Manish Agarwal of Humyn Labs rejected the idea that robots will take jobs and said humans and robots "will work together."

Why the stakes are high in India

India's government think tank, NITI Aayog, counts 490 million informal workers and has warned about white-collar job losses while noting that little attention goes to informal labour. Morgan Stanley has projected more than a billion humanoid robots in use by 2050, though mostly in industrial and commercial settings rather than homes. Those are forecasts, not evidence that these particular jobs will vanish. Today's pay is small, but it is real income for people with few comparable options.

What is missing is how long the demand will last. Developers need large volumes of footage now, while their robots are being taught; once the models are good enough, the need may fall. Details to watch are published pay rates, the identity of the end buyers, and whether workers get any say over how their footage is reused.
