Renters' reform takes effect as landlords warn of higher rents

In one sentence
England's Renters' Rights Act, which ended no-fault evictions from 1 May 2026, faces its real test in listings and rent data, not in landlords' warnings of higher rents.

England's biggest change to private renting in a generation is no longer a prospect. The Renters' Rights Act 2025 received Royal Assent in October 2025. Its main tenancy provisions were scheduled to commence on 1 May 2026, and the argument has since moved from the statute to the evidence. Landlord groups warned for years that the law would push rents up and thin out the supply of homes to let. The question now is whether that shows up in the market or stays a lobbying position.

What changed

The central change is the abolition of Section 21 'no-fault' evictions, which let a landlord end an assured shorthold tenancy without giving a reason once a fixed term had run out. Fixed-term contracts give way to periodic tenancies that roll on until the tenant leaves or the landlord has a legally recognised ground for possession. Those grounds, such as selling the property, moving in or serious rent arrears, remain, but they must be argued rather than assumed.

Rent rises are also tighter. A landlord may raise the rent once a year, and only through the Section 13 process, which sets out the proposed figure and lets a tenant challenge it at a tribunal. The aim is to stop a rise being used as a back-door eviction.

The Act applies in England only. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland run their own tenancy regimes.

The landlords' case

Buy-to-let owners argue that losing the ability to regain a property easily raises the risk of owning one. Some, they say, will sell, and those who stay will charge more to cover the risk of a slower repossession. That chain of reasoning is plausible. It is also a forecast, and forecasts from a party with an interest in the outcome deserve checking against data.

The case for scepticism is that rents in much of England were already rising on thin supply and strong demand. Sorting out the effect of the Act from those pressures, and from higher borrowing costs, is hard. Landlords have been under strain from mortgage rates and tax changes for several years. A fall in the number of rental homes could reflect any of these, not just the new law.

What to watch

Three measures will say most. The first is the stock of homes listed to let, which agents and portals publish regularly. The second is the pace of asking-rent growth against wages. The third is the share of landlords selling up, visible in sales listings and in lender data on buy-to-let lending. Court figures on possession claims will show how landlords use the remaining grounds for eviction.

The wider market backdrop matters too. Bank Rate was held at 3.75% and a gilt sell-off has pushed UK house prices into annual decline, as reported in this edition's markets coverage. That makes selling less attractive for some landlords and keeps mortgage costs high for those who remain. Housebuilders and banks are exposed to the same gilt moves, a point picked up in the FTSE 100 week-ahead.

The government's Budget, built around housing, is the next fixed date. Any change to landlord taxation would act on the same supply decisions the Act is accused of distorting. Until the listings and rent data separate the two effects, the landlords' warnings remain unproven.
