BoE's Super Thursday looms with a hike risk markets are pricing out

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, where the Monetary Policy Committee delivers its 30 July 'Super Thursday' decision. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Markets price an 86% chance the BoE holds at 3.75% on 30 July, but chief economist Huw Pill has now voted for a hike twice running.

Five days out from the Bank of England's next rate decision, the money markets have made up their minds: hold. SONIA futures price roughly an 86% chance the Monetary Policy Committee leaves Bank Rate at 3.75% on 30 July, with only about a 14% chance of a quarter-point rise to 4.00%. The Bank's own chief economist is not nearly so settled.

Asked on BBC's Walescast programme on 9 July whether rates would need to rise over the coming year, Huw Pill gave a one-line answer: "The short answer is yes." He added that he was "concerned that we've been running the economy a little bit hotter than the supply side" — central-banker phrasing for an economy generating more inflation than its productive capacity can absorb without strain.

Pill is not merely thinking aloud. He voted for a hike at the MPC's April meeting, when the committee held 8–1, and again in June, when it held 7–2 — joined that time by Megan Greene, who also wanted Bank Rate at 4.00%. That marks two consecutive dissents from the Bank's own head of monetary analysis, an unusual position for someone whose day job is presenting the institutional forecast. A third hawkish voice, Catherine Mann, has so far stayed with the majority but says she is watching energy prices, corporate margins and next year's wage round for signs that inflation is becoming embedded — and has described herself as ready to back an "activist" hike if it does.

Where the inflation argument bites

The case for holding rests on headline numbers moving the Bank's way: CPI inflation eased to 2.6% in the year to June, from 2.8% in May, on the back of falling fuel prices. But the Bank has said for two years that services inflation — wage-linked, harder to dislodge, and largely insulated from energy swings — is the figure that actually drives policy. That measure climbed from 3.2% in April to 3.7% in May before easing only a tick, to 3.6%, in June. Comfortably above anything consistent with the 2% target, it is the number Pill and Greene keep pointing to.

30 July is what traders still call a Super Thursday: the rate vote, the minutes and a full Monetary Policy Report with updated growth and inflation forecasts, all released together at noon, followed by a press conference — relocated this time to Bloomberg's London offices while the Bank's own conference centre gets an AV upgrade. Even a hold carries information; the MPR's revised inflation path will show how much weight the committee is putting on the Pill-Greene argument versus the disinflation the headline number is now delivering.

What a surprise would do

The asymmetry is the trading story. An 86%-priced hold leaves little room for sterling to rally on a non-event, but a hike — even a narrow one — would land against positioning built for no change, pushing the pound sharply higher and forcing an unwind of carry trades funded in cheap sterling. Given a chief economist and one more MPC member have now voted for higher rates twice running, the tail risk markets are discounting looks larger than the 14% price would suggest.

The next data point that matters arrives with the decision itself: the Monetary Policy Report's forecasts, published alongside the 30 July vote, will show whether the Bank still sees services inflation as a passing effect of last year's wage settlements or as something the committee expects to fight into 2027.
