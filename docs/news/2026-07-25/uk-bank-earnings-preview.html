Barclays, Lloyds and NatWest set to post H1 profit growth

Barclays' Canary Wharf headquarters at One Churchill Place; the bank reports half-year results on Tuesday 28 July. — Secretlondon / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Barclays, Lloyds and NatWest are set to post higher H1 profits this week, testing whether UK credit conditions can outrun Gulf-driven volatility.

Britain's three big domestic banks report half-year results over the coming week, and the City's working assumption going in is that all three will post higher profits than a year ago — a modest vote of confidence in UK credit conditions at a moment when headlines have offered little else to be confident about.

Barclays reports first, on Tuesday, with analysts pencilling in pre-tax profit of roughly £5.9bn for the first six months of the year, up from £5.2bn in the same period of 2025. Lloyds follows on Thursday, alongside a strategy update, with consensus around £4.1bn against £3.5bn last year. NatWest closes the week on Friday; it does not have a published consensus figure yet, but Q1 numbers released in April beat expectations and prompted the bank to lift its income guidance for the year, so the bar for a repeat performance is set high rather than low.

If the forecasts hold, it will mark a second straight quarter of the sector shrugging off the geopolitical shock that has otherwise dominated markets since June — the widening Gulf conflict, the Hormuz blockade and the Houthi attacks that have pushed oil prices and gilt yields around and fed through into higher mortgage rates for UK borrowers. For lenders whose core business is judging exactly that kind of risk, growing profits through it is a data point worth more than a press release.

The bad-loan question

The caveat sits in the provisions line, not the headline number. In the first quarter, four of the sector's five biggest banks — NatWest, Lloyds, HSBC and Standard Chartered, though not Barclays — booked charges totalling roughly £601m tied directly to the Iran conflict, as they adjusted loan-loss models for a weaker growth and inflation outlook. Lloyds alone set aside £73m for bad debts, up 40% year-on-year, citing higher expected unemployment and living costs. Barclays avoided the Iran-linked charge but took its own £228m hit from the collapse of specialist lender MFS, a reminder that credit stress can show up from directions other than the obvious one.

NatWest's chief executive framed the sector's real exposure in a single line to analysts this spring: "the big question is how long the conflict lasts — a lot depends on the duration of the energy shock and the supply issues." That remains true four months on. Profit growth in the H1 numbers reflects lending volumes, deposit margins and cost control holding up through April and May; whether provisions climb further depends on how the third quarter plays out, not the second.

Taken together with Q1, in which the sector's combined pre-tax profit of £15.6bn beat a £15.5bn consensus, analysts have full-year 2026 pre-tax income for the big five UK banks at roughly £58bn, about £7bn above 2025. That would be a strong year by recent standards. It would also leave the sector more exposed than usual to a single geopolitical variable it cannot control.

Barclays reports Tuesday 28 July, Lloyds Thursday 30 July with its strategy update, and NatWest closes the run on Friday 31 July — three data points in four days on whether UK credit conditions are holding, or merely have not yet caught up with the shock.
