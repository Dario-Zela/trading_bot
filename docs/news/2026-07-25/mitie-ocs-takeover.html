Mitie accepts £3.1bn OCS bid as UK M&A hits a record pace

The Shard, London, where Mitie's headquarters sit on level 12 — the facilities group has agreed to a £3.1bn takeover by rival OCS. — Fred Romero / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
OCS's £3.1bn, 44.7%-premium bid for Mitie is the 11th UK mega-takeover of 2026, part of a record $231bn wave of foreign and PE buying of London-listed firms.

OCS Group agreed on Monday to pay £3.1bn for Mitie Group, the FTSE 250 facilities-management firm that cleans, guards and maintains buildings across Britain — including, improbably, the Houses of Parliament. Mitie shareholders will get 218.5p a share in cash plus a final dividend of up to 3.1p, a package worth up to 221.6p a share and a 44.7% premium to where the stock closed before the approach leaked.

Mitie's board recommended the offer unanimously. Chief executive Phil Bentley, who has run the company since 2016 and rebuilt it after a 2018 profit warning, called it "a testament to everything we have achieved at Mitie in recent years." He will stay on through completion, expected in the first quarter of 2027, before handing the combined business to OCS chief executive Rob Legge. The enlarged group will employ roughly 136,000 people and turn over about £8.5bn a year.

On its own terms, this is an unremarkable trade story: one facilities firm buying another to get bigger. What makes it a front-page item is where it sits in the calendar. Mitie is the eleventh UK-listed company this year to attract a takeover bid worth £1bn or more — a pace of mega-deals not seen in London since before the pandemic, according to AJ Bell. Nobody expects it to be the last.

A record built deal by deal

Run the tape back and the list reads like a survey of British industry changing hands. EQT's £9.4bn approach for testing group Intertek was Britain's largest private-equity takeover since Alliance Boots in 2007. Zurich Insurance bid £8.1bn for Beazley at a 59.8% premium. Nuveen offered £9.9bn for asset manager Schroders. ABB is paying £4.1bn for engineer Rotork, a 73% premium. Apollo has EasyJet in its sights at an 81% premium. Ingredion, the US corn-refining group, bought Tate & Lyle in June. Add Mitie and the running total of live and completed bids for London-listed companies this year comes to roughly £69.3bn — about 2.4% of the entire FTSE All-Share and AIM market by capitalisation, the highest share since before Covid.

Zoom out further and the number gets starker. Reuters data put the total value of takeover offers for UK companies at more than $231bn by the start of July — up 210% on the same point last year, and the second-highest year-to-date figure since records began in 1980. Foreign buyers account for 86% of that by value, itself an all-time high, up from 75% a year earlier. American acquirers alone made up more than half of the foreign approaches. Across the deals where terms are public, the average premium paid this year is 43% — Mitie's 45% sits comfortably within that band, not above it.

Cheap by whose measure

The standard explanation is that UK plc is on sale. The FTSE 100 has traded at a persistent discount to US and European peers for years, a gap that widened further this spring as an Iran-linked oil shock rattled gilts and sterling. For an overseas buyer, or a private equity house sitting on unspent capital, a London-listed target priced in cheap currency and cheap multiples is arithmetic, not conviction. Dominic Ross, a partner at Clifford Chance who advises on UK takeovers, put the underlying draw more plainly: "The UK is a tried and tested market" — predictable law, predictable process, assets nobody else seems to want at the price on offer.

Michael Field at Morningstar frames the mechanism from the other side: "Certain targets with depressed valuations… are at the mercy of larger companies that see now as the time to swallow them up." Since 2023, London has fielded 154 bids for companies worth more than £100m, erasing roughly £165bn of market capitalisation. New listings over the same period — companies choosing to join the market rather than leave it — number just 11, adding back only £6bn. Bids are currently outstripping new listings by around 27 to one by value, according to Bloomberg's tally, with London losing more than $2bn a week in market capitalisation to takeovers this year alone.

Two readings, one balance sheet

Whether that is a crisis or simply capital doing its job depends on where an investor sits. Bulls point out that shareholders keep collecting 40-50% premiums almost every time — Mitie's holders are being paid handsomely to give up a company that, on Monday's price, the market had been quietly undervaluing. Sellers of this kind of deal do not tend to complain.

The more uneasy reading comes from people who worry about what is left standing. Steven Fine, chief executive of broker Peel Hunt, has described the London equity market as "a strategic national asset" now suffering "a slow leak across multiple channels" — fewer listed companies, thinner liquidity, a shrinking pool for domestic pension funds and index trackers to invest in. Dan Coatsworth at AJ Bell makes the more mechanical version of the same point: "The ongoing takeover trend in general is bad for investors because it is reducing choice." Rebecca Maclean of Aberdeen Investments summed up the arithmetic problem bluntly after the Mitie news broke: the number of listed UK companies has fallen 11% in five years, and "sooner or later we are going to run out of them."

What happens next

Mitie's deal still needs shareholder approval, Competition and Markets Authority clearance and a national security review — the same gauntlet every large UK takeover now runs, and one the government has shown no appetite to tighten. None of those hurdles looks likely to stop it. The more consequential test is whether the pace holds through the second half: another Iran-linked oil spike, a rockier gilt market, or a sharp re-rating of the FTSE could just as easily choke the pipeline as accelerate it. For now, with $231bn already committed and the CMA clock still running on several live approaches, the base case among dealmakers is that London logs a twelfth mega-takeover before its thirteenth.
