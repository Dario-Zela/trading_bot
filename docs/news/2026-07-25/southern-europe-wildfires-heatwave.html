Southern Europe's wildfires and record heat force mass evacuations

Wildfire smoke rises over Los Gallardos, Almería, in the blaze that killed 13 people in July 2026. — Urci dream / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Spain declared its first-ever wildfire national emergency and France mobilised troops as fires near Madrid and Bordeaux forced 200,000+ evacuations and killed 13.

The fire that tore through the hills above Los Gallardos in Almería province on the night of 9 July gave residents almost no warning. By the time it was contained three days later, it had burned some 7,000 hectares of dry esparto grass and scrub, killed 13 people — some in their cars as they tried to flee — and left ten more missing. It is now the deadliest wildfire in Andalusian history and the third-deadliest in Spain's.

Two weeks on, the fire that started that crisis has become a continental one. On 24 July, Spain's government invoked a national emergency over wildfires for the first time ever, a step that hands Madrid direct operational control and had previously only been used once before, during last year's Iberian power blackout. Hours later, French President Emmanuel Macron ordered the military to mobilise against a blaze bearing down on Bordeaux, and requested help from Brussels. Across the two countries, more than 200,000 people have fled their homes — some by boat.

Two fronts, one summer

Spain's emergency was triggered less by Almería, which had already been contained, than by new fires closing on Madrid itself — blazes in Ávila province came within roughly 50 kilometres of the capital, forcing more than 10,000 evacuations even as crews were still working other fronts. Nationally, more than 100,000 hectares have now burned in Spain this year, already matching the country's average annual burned area over the past decade, with two months of fire season still to run.

"We're living through a dramatic situation, not just in various Spanish provinces but also in regions of neighbouring countries," Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez said in announcing the emergency declaration, a rare acknowledgment that this is no longer a national problem to be managed alone.

In France, the main fire has been burning since 22 July near Cap Ferret, roughly 60 kilometres from Bordeaux, and has consumed some 125 square kilometres — Macron has called it the worst wildfire the country has seen. France activated the EU Civil Protection Mechanism, and Brussels has responded with two Croatian Canadair water bombers, two Portuguese Air Tractor planes and Black Hawk helicopters from the Czech Republic and Slovakia — part of a bloc-wide deployment this year of 22 firefighting aircraft, five helicopters and 777 pre-positioned firefighters. Satellite data compiled by the EU's EFFIS monitoring system put 2026 on track to be the second-largest wildfire year in the bloc in two decades.

The heat behind the fires

None of this is happening in isolation from an extraordinary run of heat. The UK's Met Office confirmed this month that Britain had recorded its eighth day above 34°C in 2026 — a new calendar-year record, beating the previous mark of seven days set in 1976 and again in 2020 — with a provisional 34.4°C measured at Wisley in Surrey. The country has now recorded temperatures of 35°C or higher in May, June and July in the same year for the first time since records began; June alone produced a high of 37.7°C in Norfolk. Germany and Spain have separately reported all-time national temperature highs this summer.

The heat is not just fuelling fires — it is straining the infrastructure meant to keep the region running. With river temperatures on the Garonne, Rhône and Meuse rising in the heat, France's grid operator EDF has been forced to curtail output at nuclear reactors in Golfech, Bugey and Chooz that depend on those rivers for cooling, taking out roughly 12% of the country's nuclear capacity at the height of summer demand. Belgium's public health institute, Sciensano, has already logged 1,747 excess deaths during a June heatwave, with the Netherlands recording 480 more than expected over the same stretch — an early signal of a mortality toll that, as with past European heatwaves, will take months to tally in full.

Costs that don't burn out

What distinguishes this summer from previous bad ones is less the individual fires than the simultaneity — two major EU economies mounting national-level emergency responses in the same week, against a backdrop of reactors going offline for heat rather than mechanical failure. Reinsurers have flagged wildfire as the fastest-growing peril on their books, with insured losses in the category rising by an estimated 12% a year; the deeper problem, as European regulators at EIOPA have warned in recent months, is that only around a quarter of Europe's extreme-weather losses have historically been insured at all, leaving governments to absorb the rest through emergency declarations like Spain's.

That is the throughline connecting a burned Andalusian village, a curtailed nuclear reactor and a reinsurer's loss model: infrastructure and institutions built for a narrower range of weather are now routinely operating past its edges, in economies with neither the insurance depth nor the grid headroom to treat it as routine. The immediate test is whether containment holds through the last week of July, when temperatures are forecast to stay elevated across the western Mediterranean. The larger one — how the EU prices this into energy planning, insurance regulation and the pace of its own climate targets — will be argued over for considerably longer than any single fire burns.
