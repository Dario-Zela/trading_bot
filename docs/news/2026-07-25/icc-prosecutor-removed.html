ICC ousts chief prosecutor Karim Khan in unprecedented vote

The International Criminal Court headquarters in The Hague, the seat of the tribunal that voted to remove its own chief prosecutor. — Choinowski / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
ICC states voted 82-43 to oust prosecutor Karim Khan over misconduct claims, months after judges unanimously cleared him, amid US and Israeli fury over his Netanyahu warrant.

The International Criminal Court removed its own chief prosecutor on Friday, the first time in the court's 24-year history that member states have voted a sitting prosecutor out of office. Karim Khan — the barrister who built the ICC's case for arrest warrants against Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and former defence minister Yoav Gallant — was ousted over allegations that he sexually harassed a female aide, in a secret ballot at the UN's New York headquarters that fell squarely across the fault line the court has straddled for the past two years: institutional independence versus the political weight of the world's most powerful states.

82 of the ICC's 125 member states voted to dismiss Khan, comfortably clearing the 63-vote threshold for removal. He had already been suspended from his duties since June, when the Assembly's Bureau found he had committed "serious misconduct." He denies all wrongdoing.

A complaint, two dead ends, and a UN inquiry

The allegations against Khan surfaced on May 2, 2024 — the same day he announced he was seeking arrest warrants against Netanyahu and Gallant over the war in Gaza. A colleague referred the complaint to the court's Internal Oversight Mechanism the next day. That inquiry closed within weeks after the complainant, an ICC staffer identified publicly only as "Sarah," declined to cooperate; a second attempt in the autumn of 2024 met the same fate. She later told CNN that Khan's behaviour had escalated from touching and groping to an incident in which he allegedly touched her intimately while she appeared to be asleep.

With the internal process stalled, the Assembly's president asked the United Nations' own watchdog, the Office of Internal Oversight Services, to investigate. Its 150-page report landed in December 2025. What happened next is the detail that gives this story its edge: an independent three-judge panel reviewed that report and, on March 9 this year, unanimously concluded its findings "do not establish misconduct or breach of duty" under the relevant legal standard. Khan, by that reading, had been cleared.

The ICC's Bureau — the body of elected officials that runs the Assembly's affairs between sessions — disagreed with its own judicial process. On April 2 it voted to set aside the panel's conclusion and weigh the evidence itself. By June 8 it had reversed the exoneration outright, suspending Khan and reframing the episode as an improper "sexual relationship" rather than the non-consensual conduct originally alleged. Six weeks later, the full Assembly ratified that reversal by an overwhelming margin.

The Netanyahu shadow

It is difficult to read the Bureau's about-face in isolation from the pressure the court has been under since Khan sought the Netanyahu and Gallant warrants — the first time the ICC had targeted officials of a state allied with Washington. The Pre-Trial Chamber issued both warrants in November 2024. Three months later President Trump signed an executive order sanctioning ICC officials, with Khan named first: his US visa was revoked, his wife and children barred from entering the country, and accounts tied to him frozen. Eleven court officials and several NGOs supporting the Gaza investigation have since been sanctioned. Secretary of State Marco Rubio has described the campaign as an effort to "dismantle the threat posed by the International Criminal Court to US sovereignty," and has pressed member states to withdraw from the institution altogether.

Israel's reaction to Friday's vote made the connection explicit rather than incidental. UN Ambassador Danny Danon said Khan had believed that "by launching a political witch hunt against Israel and issuing a politically motivated arrest warrant against Prime Minister Netanyahu, the world would ignore the grave sexual misconduct allegations against him. He was wrong." Foreign Minister Gideon Saar called the removal "long-delayed" and urged the court to drop the warrants entirely.

Khan's legal team argues the sequencing itself is the scandal. "The independence of the International Criminal Court depends upon its elected officials being protected from removal through processes that are political, procedurally unfair or inconsistent with the findings of independent judicial bodies," they said in a statement, adding that Khan had been removed "by an executive vote while under sanctions and while the Court is under immense political pressure." More than 180 Palestinian civil society and rights organisations went further, arguing the disciplinary process itself "has been reduced to a political referendum following the national interests of individual States Parties."

What the vote settles, and what it doesn't

Khan's removal does not touch the warrants he built. Only the ICC's judges can withdraw them, and there is no indication they intend to. Deputy prosecutors Nazhat Shameen Khan and Mame Mandiaye Niang will run the office in the interim, with a permanent successor expected to be elected next year. Venezuela used the occasion to announce its own withdrawal from the court, citing what it called a longstanding bias toward prosecuting African and Latin American states — a reminder that Washington and Jerusalem are not the only capitals with grievances against The Hague.

What the episode does settle, more clearly than any single ruling could, is how a body built to sit above politics behaves when politics presses hard enough. A judicial panel cleared Khan on the merits; the body that appointed him overruled its own judges and then put the matter to a vote its harshest critics had spent a year demanding. The next prosecutor will inherit not just Khan's caseload but the precedent that a sitting chief prosecutor can be removed over the head of an independent judicial finding — a precedent that will sit in the back of the mind of any future prosecutor weighing whether to open a case against a state with the leverage to make the cost personal.
