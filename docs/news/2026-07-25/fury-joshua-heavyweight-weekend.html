Fury stops Wach, keeps Joshua's comeback fight on track for tonight

Tyson Fury, pictured ringside in 2017, stopped Mariusz Wach in the seventh round to stay on course for a Joshua unification fight. — Mike DiDomizio / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Fury stopped Mariusz Wach in round seven in Thailand; a day later Joshua fights Kristian Prenga in Jeddah, both clearing final hurdles to a signed late-2026 unification bout.

Tyson Fury did his job on Friday night. Now it is Anthony Joshua's turn.

Fury stopped the 46-year-old Polish veteran Mariusz Wach in the seventh round in Pattaya, Thailand, dominating from the opening bell and switching between orthodox and southpaw stances as he peppered Wach with jabs and uppercuts — at one stretch landing five uppercuts in a row. Wach's corner pulled him out after the round, and Fury's win keeps him on course for the fight both British heavyweights have spent years dancing around: a Fury–Joshua unification bout, contracts reportedly signed, targeted for late 2026.

Less than 24 hours later, Joshua steps in himself, facing Albanian contender Kristian Prenga at the Jeddah Superdome in Saudi Arabia — a card promoted as "The Comeback" and originally slated for Riyadh before a late venue switch. It is Joshua's first fight since a fifth-round knockout loss to Daniel Dubois in September 2024, and his first appearance of any kind since a December car crash in Nigeria killed two members of his camp, strength coach Sina Ghami and trainer Latif Ayodele. Prenga carries a 20-1 record but sits 35th in BoxRec's world rankings — a opponent chosen, plainly, to get Joshua a win rather than a scare.

A weekend built to order

The compression is the story. Two fighters both angling for the same November payday chose to clear their final obstacles inside a single weekend rather than spacing them out — Fury on Friday, Joshua on Saturday, both in Saudi-backed shows funded by the kingdom's continuing push into heavyweight boxing. That is not how comeback fights are normally sequenced, particularly for a 36-year-old returning from a brutal knockout loss and a personal tragedy. It reads less like a coincidence of matchmaking and more like a promoter's calendar — Turki Alalshikh, the Saudi power broker who has bankrolled the sport's biggest recent cards, wanted both routes to November cleared at once, and got his wish.

Fury, for his part, made clear he saw his outing as low-stakes bookkeeping. "I wanted to get the rounds in. I want to be active," he said afterwards, before confirming he would skip Joshua's fight entirely: "I'm definitely not going to Jeddah. I'm going home to my wife and kids." He had been away from home for ten weeks of camp on top of four months prior, he said — context that undercuts any reading of this as two camps drafting off each other's momentum. Fury treated Wach as a formality to be dispensed with quickly so he could go home; Joshua's assignment carries rather more weight.

The harder half of the equation

That asymmetry is what makes the back-to-back scheduling worth noting rather than shrugging off. Fury needed an active-fighter box ticked. Joshua needs to prove, in front of a live gate and a DAZN pay-per-view audience, that he can still take a step forward physically and mentally after the worst eighteen months of his career — the Dubois defeat, then the Nigeria crash. Prenga is not expected to test that in any serious way, but visible ring rust, a bad cut, or a shaky chin would immediately reprice the November fight, which bookmakers and broadcasters alike are already treating as one of the sport's biggest paydays of the year, a Ring Magazine-sanctioned show set for Netflix.

Fury's throwaway line about Joshua's "balls" was needle, not analysis, but it points at the real question hanging over the weekend: whether either man's warm-up bout tells anyone anything useful about November, or whether both are simply contractual boxes being ticked on the way to a fight neither side wants to risk before it happens.

What's next

The Principality Stadium in Wales has emerged as the front-runner to host Fury–Joshua, with organisers still working through curfew restrictions and US time-zone scheduling — meaning a venue and date announcement is the next concrete marker to watch, likely within weeks of Saturday's result in Jeddah. Betting markets on the unification bout will move on how convincingly Joshua handles Prenga; a clean, quick finish keeps the price roughly where it sits now, while a laboured points win or a knockdown suffered would shift odds meaningfully against him heading into a fight both fighters, and their Saudi backers, need to happen on schedule.
