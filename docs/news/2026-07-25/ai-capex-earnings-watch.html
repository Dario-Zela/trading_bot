Big Tech's AI-spending jitters roll into Microsoft and Apple earnings

Cooling towers at Google's data center in The Dalles, Oregon — the kind of AI infrastructure driving the capex spending investors are now questioning. — Tony Webster / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Alphabet and Tesla beat revenue estimates but shares fell sharply on AI capex fears, and Microsoft and Apple's results next week face the same test.

Alphabet and Tesla both beat Wall Street's revenue targets this week. Both stocks got hammered anyway. The reason in each case was the same: investors have stopped rewarding AI spending on faith and started asking when it pays for itself.

Alphabet's Q2 report, released Wednesday, was by most measures strong — revenue of $119.8 billion, up 24% year-on-year and above the $116.9 billion consensus, with Google Cloud revenue climbing 82%. Shares fell 7.7% regardless. The trigger was capital expenditure: Alphabet raised its full-year 2026 capex guidance to a range of $195 billion to $205 billion, up from roughly $190 billion previously, and finance chief Anat Ashkenazi told analysts spending would rise "significantly" again in 2027. Free cash flow came under visible pressure. For a company still printing double-digit revenue growth, the market's verdict was that the bill for AI infrastructure is arriving faster than the returns.

Tesla's numbers were worse

Tesla reported the same day, and the reaction was sharper. Revenue of $28.2 billion beat estimates, but adjusted earnings of $0.33 a share missed the $0.49 consensus by a wide margin, and operating margin collapsed to 1.4% from 4.1% a year earlier. Tesla burned roughly $5.8 billion on AI initiatives in the quarter — capex up 142% year-on-year, on a path to exceed $25 billion for 2026 — and posted its first negative quarterly free cash flow in more than two years, a $1.1 billion outflow. The stock fell as much as 14.5% at one point, its worst single session since March last year. Together, Alphabet and Tesla erased close to $500 billion in combined market value inside a few trading days, and the Nasdaq is now on track for its second straight weekly decline.

Microsoft and Apple are next

The question now is whether the same skepticism follows Microsoft and Apple into next week's results. Microsoft reports fiscal fourth-quarter earnings on July 29, guiding to $86.7-87.8 billion in revenue and Azure growth of 39-40%; it has already flagged capex exceeding $40 billion for the quarter alone — a new record — on a path to roughly $190 billion for calendar 2026, about $25 billion of which the company attributes to a global memory-chip price crunch. Microsoft's counterargument to the Alphabet playbook is a number it rarely discloses: an AI business run rate of $37 billion, up 123% year-on-year, offered as evidence the spending is converting into revenue rather than sitting on the balance sheet as depreciation risk. Apple reports a day later, on July 30, with EPS expected to rise roughly 20.5% to $1.89 on revenue up 15.5% to $108.6 billion — a much lighter capex profile than its cloud-and-chip peers, which may make it a comparatively safe harbor if the AI-spending selloff continues.

The pattern across this earnings season suggests the market's tolerance for AI investment has a ceiling, and that ceiling is proximity to profit. Revenue beats no longer buy mega-caps a pass on capex guidance; what moves the stock now is whether spending has a visible route to return. Microsoft's Tuesday numbers, and Apple's the day after, will show whether that judgment applies evenly across Big Tech or whether Alphabet and Tesla's AI bills were simply the largest and least explained.
