Dimon renews warning to Burnham government over bank levy

Jamie Dimon, chief executive of JPMorgan Chase, has renewed criticism of the UK's bank tax surcharge. — Steve Jurvetson / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Jamie Dimon renewed his threat to pull back JPMorgan's £3bn London tower if Burnham's government raises the UK bank surcharge.

Jamie Dimon has been here before. In 2015, JPMorgan's chief executive threatened to shift capital out of Britain over the bank levy; the following year George Osborne capped it and layered in a corporation-tax surcharge instead. A decade on, with Andy Burnham newly installed in No.10 and John Healey newly installed at the Treasury, Dimon is running the same play — only this time there is a £3bn tower in Canary Wharf sitting on the table as collateral.

Speaking on the Master Investor podcast with Wilfred Frost, recorded on 16 July and published as Burnham's government took shape, Dimon repeated a line he has used on and off since the surcharge was introduced: "If you have an uncompetitive tax system, capital leaves your country." Banks currently pay corporation tax at 28% — the standard 25% rate plus a 3% surcharge, itself cut down from 8% by Rishi Sunak in 2023 after years of sector lobbying. Dimon wants it cut further, or at minimum left alone. "JP Morgan did not damage the UK," he said. "I just thought it was a lack of principle to punish a company that had nothing to do with the crisis." He put a figure on the sector's grievance too: roughly $5bn paid by shareholders in the extra levy since its introduction.

The tower as leverage

What gives this round of criticism teeth is the Canary Wharf project. JPMorgan signed off in 2023 on a 3-million-square-foot, 279,000-square-metre tower designed by Foster + Partners — its planned European headquarters, big enough to house more than half the bank's 23,000-strong UK workforce, and pitched by supporters as adding close to £10bn to the economy and 7,800 construction jobs. A memorandum of understanding exempting the site from business rates was signed as recently as March. Dimon first floated scrapping the project in May if a change of government turned hostile to banks; on the podcast he declined to make what he called a "binary decision" on its future, saying only that a tax rise would leave him "very cautious."

The timing is pointed. Rachel Reeves, seen in the City as a known quantity on bank taxation, was removed from cabinet the Monday before Dimon's remarks aired, with Healey installed as chancellor. Neither Burnham nor Healey has said publicly whether the surcharge is under review, but Dimon's intervention reads as a pre-emptive marker laid down before any budget decisions are taken — an attempt to price in the political cost of touching it before the new Treasury team has settled on a position.

For Burnham, the calculation is not abstract. The surcharge raises real revenue against a fiscal backdrop already under strain, and a government elected partly on a growth-and-fairness platform faces an awkward optics problem in cutting bank taxes further. But if JPMorgan follows through on redeploying capital or shelving its tower, the reputational cost to London as a financial centre — one Osborne and successive chancellors spent a decade protecting — would be hard to undo. Barclays, Lloyds and NatWest report first-half results in the coming weeks, giving Healey an early read on how domestic lenders are faring under the current regime before any decision on Dimon's demand is made.
