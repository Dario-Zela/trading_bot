US pauses Iran strikes as Hormuz blockade and Houthi attacks widen the Gulf conflict

US Navy vessels transit the Strait of Hormuz, the chokepoint at the centre of Washington's reinstated blockade and 20% cargo toll. — U.S. Navy / Alex R. Forster, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
US strikes on Iran went unannounced for the first time in two weeks as Trump declines a deal timeline, while a Hormuz toll and new Houthi tanker strikes widen the Gulf conflict.

For the first time in two weeks, US Central Command did not announce a fresh wave of strikes on Iran on Friday. After 13 consecutive nights of bombardment, the silence was conspicuous enough that CNN said it had asked the Pentagon to confirm whether operations had actually paused, or whether the military had simply stopped posting about them. Nobody in Washington was rushing to explain which.

That ambiguity is, in miniature, the state of the whole war. Three and a half months after the United States first blockaded Iran's ports, the conflict has widened rather than resolved — spreading from direct strikes on Iranian territory into a naval chokepoint fight in the Strait of Hormuz and now a second front in the Red Sea, where Houthi forces this week struck two Saudi oil tankers by name. Markets are pricing all three simultaneously, and not always in the same direction.

A pause, or just a quiet night

The pattern had been unbroken since the last ceasefire collapsed: nearly two weeks of nightly CENTCOM statements confirming strikes on Iranian military targets, following on from an earlier round of fighting that began with an April blockade and a failed round of talks in Islamabad. Friday's silence broke that rhythm without explaining it.

President Trump filled some of the gap himself, saying the US and Iran remain in contact but that he does not "think it's time yet" for a deal. He described Iran as eager to negotiate — "they would like to do something" — while insisting Tehran isn't ready: "They need more of the same." It is a formulation Trump has used before in this conflict, going back to the collapsed ceasefire in the spring, and it functions less as a diplomatic signal than as a way of keeping both strikes and talks alive at once.

Iran, for its part, has not paused anything. Its military says it struck US bases in Bahrain, Jordan and Kuwait this week with drones and missiles, hitting equipment depots and hangars at Shaikh Isa Air Base and al-Azraq in retaliation for the American bombing campaign. Jordan says it intercepted seven missiles and six drones with no casualties, and Washington has not confirmed damage to its own installations — but the exchange underscores that a pause in one direction, if that is what Friday was, has not been matched in the other.

The blockade and the barrel

The more durable escalation is economic. On July 13, Trump announced the US would reinstate its naval blockade of the Strait of Hormuz — a waterway that carries roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil — and, in a move without real precedent, said Washington would charge a 20% toll on all cargo shipped through it, styling the US as "the guardian of the Hormuz Strait." The International Maritime Organization pushed back within days, its secretary-general Arsenio Dominguez calling the toll a violation of the transit-passage rights guaranteed under the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea and warning there is "no legal basis" for it.

Oil markets reacted immediately and have kept reacting. Brent crude jumped more than 9% on the blockade announcement, then spiked further this week — touching $102 a barrel on July 23, its highest level in eight weeks, before easing to just under $98 by Friday. Even with that pullback, Brent finished the week up more than 12%, evidence that traders are treating the risk as cumulative rather than one-off. The pattern of the last three weeks — spike, partial retreat, higher spike — suggests a market still searching for a ceiling rather than one that has found it.

A second front opens in the Red Sea

The newest driver is Yemen's Houthi movement, which on July 23 claimed missile and drone strikes on two Saudi-linked tankers, the Encelia and the Layla, in the Red Sea. Saudi state media confirmed the Encelia was hit, causing a fire at its bow; the crew was reported safe, and there was no immediate confirmation on the Layla. The Houthis framed the attack as enforcement of a naval blockade they had just declared against Saudi Arabia — a marked shift from the group's earlier, more scattershot campaign against commercial shipping toward deliberate strikes on Gulf energy infrastructure.

The timing matters. Bab el-Mandeb, the strait the Houthis are now contesting, carries a smaller share of global trade than Hormuz — roughly 5% of maritime traffic — but it is one of the few alternate routes for Gulf crude that doesn't pass through the strait the US is currently blockading. Squeezing both chokepoints at once removes the redundancy that had kept shipping executives calm through earlier rounds of Red Sea disruption. Trump responded by threatening "major military punishment" against Iran and the Houthis over further tanker attacks, and the Caspian Pipeline Consortium separately suspended loadings at its Black Sea terminal after unrelated tanker strikes — a sign the nervousness is no longer confined to the Gulf.

What the pause is worth

Taken together, the picture is of a war that has grown more diffuse without becoming any less dangerous. A one-night gap in CENTCOM's strike announcements is not a ceasefire, and Trump's refusal to set a deal timeline leaves the diplomatic track exactly as uncertain as it was a week ago. Meanwhile the blockade fee sits unresolved against international law, and the Houthis have shown they can open a second pressure point on Gulf oil whenever they choose. None of that has stopped Brent from pulling back off its Thursday peak — but a market pricing in an off-ramp and a market pricing in fatigue with volatility can look identical for a few days before they diverge. The next test is whether Saturday brings a CENTCOM strike announcement or another silence, and whether Riyadh responds to the tanker attacks with anything beyond the statement it has already issued.
