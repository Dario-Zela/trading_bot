Ebola outbreak grows in DR Congo's Ituri province, WHO warns

In one sentence
DRC's Ebola outbreak has hit 2,905 cases and 1,269 deaths — the fastest-spreading on record — as Ituri's epicenter turns violent and funding needs triple to $1.4bn.

The World Health Organization's director-general, Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus, told reporters in Geneva this month that the Ebola outbreak tearing through the Democratic Republic of Congo's Ituri province is now the third-largest ever recorded — and the fastest-growing in the disease's history. The numbers back him up. Confirmed cases in the DRC reached 2,905, with 1,269 deaths, as of July 22, according to the country's National Institute of Public Health — up sharply from 2,536 cases a day earlier, after authorities reconciled provincial and national databases that had been running out of sync.[^1] The case-fatality rate sits near 43%.

When the outbreak was first confirmed in mid-May, DRC's health ministry counted 246 suspected cases. Ten weeks on, that figure has been overtaken more than tenfold. The 2018–2019 Ebola epidemic in eastern DRC, the second-largest on record, took more than ten months to reach 2,000 confirmed cases; this one got there in about two.[^2] The pathogen behind it, Bundibugyo ebolavirus, has no approved vaccine or targeted treatment — unlike the more familiar Zaire strain, for which an effective vaccine exists.

A province where the response can't keep up

Ituri, a mineral-rich but conflict-scarred region in DRC's restive east, remains what Tedros called WHO's "biggest concern." More than 80% of new infections are being found outside known contact-tracing networks, and roughly two-thirds of deaths are occurring in the community rather than in a health facility — a sign that people are avoiding care, dying at home, and in some cases continuing traditional burial practices that spread the virus further.[^2]

That mistrust turned violent on July 15, when a group of young men in the Ituri town of Nyakunde stormed and set fire to part of a local Ebola treatment centre after health workers declined to release the body of a friend suspected of having died from the disease. Clashes that followed killed several people. The UN peacekeeping mission MONUSCO responded by deploying a mobile base nearby the next day to give medical staff and patients protection.[^3]

Across the border, the picture is brighter: Uganda's last confirmed patient was discharged on July 16 after two negative tests, starting a 42-day countdown toward an official declaration that its outbreak — 20 cases, two deaths — is over.

The response is also short of money. Africa CDC's director-general, Jean Kaseya, said on July 22 that the continental funding requirement has jumped from an initial $518 million to $1.4 billion as the outbreak has worsened, while UNICEF says only a quarter of the funds it needs are in hand.[^4] Trials of two therapeutics, remdesivir and MBP-134, began July 12, and the first volunteer was vaccinated July 24 in an expedited trial of one of three candidate vaccines from IAVI, Moderna and Oxford — but none is yet approved, and none will arrive fast enough to blunt the current wave.

The next marker is Uganda's 42-day clock, which runs out in late August; DRC's case count, still being reconciled between local and national reporting systems, is the more urgent one to watch.
