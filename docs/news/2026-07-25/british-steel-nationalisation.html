UK completes British Steel nationalisation as Jingye seeks compensation

Blast furnaces at British Steel's Scunthorpe works, the UK's last site producing virgin steel from raw ore. — Alan Murray-Rust / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
The UK has fully nationalised British Steel from Chinese owner Jingye, which is now seeking compensation and has warned it may pursue international arbitration.

The government now owns British Steel outright. On 16 July, ministers used freshly minted powers under the Steel Industry (Nationalisation) Act 2026 to convert what had been a temporary emergency takeover into full public ownership, ending Chinese conglomerate Jingye Group's six-year run as the company's owner. Jingye wants to be paid for what it lost — and Beijing has made clear it is watching closely.

The dispute is a small case study in a much larger shift: Western governments moving from subsidising strategic industries to simply owning them when a foreign owner's incentives stop lining up with the state's. Steel is not glamorous. But blast furnaces are hard to relight once cold, and Scunthorpe's are the last in Britain capable of making steel from raw ore rather than recycled scrap — a capability the government has decided is worth keeping regardless of what it costs the exchequer, or what it costs the relationship with China.

From special measures to full ownership

The endgame began in March 2025, when British Steel told the government it planned to consult on closing Scunthorpe's two blast furnaces, citing losses of roughly £700,000 a day. With up to 2,700 of the plant's 3,500 jobs on the line, Parliament was recalled for a rare Saturday sitting on 12 April 2025 and passed the Steel Industry (Special Measures) Act within a single day, handing the then-business secretary, Jonathan Reynolds, the power to direct the company and buy the raw materials Jingye had stopped ordering. Ministers said at the time that Jingye had planned to let existing stock run down and cancel further orders — effectively forcing a shutdown by starvation of supply.

That special-measures period was always meant to be temporary. It ended on 15 July this year, when the Steel Industry (Nationalisation) Act received royal assent, and the government transferred British Steel into full public ownership the next day. Business Secretary Peter Kyle framed it plainly: "British Steel now belongs to the British people. Our priority is to stabilise the business, support the communities connected to it, and build a sustainable, competitive, low-carbon steel sector." The National Audit Office has tallied the cost of keeping the furnaces running at about £1.3 million a day — £377 million by January, an estimated £600 million by the end of June, and a trajectory that could top £1.5 billion by 2028 if losses continue at the current rate.

Jingye's reckoning

Jingye is not walking away quietly. In a statement issued days after the takeover, the company said the nationalisation had "tarnished the credibility of the British government, spooked international investors and caused great losses to the company's operations and British taxpayers' funds." It argues the government "disregarded Jingye's continuous investment and significant contribution and was only willing to provide almost zero compensation," pointing to the roughly £1.2 billion it says it ploughed into the plant since buying British Steel for a nominal sum in 2020, when the business was in insolvency.

The company has opened formal consultations under the UK-China bilateral investment treaty, a 1986 agreement that protects foreign investors from "arbitrary expropriation without compensation," and says it is reserving the right to pursue international arbitration if talks fail. Under the nationalisation act, the government must appoint an independent valuer to determine what, if anything, is owed; the compensation regulations governing that process are due before Parliament in the autumn, with a right of appeal to the Upper Tribunal built in. Jingye's own account of the losses it says British Steel management racked up is disputed by the government, whose position remains that the business had, in effect, no positive value to compensate.

A test for Burnham

China's response has been pointed rather than muted. The Ministry of Commerce called itself "strongly dissatisfied," saying the takeover dealt "a severe blow to Chinese companies' confidence in investing in the UK" and accusing London of having "forcibly" seized the firm while disregarding Jingye's contribution to the British economy. The Foreign Ministry followed with a warning that how Britain handles compensation "will directly affect Chinese investors' confidence in the UK's investment climate and shape public perceptions in China of the British government's credibility."

The timing puts the dispute squarely on Andy Burnham's desk. The nationalisation was among the last major acts of the outgoing government before Burnham took over at No. 10, and he now inherits a live diplomatic irritant with Beijing just as his administration is finding its footing. It is not a crisis on the scale of the gilt-market jitters or the Gulf conflict now competing for Downing Street's attention, but it sits at an uncomfortable intersection: a Labour government that wants to keep courting Chinese trade and investment while also asserting the right to nationalise strategic industry on national-security grounds whenever it judges a foreign owner is not acting in Britain's interest.

That tension is not unique to Britain. Governments across Europe and North America have grown more willing to intervene directly in industries — semiconductors, ports, energy grids — once considered safe to leave to private, including foreign, capital. British Steel is a modest-sized company by global standards, but the precedent it sets for how Britain treats Chinese-owned assets deemed strategically sensitive will be read closely in Beijing, and by other foreign investors weighing whether UK ownership rules still mean what they used to.

The next concrete marker is autumn, when the compensation regulations go before Parliament and the independent valuer's process begins in earnest. Whatever figure emerges — and whether Jingye accepts it or pushes on to arbitration — will set a benchmark for how costly it is, in cash and in diplomatic capital, for Britain to take a strategic asset back into public hands.
