US and China edge toward adding agriculture to tariff-reduction deal

Container terminal at the Yangshan Deep-water Harbour Zone, Port of Shanghai. — Alex Needham / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
China's June exports jumped 27% on tariff front-running as Beijing confirmed an 'in-principle' — but still unsigned — deal to cut agricultural tariffs.

China's factories spent June racing the clock. Exports jumped 27% year-on-year to a record $412.4bn — the fastest monthly pace since October 2021 and well above the 18.2% economists had penciled in — as manufacturers rushed goods onto ships before a fresh 25% US tariff on another tranche of Chinese industrial and consumer products took effect on 1 July. Shipments to the US alone rose roughly 14% on the year. Semiconductors and other AI-linked hardware did the heavy lifting elsewhere, adding 6.9 percentage points to overall export growth, while the trade surplus widened to $125.6bn and imports hit a five-year high.

The timing matters because the front-running coincides with something else: Beijing's confirmation that the US and China have agreed, in principle, to fold agricultural products into their reciprocal tariff-reduction framework. China's Ministry of Commerce, through spokesman He Yadong, said in early July that the two sides had "agreed in principle to include relevant agricultural products under the reciprocal tariff-reduction framework" following trade consultations that trace back to a Trump-Xi summit in May. No date, no signed text, no schedule for phasing in cuts — just an in-principle understanding layered on top of the broader truce.

The gap between pledge and cargo

On paper, the numbers attached to the ag framework are large. China has committed to buying at least 25 million tonnes of US soybeans a year through 2028, plus a minimum $17bn annually in American agricultural goods across 2026-28, according to terms described by the White House. Beijing is also expected to drop its 10% tariff on US soybeans and grains if Washington reciprocates by lifting its own 10% fentanyl-linked tariff. China would additionally restore market access for US beef and resume poultry imports from states cleared of bird flu.

Actual purchasing has not kept pace with the rhetoric. Commitments from Chinese buyers so far amount to just 200,000 tonnes of soybeans for the marketing year that opens in September — a fraction of the pledged volume. Traders and crushers cite the tariffs still sitting on the books and the absence of a signed, binding text as reasons to stay on the sidelines. MOFCOM's own language underlines the caveat: trade is to proceed "according to market principles," with enterprises making independent purchasing decisions rather than following government direction — a hedge that leaves Beijing room to point to market conditions if volumes disappoint.

What the export data is really pricing

The June export surge is the one hard data point in this story; the ag framework is still a political signal. That the two are showing up together is the interesting part — it suggests exporters and buyers on both sides are already positioning for a friendlier tariff environment, front-loading shipments and inventory ahead of formal terms rather than waiting for the paperwork. That behavior compresses the eventual market reaction if the deal firms up on schedule, but it also means a delay, a narrower scope, or a stall in Beijing's soybean purchases carries more room to disappoint than it would if expectations were still unformed.

The next checkpoints are the September marketing-year opening, when China's actual soybean offtake will either validate the 25-million-tonne commitment or expose it as aspirational, and any formal signing of the tariff-reduction text — which, as of now, does not exist.
