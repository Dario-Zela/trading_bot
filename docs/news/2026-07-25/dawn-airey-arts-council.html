Dawn Airey named surprise new chair of Arts Council England

Arts Council England, which Dawn Airey will chair from 1 August, distributes more than £1 billion in public and lottery funding a year. — Arts Council England
In one sentence
Media executive Dawn Airey becomes Arts Council England's 17th chair as the body scraps its old strategy for a vaguer one built around "excellence."

Dawn Airey has spent three decades running television networks, not galleries. She co-founded Channel 5, ran Sky's channels business, sat in the chief executive's chair at Getty Images, and until this year served as deputy chair of Channel 4. None of it is exactly Tate Modern. Which is why her appointment as the 17th chair of Arts Council England, confirmed by the Department for Culture, Media and Sport, landed in the sector as something of a jolt.

Airey takes over on 1 August from Sir Nicholas Serota, the former Tate director who has chaired the council for nearly a decade, for a four-year term paid at £60,000 a year for two days' work a week — a modest honorarium for an organisation that distributes well over £1 billion in public and lottery money. Culture secretary Lisa Nandy framed the pick as an effort to "put people back at the centre" of how the council thinks about culture. Serota, for his part, offered the traditional handover blessing, saying Airey understands "the unique role the arts play in society." Airey herself struck a note about human creativity mattering more, not less, in what she called an AI-driven world — the kind of line executives reach for when the machines start writing press releases too.

The part that actually matters

Strip away the personnel story and there's a more consequential one sitting underneath it, half-told. Arts Council England has just scrapped Let's Create, the ten-year strategy that governed its funding decisions, after Margaret Hodge's independent review — commissioned by government and published last December — found the framework had become a bureaucratic thicket. Organisations of every size and artform were made to fill out identical, heavily metric-laden applications through Grantium, a case-management system so widely loathed that, per the review, it put people off applying altogether.

In its place, ACE has installed an interim "Strategic Framework" built around three principles: support excellence, deliver for everybody, reach everywhere. That is a rhetorical pivot back toward the word "excellence" after a decade in which Let's Create foregrounded cultural democracy and co-creation — and critics have already noted the new framework doesn't define excellence, or set out how diversity commitments will be measured going forward. The stakes are not abstract: the 2023–26 National Portfolio round drew 1,730 applications chasing more than £2 billion against £1.34 billion available, and the next cycle, covering 2028 to 2033, is where the new rules get tested for real.

What's next

Application guidance for that next funding round is due in September, with the window opening in October or November and decisions not landing until July 2027 — which means Airey will be roughly a year into the job, still finding her way around an arts sector she has never worked inside, when the first real test of the new framework arrives. Whether "excellence" turns out to mean anything more precise than a rebrand of the old rulebook is the story still waiting to be written.
