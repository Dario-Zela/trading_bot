Trump adds 50% tariffs on Canadian goods as Carney holds off retaliating

President Trump has used a dormant 1930s trade statute, Section 338, to impose the new Canadian tariffs. — The Trump White House / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Trump's 50% tariffs on Canadian autos, alcohol and dairy take effect August 19; Carney says retaliation is possible but won't move first.

The United States is set to impose 50% tariffs on close to $20 billion of Canadian imports from August 19, after President Donald Trump signed three proclamations on July 20 targeting motor vehicles, alcoholic beverages and dairy products. Ottawa's response so far has been to wait.

The products swept up read like a cross-section of Canadian export life — wine, cheese, hockey sticks, cement — and the tariffs stack on top of duties already in place, taking the effective rate on some goods well past 50%. Prime Minister Mark Carney has called the measures unwarranted, but has explicitly ruled out striking back before the deadline. That combination — a hard date, an unresolved negotiation and a government publicly withholding its hand — is what turns this from a trade-policy story into a pricing problem for markets.

The mechanism: a 1930s law revived

The White House is not using the tariff authorities that have dominated this year's trade fights. Instead it has reached for Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930, a provision that lets the president impose duties of up to 50% on countries found to discriminate against American commerce — and one that, by most trade lawyers' accounts, has not been invoked since the 1940s.

The administration's fact sheet lays out three specific grievances. On autos, it says Canada applies tariffs and quotas to U.S.-made vehicles that it does not apply to imports from other countries, and that American motor-vehicle exports to Canada fell roughly $5.6 billion, or 22%, over the past year. On alcohol, it points to a boycott: all but two Canadian provinces and territories halted purchases of U.S. spirits and wine after earlier trade disputes, cutting American alcohol exports to Canada by about $582 million, or 81%. On dairy, it argues Canada's tariff-rate quotas on U.S. cheese are markedly tighter than the quotas it grants the European Union.

Each grievance produces its own 50% tariff, covering a distinct set of goods — hence a list that runs from wine to cement to hockey sticks rather than a single blanket rate. Energy, potash, fish, critical minerals and goods already hit under Section 232 are carved out. The tariffs apply regardless of whether a product would otherwise qualify for USMCA preference, which is the detail that turns this from a symbolic gesture into a real cost line for importers with 30 days' notice to plan around.

Carney's calculated pause

Meeting Canada's 13 premiers in Charlottetown on July 23, Carney struck a deliberately unresolved tone. "Everything's on the table depending on the outcome of the negotiations," he told reporters, while declining to specify what retaliatory measures Canada might deploy. Asked directly about timing, he was blunter: "We don't need to respond in advance," he said, calling pre-emptive retaliation "counterproductive at this stage." His government, he added, is pursuing "a comprehensive agreement that addresses all tariff-related sectors" rather than a sector-by-sector scramble.

The premiers' meeting produced unity on message, if not on specifics — provincial leaders emerged describing themselves as aligned behind Ottawa, though several were candid that they see no guarantee of a deal before the deadline. Carney's calculation appears to be that keeping Canada's options undisclosed preserves leverage in the talks; the cost is that markets are left pricing a binary outcome — deal, partial deal, or tariffs plus an unknown Canadian response — with five weeks to resolve it.

What is already moving

Currency positioning got ahead of the announcement. CFTC data released the Friday before Trump's proclamations showed speculative net-short positions on the Canadian dollar at roughly $12.5 billion, the largest since December 2024 and enough to make the loonie the most heavily shorted major currency on the Chicago Mercantile Exchange, overtaking the yen. USD/CAD has pushed toward its 2025 high as the tariff threat has combined with softer Canadian inflation data, which has narrowed expectations for further Bank of Canada tightening and thinned out the yield support that had been underpinning the currency. The Bank of Canada has separately trimmed its 2026 growth forecast to 0.7% from 1.2%, citing trade uncertainty.

The asymmetry is worth noting: shorting the loonie is, as one trader put it, a bet without a central bank standing in the way — unlike short positions against the yen, which carry the risk of Bank of Japan intervention. That makes CAD a comparatively unimpeded proxy for tariff-deadline risk, and it is already trading that way.

What to watch

The five weeks to August 19 are the trading window. A negotiated deal — Carney's stated preference — would likely unwind some of the short positioning and firm up the Canadian dollar; a lapse into tariffs with no Canadian response would confirm the current pricing without adding fresh volatility; a lapse into tariffs followed by Canadian retaliation is the scenario markets have the least information about, since Ottawa has declined to specify targets. Wine, dairy and auto-adjacent equities on both sides of the border, along with USD/CAD spreads, are the instruments most directly exposed to whichever of those three paths materializes.
