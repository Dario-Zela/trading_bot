EU fines Google €890m under Digital Markets Act; Trump threatens tariff retaliation

Google's Mountain View headquarters. The EU's €890 million fine targets Search ranking practices and Play Store rules. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Brussels fined Google €890m under the DMA for search self-preferencing and Play Store restrictions; Trump answered with a Section 301 tariff threat against the EU.

Brussels has spent five years building the legal machinery to discipline Big Tech without asking Washington's permission. On Thursday it used that machinery for the first time against Google, and within a day Donald Trump had turned the case into a trade dispute.

The European Commission fined Alphabet's Google €890 million — roughly $1 billion — for breaching the Digital Markets Act, the EU's 2022 rulebook governing so-called "gatekeeper" platforms. It is the first DMA non-compliance decision against Google and the largest since the law took effect. Google has 60 days to fix the underlying conduct or face periodic penalties of up to 5% of its average daily worldwide turnover — a figure that, at Alphabet's scale, would run into the tens of millions of dollars per day.

Within hours, Trump said he would pursue "a substantial TARIFF" on the EU in retaliation, framing the fine as the confiscation of American corporate earnings by a foreign regulator. The exchange sets up a test of whether the DMA can function as intended — as a standing enforcement regime — or whether every finding against a US platform now comes bundled with a trade threat.

What Google actually did

The Commission split the penalty into two findings. The larger piece, €460 million, addresses self-preferencing in Google Search: the company ranked its own shopping, hotel, flight and local-transport results above rivals', a practice EU competition chief Teresa Ribera said deprived users of the market's actual best options. "The best products should succeed because they're better, not because they're owned by the company running the search engine," Ribera said in announcing the decision. "Google has fallen short of effective compliance with the Digital Markets Act, and today we have taken decisive yet balanced enforcement action sanctioning these breaches."

The remaining €430 million concerns the Play Store, where the Commission found Google restricted app developers from telling users about cheaper offers available outside the store or steering them to external payment options. "European consumers have a right to be told by app developers where to sign up to the best offers, even when the app store owner does not get a cut," Ribera said.

Google has said it will likely appeal to the EU courts. Its public response cast the ruling as "product degradation driven by a small group of self-serving complainants," arguing that compliance would strip away real-time Search features — instant pricing, live availability for hotels and flights — that European users rely on, and weaken safety vetting inside Play. That argument didn't move markets much: Alphabet shares actually closed higher the day of the announcement, a sign investors are treating the fine as a cost of doing business in Europe rather than a threat to the underlying franchise. The company's last quarterly revenue exceeded $1 billion roughly every eight hours.

The tariff threat

What changed the story's shape was Trump's response, posted within a day of the Commission's announcement. He said the fine amounted to the EU "ROBBING" an American company and, by extension, American taxpayers, and directed his trade team to open a Section 301 investigation — the same statute, dating to the Trade Act of 1974, that he has increasingly leaned on this year after the Supreme Court struck down the tariff authority he'd been using under the emergency-powers statute. Section 301 lets the US Trade Representative investigate foreign practices deemed unreasonable or discriminatory and, if the president acts on the findings, impose retaliatory duties.

USTR Jamieson Greer echoed the complaint in blunter terms: "It is clear the EU continues to target the most competitive U.S. companies," he said, adding that Brussels's recent enforcement actions were "creating massive uncertainty for U.S. exports" and undermining efforts at constructive dialogue. Trump has made the same argument before, about EU cases against Apple and Amazon; this is the first time the DMA specifically has drawn a tariff threat rather than a diplomatic complaint.

A Section 301 case is not a tariff. It requires an investigation, findings, and — historically — a comment period before any duty takes effect, so nothing changes at the border this week. But the threat lands on ground that is not empty. The US and EU agreed a trade framework in August 2025 that set a 15% US tariff on most European goods — down from the 30% rate Washington had threatened — in exchange for the EU dropping duties on US industrial goods and opening its market further to American agriculture. That deal was sold on both sides as a truce. Trump's Google response is the clearest signal yet that Washington regards it as revisable whenever a US company loses a European regulatory fight.

What the DMA fine was supposed to prove

The DMA was written explicitly to avoid the slow grind of traditional EU antitrust cases — the kind that took a decade and multiple appeals to produce the €4.1 billion Android fine the European Court of Justice upheld against Google earlier this year. Under the DMA, gatekeeper platforms get a compliance deadline and a fast, structured enforcement track; the Google case is the first full test of whether that track produces actual behavioral change rather than just periodic fines that a company the size of Alphabet can treat as a line item.

Google now has 60 days — into late September — to alter how Search ranks its own services and how Play treats developer links to outside offers, or the penalty clock restarts at up to 5% of daily global turnover. Ribera has previously framed the DMA period as a test of whether Brussels can compel structural change rather than simply collect fines after the fact; this case is the one regulators and platforms alike will use to judge that.

The tariff threat complicates that test without necessarily derailing it. Brussels has enforced against Apple and Meta under the same law this year without a comparable response from Washington; what's different here is the scale of the fine and the direct presidential attention. If Trump proceeds with a formal Section 301 filing, the timeline for any actual duties would likely stretch past Google's own 60-day compliance window — meaning the company's next move in Brussels and the White House's next move in Washington will probably arrive on separate clocks, not simultaneously.

What's next

Google's compliance deadline falls in late September; how it redesigns Search rankings and Play Store disclosures will determine whether the Commission considers the case closed or escalates to daily penalties. On the trade side, watch for whether USTR Greer's office formally opens the Section 301 investigation Trump referenced — that filing, not the social-media post, is the step that would start a clock toward actual tariffs. Markets so far are pricing this as noise: Alphabet's stock move on the day of the fine suggests investors expect the trade threat to follow the pattern of Trump's other 301 actions this year — loud opening, extended negotiation, and a resolution that arrives well after the news cycle has moved on.
