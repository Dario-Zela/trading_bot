Jobless claims sink to 1969 lows as Fed's Warsh holds hawkish line into July 29

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C., where the FOMC meets July 28–29. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Jobless claims sank to a 1969 low just as an oil shock from the Gulf conflict pushed Fed rate-hike odds for July 29 above one-in-three.

Thursday's initial jobless claims report was supposed to be a footnote ahead of next week's Federal Reserve meeting. Instead it landed as the headline. The Labor Department said new claims for unemployment benefits fell by 22,000 to a seasonally adjusted 187,000 in the week ending July 18 — the lowest weekly total since September 1969, when the U.S. population was roughly 60% smaller than it is today. Economists polled by FactSet had expected something close to 210,000.

The number arrives five days before the Fed's July 28–29 meeting, and it lands in the middle of an already tense debate: growth indicators have been softening for months, yet the labor market keeps refusing to crack. That tension is now colliding with a separate shock — a spike in oil prices tied to the widening Gulf conflict — and together they have done something markets did not expect two weeks ago: put a real, non-trivial probability of a rate hike back on the table.

A 57-year low, and what it actually means

The scale of the drop is not a rounding error. The four-week moving average, which smooths out weekly noise, fell 7,250 to 207,500 — itself a multi-decade low. Continuing claims, which capture people already receiving benefits, slipped to just under 1.8 million. Taken together, the data describes an economy where employers are still reluctant to let people go, even as headlines about AI-driven layoffs and slower hiring have piled up through the summer.

That is the awkward part for policymakers. A tight labor market is usually read as a wage-growth signal, and wage growth is the input the Fed watches most closely for whether inflation can actually get back to 2%. Claims this low don't prove wages are about to reaccelerate, but they remove one of the arguments — a cooling jobs market — that rate-cut advocates on the Federal Open Market Committee had been leaning on.

Warsh's line hardens

Fed Chair Kevin Warsh, who took over from Jerome Powell in May after Senate confirmation, has spent the weeks leading into this meeting trying to have it two ways rhetorically. In July 14 testimony to Congress, he pledged that elevated inflation "will be a thing of the past" and framed the AI investment boom as evidence the economy can grow without reigniting price pressure — a notably upbeat framing from a sitting Fed chair. Days later, in follow-up remarks, he tempered that: AI-related spending could still push some prices higher over the next year, he said, even if that effect proves temporary rather than genuinely inflationary.

Whatever the nuance, the operative message has stayed consistent: inflation is "too high," and anyone expecting the Fed to tolerate readings meaningfully above 2% "would be disappointed." That line matters because the committee itself is not unified behind it. Minutes from June's meeting showed the 18 policymakers roughly split down the middle — half comfortable holding or eventually cutting, half pushing for a hike before year-end. Governor Lisa Cook has pointed to inflation running at 3.7%; Vice Chair Philip Jefferson and Governor Christopher Waller have both said the committee would need to reconsider its stance if price growth doesn't cool from here. May's PCE reading came in at 4.1%, the hottest since April 2023, with core PCE at 3.4%, the highest since October 2023.

Oil adds a second shock

What has changed the market's math more than jobless claims alone is the Gulf escalation. Brent crude has surged more than 12% over the past week, touching levels above $100 a barrel, as Iran has moved to disrupt shipping through the Strait of Hormuz — a chokepoint for roughly a fifth of global oil flows — and Washington has weighed a naval response. A prolonged disruption of that scale, which the IEA has called unprecedented in oil-market history, feeds directly into headline inflation the same way the 2022 energy shock did, and it arrives just as core price pressure was already proving sticky.

The combined effect on rate expectations has been sharp. CME FedWatch pricing showed the odds of a 25-basis-point hike at the July meeting near 37–38% as of July 23, up from roughly 11% barely a week earlier — even as a hold at the current 3.50%–3.75% range, which would mark a fifth consecutive pause, remains the base case at somewhere near 62–63% probability. Futures markets have also nudged up the odds attached to September, treating that meeting as the more likely venue for any actual move if inflation data continues to run hot.

What happens next

The FOMC's decision lands July 29, alongside Warsh's press conference and an updated summary of economic projections — the dot plot will show whether the committee's internal split has hardened or narrowed since June. A hold is still the expected outcome; a hike this month would be a genuine surprise to most desks. But the gap between "expected" and "priced with real conviction" has narrowed measurably in the space of ten days, driven by a labor market that won't soften and an oil shock the Fed didn't choose. The next test comes fast: August's jobs report and PCE print will tell markets whether this week's data was a blip or the start of a pattern the Fed can no longer look past heading into September.
