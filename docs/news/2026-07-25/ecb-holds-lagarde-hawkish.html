ECB holds at 2.25% but Lagarde leaves door open to a September hike

ECB President Christine Lagarde, whose July press conference left the door open to a September rate hike. — European Central Bank / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The ECB held its deposit rate at 2.25% on 23 July but Lagarde signalled a September hike is live if energy-driven inflation persists.

The European Central Bank left its deposit rate unchanged at 2.25% on Thursday, pausing for breath after June's surprise quarter-point increase — but President Christine Lagarde spent her press conference making clear the pause is not a pivot. Markets had priced better than a 99% chance of a hold going in, and got one. What they didn't get was the dovish exit some had been betting on.

The Governing Council's vote was unanimous, Lagarde said, though she volunteered that some Eurosystem governors had "asked themselves" whether another hike was warranted right now. That detail did more work than any forward guidance: a unanimous hold with visible internal debate about hiking is not the same signal as a unanimous hold full stop, and traders read it accordingly. The euro slipped against the dollar to around 1.1408 in the aftermath, extending a broader dollar rally that had already pushed the greenback to a 40-year high against the yen.

The energy math hasn't finished

June's hike — the ECB's first since 2023 — was itself a break from the developed-world disinflation script, forced through by the energy shock radiating from the conflict in the Middle East and the disruption around the Strait of Hormuz. The Bank lifted its 2026 inflation forecast to an average of 3%, with a return to target not expected until the second half of 2027.

Eurozone inflation actually eased to 2.8% in June, down from 3.2% in May, which is the number the rate-cut crowd has been pointing to. Lagarde pointed the other way: to Brent crude, which she noted had surged more than 6% toward $100 a barrel even as she was speaking, and to a ceasefire in the region she described as "short-lived," broken "several times." Her framing was blunt — "the full inflationary impact of the energy shock has yet to play out" — and she flagged the risk that sustained high energy costs feed into wages and broader prices through second-round effects the data hasn't shown yet, but hasn't ruled out either.

Asked directly whether September was live, Lagarde didn't hedge: "the burden of proof is on data." She said ECB staff have been instructed to run fresh scenarios on oil and gas prices ahead of the next meeting on September 10, by which point the Council will have two more inflation prints and updated growth figures in hand. ING was among the desks that labelled Thursday's outcome a "hawkish hold" — a hold in name, a hike warning in substance.

For a market that spent much of the year positioned for a synchronized global easing cycle, that is an awkward wrinkle. The ECB is not alone in facing this: with US jobless claims at 1969 lows and the Fed's Warsh holding a hawkish line into month-end, and the Bank of England's own hike risk still being priced out ahead of its Super Thursday meeting, the divergence in central-bank paths this summer owes less to domestic demand than to a single shared variable — the price of oil. September 10 is now the date that matters most for EUR positioning; two inflation reports between now and then will decide whether Lagarde's warning becomes a vote.
