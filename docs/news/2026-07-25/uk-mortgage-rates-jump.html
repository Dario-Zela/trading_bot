UK mortgage rates jump as Middle East oil shock feeds through to gilts

The Bank of England has held its base rate at 3.75%, but gilt yields — and mortgage pricing — have moved sharply without it. — David Iliff / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
UK two- and five-year mortgage rates posted their biggest daily rise since April as a Houthi-driven oil spike pushed gilt yields above 5%.

Two- and five-year fixed mortgage rates in the UK jumped by their sharpest daily margin since April on Friday, after a fresh escalation in the Middle East sent oil prices and gilt yields lurching higher within hours. For a market that algorithmic pricing desks like to treat as a slow-moving function of central bank policy, the move was a reminder that it isn't just the Bank of England that sets the price of a mortgage anymore.

The average two-year fixed rate across all lenders reached 5.06% and the five-year average 5.14%, according to Uswitch data — both the highest levels in a month. Barclays, Halifax and HSBC had already begun repricing on Thursday, lifting selected fixed deals by as much as 0.20 percentage points; Barclays' EMC Reward two-year fix at 70% loan-to-value moved from 4.76% to 4.96% in a single update. Two-year swap rates, the wholesale funding cost lenders actually price against, climbed to 4.179% and five-year swaps to 4.26% — up from below 4% at the start of July.

The proximate cause was oil. Brent crude broke back above $100 a barrel this week for the first time since May after Iran-backed Houthi militants said they had struck two Saudi tankers in the Red Sea, widening a blockade that has already pushed exporters to reroute cargo away from a Strait of Hormuz effectively closed since the spring. Brent has since eased to around $98, but the 10-year gilt yield has held above 5% for its longest stretch since 2008, dragging fixed-rate mortgage pricing with it.

"The sharp rise in swap rates, driven by heightened geo-political tensions and the escalating conflict in Iran, has prompted lenders to reprice," said Hina Bhudia of Knight Frank Finance. Katy Eatenton of Eatenton Finance put it more bluntly: "The mortgage price war ended just as quickly as the ceasefire."

Two sources of the same shock

What makes this repricing awkward for pricing models is that oil is not the only thing moving gilts this week. Ten-year yields also rose eight basis points on Andy Burnham's first days as prime minister, after his chancellor, John Healey, signalled he would seek "flexibility" in the fiscal rules — a remark that pushed 30-year yields to 5.75%, their highest since May. Geopolitical risk and domestic fiscal risk are arriving in the gilt market at the same time, from different directions, and swap desks are pricing both into mortgage funding costs without much ability to separate one from the other. Ben Perks of Orchard Financial Advisers summed up the mood for borrowers: "As long as war rages, mortgage rates will be turbulent."

That conflation is the blind spot worth naming. Rate models built to track Bank Rate and inflation prints handle scheduled central-bank decisions well; they are less suited to a week in which a tanker attack in the Red Sea and a chancellor's press conference move the same yield curve within 48 hours.

The next scheduled test is the Bank of England's own meeting, where markets have been paring back expectations of a hike even as gilt yields drift higher without it. Until then, brokers expect lenders to keep repricing daily rather than weekly — a pace that tracks the news out of the Gulf more closely than anything from Threadneedle Street.
