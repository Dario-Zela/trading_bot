Burnham's zero-VAT energy move collides with jittery gilt markets

In one sentence
Burnham's £850m zero-VAT electricity move lands as gilt yields hit multi-month highs, exposing the fiscal tightrope facing new Chancellor John Healey.

Two numbers tell the story of Andy Burnham's first ten days in Downing Street. The first is £850m — what it will cost the Treasury to strip VAT off household electricity bills from October. The second is 5.1% — where the 10-year gilt yield sits after climbing to its highest level since May, on its longest run above 5% since July 2008. One number is a modest, symbolic gesture to cost-of-living pressure. The other is the market's verdict on the government's capacity to pay for gestures like it.

A £45 saving, and a funding gap already under scrutiny

From 1 October, VAT on domestic electricity supplies falls from 5% to zero, a temporary measure running to the end of the 2026-27 financial year. The Treasury puts the saving at roughly £45 a year for a typical household — about 87p a week — and has costed the policy at £850m for the year. It applies only to residential electricity, not gas and not commercial supply, a distinction officials say reflects the government's wider push to electrify heating and transport rather than subsidise fossil consumption broadly.

Energy suppliers, for what it's worth, welcomed it without much reservation. Centrica chief executive Chris O'Shea called it "encouraging to see the new Prime Minister move quickly to help households," and confirmed British Gas would pass the reduction straight through, while noting he'd still prefer a targeted social tariff over a blanket cut: "Would I rather we had more targeted support? I would... But we should applaud the move." That ambivalence is the more telling reaction. A flat-rate VAT cut benefits every household equally regardless of income, and Cornwall Insight has already warned that the saving risks being swallowed by the next Ofgem price cap move, expected to rise around 5% in October on the back of firm wholesale gas prices — an annualised increase of roughly £93 that would more than offset the tax relief.

The financing has drawn sharper criticism than the policy itself. Downing Street says the £850m is covered by scrapping the previous government's Digital ID programme, budgeted at £1.8bn over three years. But the Office for Budget Responsibility has pointed out that Digital ID was never actually funded in the first place — cancelling an unfunded commitment frees up nothing — and separate costings suggest the scheme's real near-term spend was closer to £600m, leaving a shortfall of around £250m against the £850m bill. For a chancellor who has spent his first week insisting on fiscal discipline, that is an awkward opening arithmetic problem, and it is precisely the kind of loose costing that gilt investors have been pricing in all week.

Gilts price in the skepticism

The electricity VAT cut landed the same week bond markets delivered their first real verdict on the Burnham government. The Prime Minister told reporters he intends to stick to the fiscal rules but would use "any flexibility" within them — a phrase that, rather than reassuring investors, triggered one of the sharper gilt selloffs of the year. The 10-year yield jumped roughly 8 basis points on the remarks alone and has held above 5% for its longest stretch since 2008, touching a multi-month high near 5.1%; the 30-year yield climbed to 5.78%, a two-month high. Britain's borrowing costs are now the highest in the G7.

Some of that move is not really about Burnham at all — Brent crude has been trading near $100 a barrel amid the widening Gulf conflict around the Strait of Hormuz, and global bond markets more broadly have been unsettled by inflation and debt concerns this month. But traders and analysts have been explicit that the new government is compounding, not just riding, the volatility: Bloomberg described gilts as caught between "twin risks" from the Burnham debut and the oil shock, with uncertainty over the incoming administration's spending intentions leaving UK debt at a particular disadvantage even against that noisy backdrop. An £850m giveaway funded by contested arithmetic, arriving days after a market-rattling comment about fiscal "flexibility," is not the confidence-building combination a chancellor would choose if he had the option.

Healey's arithmetic problem is much bigger than £850m

That chancellor is John Healey, appointed by Burnham in a move that startled Westminster as much as it startled the bond desks. Healey had just resigned as defence secretary, rejecting a delayed military spending plan he said "falls well short" of what Britain needs against a plan that would have lifted defence spending to 2.68% of GDP by 2030 — a stance that also cost the government its armed forces minister, Al Carns, who quit in solidarity. Handing the Treasury to the cabinet's most prominent internal critic of spending restraint was read, initially, as a signal that Burnham intends to loosen the purse strings.

Healey's early public statements point the other way. "Fiscal control is the first duty of any Chancellor," he told Treasury staff, adding that he and Burnham had "talked about how we will do that in lockstep, ensuring there's a buffer also for uncertainty." That leaves him squeezed from both directions at once: a defence establishment he himself just quit to defend, a welfare lobby pressing for its own increases, and a set of fiscal rules — borrow only to invest, fund current spending from tax revenue — that he has publicly recommitted to rather than relaxed. He cannot simply spend his way out of the contradiction, which is what makes the electricity VAT cut, small as it is, a useful early tell: relief has gone to households, not to defence or welfare, and it has been financed through a creative accounting manoeuvre rather than new revenue or a rules change.

What ties it together

The VAT cut is the easy version of the choice Healey faces — a low-cost, high-visibility gesture that could be funded, however imperfectly, without touching the fiscal rules. Defence and welfare are the hard version, where the sums involved are an order of magnitude larger and no comparable accounting sleight is available. Gilt markets are not waiting for the autumn Budget to start pricing that in; the reaction to Burnham's "flexibility" comment shows investors are treating loose talk and loose costings as data points now, ahead of the formal event. The £850m question was whether Burnham would help households. The far larger question, which Healey now owns, is whether he can do that and fund defence and welfare commitments without a fiscal rules rewrite gilt investors would punish far harder than they've punished this week's remarks. The next marker is the autumn Budget and the OBR forecast that accompanies it — the first moment the government has to show its costings in full, rather than in press-release form.
