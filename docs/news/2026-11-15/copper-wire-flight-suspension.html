Copper wire upgrade behind US flight suspension, transport secretary says

In one sentence
Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy blames a copper-cable upgrade for a US flight suspension, but the mechanism and scale are unconfirmed, and his last explanation was disputed.

A telecoms fault grounded flights across part of the US, and the Transportation Secretary has put it down to the replacement of old copper cabling. That is the account being reported. Its main details are not independently confirmed: the date, the number of flights affected, the airports involved and the precise point of failure are all missing from the reporting available so far.

The claim is plausible, because it fits a pattern. It also invites caution, because the official explanation for these outages has already been disputed once.

A pattern, not a one-off

The Federal Aviation Administration has spent more than a year replacing copper lines with fibre. The work followed the 2025 breakdowns at Newark, where the radar system went offline at least three times in April and May. Officials at the time pointed to copper transmission as the cause. Fibre has since been installed at Newark, LaGuardia and Kennedy. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy has described the old system as limited by the speed of copper wires.

The programme is large. Verizon holds a 15-year, $2.4bn FAA contract, awarded in 2023, for the upgrade. A government report recently put the cost of the telecommunications work at $5.91bn, up from $4.75bn. Duffy has asked Congress for about $20bn more for the next phase of air traffic control modernisation. FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford has said the outdated copper should be fully replaced by September 2027.

A fault during a cutover is therefore unsurprising. Switching live air traffic data from one medium to another is delicate, and any step in the process can interrupt feeds to controllers. When that happens, the safe response is a ground stop.

Why the account needs testing

Two things argue against taking the explanation at face value.

First, the headline and the one-line description differ. One names a specific copper upgrade, the other a general infrastructure fault. Those are different failures. A planned cutover that went wrong is a project-management problem. A fibre cut or switch failure is a resilience problem, and the fix is different.

Second, the last comparable episode produced a contested story. In September 2026 a ground stop hit Northeast airports after a circuit failed at a Philadelphia facility and a fibre cable was cut near New Brunswick, New Jersey. Duffy blamed an Amtrak construction crew on X. Amtrak disputed that, and the sequence of events was not settled in the coverage found. A statement from the secretary is a starting point, and the technical findings usually come later from the FAA or the carrier.

No FAA or contractor statement confirming the mechanism has turned up in available reporting.

What it says about the system

Even taken at face value, the episode shows how much depends on shared infrastructure. Air traffic control draws on a mesh of leased lines, switches and radar feeds that were laid down over decades. One failing link, or one botched replacement, can halt traffic well beyond the site where it happened. Modernisation reduces that exposure over time. During the transition, it adds moments when the system is more fragile than before.

What to watch

The test is whether the FAA publishes a technical account naming the failed component, the site and the duration. Also worth watching is whether the Bedford timeline of September 2027 holds, and whether Congress approves the roughly $20bn Duffy wants. A second cutover-related stoppage would make the case that the transition itself is the risk.
