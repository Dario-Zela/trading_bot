Gymshark trademark fight draws criticism from small fitness sellers

In one sentence
A Cotswolds gym says Gymshark's 18-month UKIPO challenge to its registered name has cost it nearly £30,000; Gymshark declined to comment and no ruling has been reported.

A small gym in the Cotswolds is in a trademark dispute with Gymshark, the Solihull sportswear company valued at £1.25bn. The gym's owner says the cost of defending a name he registered about five years ago is threatening the business. Gymshark, which is privately held, declined to comment.

The case rests on a single report, in the Guardian, which is also the source for the figures below. It is one dispute at the UK Intellectual Property Office (UKIPO), not a ruling or a change in policy. No hearing dates have been reported.

The dispute

The Conditioning Club is a gym in Fairford, Gloucestershire, run by John Watson. By the Guardian's account it has nine staff, three self-employed trainers and about 500 members. Watson registered the name with the UKIPO for gym and fitness services roughly five years ago.

In March last year Gymshark applied to register "Conditioning Club" and "The Conditioning Club" for clothing, retail and entertainment services. Watson opposed the application. He also sought to extend his own registration into retail and related classes.

Gymshark has since opposed that extension and asked the UKIPO to invalidate his original registration altogether. Its argument is that the name is descriptive, generic and not distinctive enough to qualify for protection. The Guardian notes that Gymshark holds several registrations for "Lifting Club", a name that is arguably as generic.

The proceedings have run for almost 18 months. Watson puts his legal costs at nearly £30,000. A CrowdJustice appeal has raised just over £5,000. "They can afford a prolonged trademark battle," he said of Gymshark.

What the legal argument turns on

The dispute is a familiar one in trademark law. A mark that merely describes a service is harder to protect. A name that is distinctive is easier to protect. Gymshark's case is that "Conditioning Club" describes a type of fitness activity. Watson's case is that it functions as a name for his business. Whether it does is for the UKIPO to decide. Nothing in the reporting shows how the tribunal is likely to rule.

The forum is the point. UKIPO proceedings are cheaper than High Court litigation, but each side still pays for filings, evidence and representation. The side with the thinner budget can find itself deciding whether the name is worth the money before the merits are reached. That is the substance of the criticism in this case, and it is Watson's own account, not an independent finding.

Gymshark's wider record

Gymshark has also won cases when it was the one challenging. In the GymBull case, the UKIPO refused an application on bad-faith grounds, after Gymshark pointed to the applicant's earlier filings for marks such as "The South Face" and "Upper Armour". The tribunal did not find that consumers were likely to confuse GymBull with Gymshark. In January 2026 the Benelux trademark office refused the "Sharkys Gym" mark after Gymshark opposed it.

Those outcomes show a brand defending a name that resembles its own. The Conditioning Club dispute is different in kind. Gymshark is applying to register a name that a smaller business already holds, and challenging that business's registration.

What to watch

The next data point is a UKIPO decision on Gymshark's invalidation request, or a settlement. Neither has a reported date. Until a ruling arrives, the case shows how costly a trademark defence can be for a small business, and nothing yet about how the law will treat the names small fitness sellers use.
