Treasury yields climb as traders price Fed hikes in December and January

In one sentence
Citi now expects a 25bp Fed hike in December after hot September CPI, as traders price tightening in December and January and Treasury yields rise, awkwardly for Trump.

The Federal Reserve has already begun raising interest rates, and bond traders are betting it will keep going. Citigroup has joined that camp: after a hotter-than-expected September inflation report, the bank now forecasts a quarter-point hike at the December meeting. That sits uneasily with the premise of the easing cycle that policymakers, and the White House, had been expecting. Treasury yields have climbed as futures price tightening at both the December and January meetings.

The framing of a market that has "moved from pricing cuts to pricing hikes" needs one correction. That shift happened earlier in the autumn. The Fed lifted its target range by 25 basis points to 3.75%–4% on 16 September, its first increase since July 2023, ending five meetings without a move. What has changed since is the expected size of the cycle: one hike now looks like the start of a sequence rather than a one-off.

From a hold to a hike

Citigroup had been among the last major holdouts, forecasting no further Fed moves this year. That changed on 14 October, after the September consumer price index.

Headline inflation rose to 3.7% a year from 3.4% in August, against a consensus of 3.6%. The monthly gain was 0.6%, driven largely by gasoline. Core inflation, which strips out food and energy, rose to 2.6% from 2.4%, against an expected 2.5%, with a monthly gain of about 0.3% versus the 0.2% forecast.

The core figure is small in absolute terms but is the one that drew Citi's attention. Per the investinglive report of the call, the bank's concern is that inflation spreading beyond gasoline is harder for the Fed to look through than an energy spike alone. Citi expects a December hike, then a hold, with cuts not beginning until June 2027. Nathan Sheets, its chief economist, has since been quoted calling a December move essentially a done deal, and has tied rising longer-dated yields to government financing needs and heavy AI-related investment.

Other houses have moved the same way. After hot October inflation data, reports on 11 November said Deutsche Bank had added a January hike to its path, Goldman Sachs and Nomura had added a March increase, and Standard Chartered had said the case for a half-point December move had strengthened. These are secondary accounts of research notes, and the notes themselves were not reviewed for this article.

What the bond market is doing

The Treasury market had been signalling strain before the Fed acted. The 10-year yield crossed 5% on 14 September, the first time since 2023 on one account and the highest since 2007 on another, two days before the September decision. CME FedWatch odds of a September hike had risen to about 90% from roughly a third a month earlier.

The current pricing is harder to pin down. Reports on the October data put the market-implied probability of a December hike in a wide range, and the sources reviewed gave no clean 10-year or two-year quote for 13 November. Futures were said to be implying a further rise in yields after the October CPI. Precise levels should be taken from a live screen rather than from this article; what the reports agree on is direction: yields up, cuts priced out, and a January move now part of the discussion alongside December.

The mechanics are straightforward. A hike expected at two consecutive meetings lifts short-dated yields, which track the policy rate. Longer yields rise when investors demand more compensation for inflation and for the supply of government debt, a point Sheets has emphasised.

Who pays

Higher yields feed through the economy by several routes. Mortgage rates are priced off the 10-year Treasury, so a sustained move above 5% keeps home-loan costs elevated. Companies refinancing debt face higher coupons. The federal government, running large deficits, pays more to borrow, which in turn feeds the supply worries that push long yields up. Equity valuations, which rest on discounted future earnings, come under pressure as the discount rate rises.

The politics are awkward. After the September hike, President Trump said rates should be "1%, or less," according to press reports, and criticised the Fed board while backing Chair Kevin Warsh. The vote in September was unanimous. A second and third hike would extend the divergence between what the White House wants and what the committee, including a chair the president favours, has chosen to do.

What to watch

The September projections showed a median year-end policy rate of 4.1%, which implies one more quarter-point move this year, and the same median for 2027. A December hike would match that median; a January one would run ahead of it. Two things will decide how far markets are right. The first is the next CPI report, due before the December meeting, and in particular whether core inflation keeps rising beyond gasoline. The second is the Fed's December projections, which will show whether officials see the path as one more move or a longer campaign. Citi's own forecast of a hold followed by June 2027 cuts is a reminder that even the hawks expect the cycle to be short.
