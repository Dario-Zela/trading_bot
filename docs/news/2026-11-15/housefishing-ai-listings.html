'Housefishing': AI-enhanced property listings under scrutiny

In one sentence
AI-edited property photos may be going undisclosed in UK listings, and it is unclear whether existing consumer law is specific enough to catch the practice.

Buying a home in the UK usually starts with a photograph. Before a viewing is booked, before a surveyor is instructed, a buyer scrolls through a listing and decides whether the place is worth an afternoon. A practice that has picked up the label "housefishing" puts that first filter in doubt: the photos may have been altered by AI tools in ways the listing never mentions.

The term comes from the BBC Business report that prompted this piece. Its origin and how widely it is used are unverified, and the details of that report could not be independently checked for this article. What follows is therefore a description of the gap the story points to, not a catalogue of cases.

What the tools can do

Editing property photos is not new. Agents have long used wide-angle lenses, brightened skies and tidied rooms. What has changed is how little skill and time it takes. Generative tools can now swap a grey sky for blue, remove a pylon or a bin store from the frame, smooth a stained ceiling, or fill an empty room with furniture that is not there.

Some of this is openly sold as "virtual staging", and many listings label it as such. The concern is the edits that come without a label, or that alter fixed features of the property rather than its furnishings. A removed power line or a repaired-looking wall is not a styling choice. It is a claim about the house.

The consumer-protection question

UK consumer law already prohibits misleading commercial practices, including misleading presentation of a product. The Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 carried these rules forward and gave the Competition and Markets Authority direct enforcement powers, including fines. Estate agents are covered as traders, and Trading Standards enforces the rules locally. The Property Ombudsman's code of practice also expects agents to describe properties accurately.

None of that was written with generative imagery in mind, and the open question is whether it is specific enough. Existing law turns on whether a buyer was misled into a decision they would not otherwise have made. That test is hard to apply to a photo. A buyer who travels to a viewing and finds a different house has lost a day and some petrol, not obviously a transaction. A buyer who makes an offer on the strength of retouched images has lost far more, but proving the link is another matter.

The gap, then, is less about legality than about visibility. No rule that this article could confirm requires an agent to disclose that an image has been AI-edited, and buyers have no easy way to tell. Whether the BBC's reporting shows widespread undisclosed edits or a handful of cases is the fact that would settle how serious the problem is, and it is not established here.

What to watch

The practical test is whether regulators or industry bodies act. Options include guidance from the CMA or Trading Standards on edited listing images, a disclosure requirement in the Property Ombudsman's code, or portals such as Rightmove and Zoopla requiring edited images to be flagged. Any of these would be a small, cheap change. Until one appears, the photo remains a marketing document that buyers are inclined to treat as evidence.
