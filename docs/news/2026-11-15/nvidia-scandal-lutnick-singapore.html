Nvidia scandal widens, pressuring Lutnick and testing Singapore

In one sentence
Reports that Commerce Secretary Lutnick was warned about Nvidia chief Jensen Huang and delayed listing a Singapore firm have cost Nvidia $430bn in value, but much remains unverified.

The Commerce Secretary, Howard Lutnick, is under growing pressure over reports that he was warned about Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang's relationship with a woman now suspected of helping divert advanced chips to China, and that his department has held back action against a Singapore-based company tied to her. Most of the detail rests on anonymous sourcing, and several key claims are unconfirmed. The market has nonetheless priced the story: Nvidia lost about $430 billion in market value over two sessions, according to Reuters.

What is alleged

The reports centre on Alice Huang, also known as Huang Le, and Mr Huang's relationship with her. Mr Huang, 63, has told employees he was unfaithful to his wife but has given no further details. He apologised to senior executives on Monday 10 November.

The Information reported that Ms Huang, her husband and her sister built a network of companies in China and Singapore to acquire Nvidia chips. When US export-restricted orders stalled, the report says, she used her Nvidia ties to move about 240,000 H100, H200 and some A100 processors into China. Some allegedly reached Alibaba, Tencent and smaller Chinese AI labs. Other reports put the value of the shipments at no less than $7.5 billion.

Reuters said it could not locate Ms Huang or independently verify these accounts. China's foreign ministry calls the spying accusations "groundless". The FBI is reportedly examining whether Mr Huang was targeted by a foreign intelligence operation. Nvidia's independent directors have hired Joon Kim, a Wachtell Lipton partner and former acting US Attorney for the Southern District of New York, to lead an inquiry. Nvidia says the board has no reason to believe national security or company secrets were compromised, and that Mr Huang has committed to cooperating.

The Lutnick question

Axios, citing sources, reported that Mr Lutnick was sent a highly classified memo warning about Mr Huang's relationship with Ms Huang. Reuters could not verify this, and no official has publicly confirmed the memo's contents or when it arrived.

The second thread is more concrete. The Financial Times reported that several US agencies pushed to add Megaspeed International, the Singapore cloud company of which Ms Huang became controlling shareholder, to the Commerce Department's entity list. Commerce has delayed, and the company is not listed. Megaspeed is linked to China-based Century Litong, which gained preferred cloud-partner status with Nvidia in 2023. Other reports say Mr Lutnick was worried that tighter restrictions would start a trade war with China; those accounts are also unconfirmed.

The politics have followed. Senate Democratic Leader Chuck Schumer demanded a full accounting of President Trump's defence of Mr Huang and called for quick approval of new chip export restrictions, saying: "He's supposed to protect America, not China, not his friends." Mr Trump said Mr Huang told him he had "absolutely no idea" the woman was a spy, and that he believed him. The New York Times reported that Secretary of State Marco Rubio briefed Mr Trump on intelligence about Mr Huang aboard Air Force One in May, at least the second such briefing in seven months; a White House official disputed that account.

The pressure on Mr Lutnick is therefore of two kinds: what he knew, and why a listing that agencies wanted has not happened. Only the second can be tested against the public record, by whether Megaspeed is added to the list.

Singapore's exposure

Singapore has been here before. In 2025 its authorities charged three people, two Singaporeans and one Chinese national, with fraud linked to the procurement and shipment of high-performance servers, and US senators later wrote to Commerce disputing Mr Huang's earlier claims that no chips had been diverted. Megaspeed puts the city-state back in the frame. Its appeal to chipmakers is a business-friendly regime and a dense data-centre sector; the cost is that every transshipment allegation raises questions about enforcement.

No Singaporean regulator action in this case has been established in the material reviewed here, and the company's own account has not been checked. The test for Singapore is whether it is seen to act on its own initiative or only after Washington moves.

What to watch

Three items will settle most of this. The first is whether Commerce adds Megaspeed to the entity list, which would answer the delay allegation directly. The second is the findings of the board inquiry led by Mr Kim, and any FBI conclusion on whether Mr Huang was targeted. The third is Congress: the push for new export restrictions on China, and whether Mr Lutnick is called to say what he received and when. Nvidia's shares, roughly flat for the year before the news and down 9.5% since, were already carrying doubts about whether heavy AI spending will pay off. Governance questions around its founder add a separate discount.
