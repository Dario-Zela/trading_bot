Government to consider changing age of consent after Russell Brand allegations

In one sentence
Ministers say they will examine the age-of-consent law after an alleged victim in the Russell Brand allegations called for reform, but no scope or timetable has been published.

Ministers have said they will examine the law on the age of consent, after an alleged victim in the allegations against the comedian and broadcaster Russell Brand called publicly for reform. Early reports, from the Guardian and the BBC, describe the commitment as a stated intention to consider change. They do not describe a formal review with terms of reference, a timetable or a named minister in charge.

That gap matters. "Consider" and "examine" are among the most elastic verbs in Whitehall. They can precede a full statutory review with evidence sessions, or a short exchange of letters that ends with the law unchanged. On what has been published so far, it is not possible to say which this will be.

What is known, and what is not

The core claim is narrow: an alleged victim in the Brand matter has argued that the law should change, and the government has said it will look at the question. The reporting reviewed for this article does not specify which part of the law is under consideration. It could be the age threshold itself, or the way the law treats relationships involving a large age gap or a position of influence. It also does not make clear which jurisdiction's rules are meant. In England and Wales the age of consent is 16, and the law already sets a higher bar where the older person is in a position of trust. Whether ministers are looking at either of those provisions has not been established.

The allegations against Brand are exactly that. He has denied allegations of sexual misconduct. Nothing in the material behind this article suggests any finding against him, and the proposed policy review should not be read as a verdict on the case or as dependent on how it ends. A government can examine whether a law is adequate without any conclusion about an individual, and the two tracks should be kept apart.

Why the policy question stands on its own

The age of consent is a long-settled line in most legal systems, and changing it is politically fraught. Arguments for reform tend to fall into two camps. One holds that the number itself is too low and that adolescents need stronger protection from much older partners. The other holds that the number is sound but that the surrounding law, covering power imbalance, grooming and coercive influence, needs sharpening. Child-protection charities and criminal lawyers have tended to prefer targeted changes to the surrounding framework over moving the headline threshold, though positions vary and none have been confirmed in this instance.

Any serious review would also have to weigh the risk of criminalising consensual relationships between teenagers, which is why the offence structure around the threshold is as important as the threshold.

What to watch

The first test is whether the government publishes terms of reference, or names a department or minister to lead the work. Ministerial answers in Parliament, or a written statement, would show whether the review covers the threshold, the trust-and-influence provisions, or both. Without those details, the announcement remains an intention rather than a policy.
