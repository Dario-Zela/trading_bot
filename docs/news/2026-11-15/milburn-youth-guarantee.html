Milburn review proposes a legal guarantee of a job or training for young people

In one sentence
Alan Milburn's youth review will recommend a legal guarantee of a job, training or support for young people, paid for by shifting spending away from benefits, with no published cost yet.

Britain's government is days away from receiving a proposal that would change what the state owes its young people. Alan Milburn, the former Labour health secretary who has spent the past year examining why so many under-25s are neither working nor studying, is recommending a legal guarantee of a job, training or employment support. The final report is due on Tuesday. Milburn set out its central idea in advance on the BBC's Sunday with Laura Kuenssberg.

The idea matters because of the word "legal". Britain has run youth job schemes before, and they have mostly been temporary programmes that ministers could close when budgets tightened. Milburn argues that is why they failed to last. A statutory duty would be different. The state would become answerable for a result, and a young person who was left without a route into work or training could in principle hold it to account.

What is being proposed

Milburn calls it an "opportunity guarantee". It would place a duty on government to ensure that young people have access to a route into employment. He compares it to the duties councils already carry to house or advise homeless people, or to accommodate children in care. Those are obligations that exist whether or not a given year's budget is generous.

The guarantee would come with conditions. Young people who refuse the help on offer could lose their benefits, Milburn said.

The problem it addresses is large. According to the latest Office for National Statistics data, almost one million young people in the UK are not in education, employment or training, the group known as NEETs. The government already spends about £22bn a year supporting economically inactive young people, a sum Milburn says is going to the wrong place.

Where the money would come from

The fiscal case is the part that will concern the Treasury. Milburn's argument is about rebalancing spending rather than adding to it. He says the state currently spends 25 times as much on benefits for young people as on helping them into work, and he wants that ratio brought down to 2:1.

"The pendulum's swung far too far from the one to the other — £25 to one," he told the BBC, adding that "we've got to be paying out less in benefits" to fund investment.

He acknowledged that some young people would lose out. He has already said that under-25s should no longer be able to claim the personal independence payment, or the health element of Universal Credit. The proposal sits alongside other recommendations to be published on Tuesday.

No cost estimate for the reforms has been published in the coverage so far. That is the weak point. A legal guarantee is an open-ended commitment: its cost depends on how many young people qualify and what the state must provide for each. A shift of money from benefits to support only balances if the benefit savings materialise and the support works. Milburn's case rests on the claim that keeping a young person on benefits costs far more over time than supporting them into work. Whether the Treasury accepts that, and how it scores a statutory duty, will decide how far the idea goes.

Politics and reaction

Prime Minister Andy Burnham called the report a "watershed moment" but declined to give a "blanket assurance" that he would accept everything in it. Speaking at the Welsh Labour conference on Saturday, he pointed to a rise in what he called "youth detachment" since the pandemic. Paying for reform, he said, means "spending differently", not "more money". He also said the government is considering a vision of schooling "not focused solely on the university route", including technical education.

The government is expected broadly to welcome the report. A statement to Parliament is due, with draft legislation early next year.

Reactions have split in familiar ways, and not only along party lines:

  • Lord Blunkett, a former Labour home secretary, said benefit savings should go to employment support, at least in the immediate future.
  • The TUC called the legal guarantee a "game changer" and described the NEET figures as a "national emergency". It warned that extra youth support should not be taken from other public service budgets, calling a zero-sum approach "counter-productive".
  • Kemi Badenoch, the Conservative leader, said government should not spend "lots of time" on employment schemes, which she said would ultimately fail. Her party and Reform UK want significant welfare cuts, partly to fund defence.
  • Robert Jenrick, Reform UK's economy spokesman, called the review "pointless" without immediate action.
  • Zack Polanski, the Green leader, said it was "disappointing" that the review reached "the same tired conclusions", and criticised using benefit sanctions to push young people into insecure work.
  • The Liberal Democrats want a 100-hour work experience guarantee and a careers coach for every young person.

What to watch

Three things will show how serious this is. The first is Tuesday's report itself, in particular whether it puts a figure on the guarantee and on the benefit savings meant to fund it. The second is the government's statement to Parliament, which will show whether ministers accept the statutory element or only the spending shift. The third is the draft legislation promised for early next year. Burnham's insistence on "spending differently" gives little room for a new recurring cost, and it falls to the Treasury to decide whether a legal duty fits that promise. It also competes with other fiscal pressures, from welfare reviews to defence.
