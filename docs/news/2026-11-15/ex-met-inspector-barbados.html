Ex-Met inspector lied about wife's death in Barbados hotel room, BBC learns

In one sentence
A BBC investigation says ex-Met inspector Warren Arter gave shifting accounts of his wife's 2024 death in Barbados, raising questions about scrutiny of officers who leave the force.

A former Metropolitan Police detective inspector gave shifting accounts of how his wife died in a Barbados hotel room, according to a BBC investigation. Warren Arter, who was under investigation over his conduct towards vulnerable women, was found dead in prison days after her death. The case raises a question about how far British policing keeps hold of an officer once he has left the service. For now it is the conduct of one man, not a finding about the force as a whole.

The death in Barbados

Rebekah Arter, 47, died in a room at the O2 Beach Club in Barbados in June 2024. Her husband gave different versions of what had happened. The BBC says he told staff and relatives that she had choked, that she had fallen and hit her head, or that he had woken to find her dead. He told his daughter-in-law they had both been asleep for 18 hours. He also suggested to friends and family that her drink had been spiked. A security guard said his explanation of how she was found kept changing.

The Barbadian response was thin. Police there said they found no marks of violence and did not order toxicology tests, because they believed she had died naturally. Evidence was not secured and key forensic tests were not carried out. Arter was briefly detained and released. Barbadian authorities gave the cause of death as aspiration pneumonitis.

A UK inquest led by the coroner Andrew Harris did not accept that finding. It heard that a Barbados pathologist had "chopped up" Mrs Arter's organs, which a UK pathologist said served no medical purpose and destroyed vital evidence. Mr Harris raised the possibility of "deliberate concealment or obstruction of further investigation". He recorded an open verdict, said her death probably involved intoxication, and said unlawful killing could not be ruled out. The inquest did not examine the allegations of abuse of other women.

What the Met knew

Four days after the death, the family reported their concerns at Bexleyheath police station, where Arter had once led rape investigations. A Gold Commander was briefed that evening. The Met did not send detectives to Barbados. It said it had considered travelling but judged it inappropriate without an allegation of criminality. Commander Simon Messinger said the force's thoughts remained with the family and urged victims of sexual offences to come forward.

That decision is the part most likely to draw scrutiny, though the BBC's material does not by itself show the force ignored a known danger. Arter had left the Met by then. He was dismissed in 2023 for drug offences, after about seven years on suspension while still receiving his full inspector's salary. The Independent Office for Police Conduct had investigated his dealings with eight alleged victims. He was never convicted.

The backstory makes the questions sharper. Mrs Arter first came to the Met as a domestic abuse complainant. Arter investigated her case in 2006, then began a relationship with her and moved in soon after. The BBC says he abused her for more than a decade. Around 2009 he was put in charge of a unit in south-east London investigating rape cases.

Unanswered questions

A week after his wife's death, Arter was arrested at Heathrow over alleged sexual offences against another woman, whose case he had investigated in 2010. He was found dead in HMP Wandsworth three days later. With no trial, the allegations were never tested in court.

The reporting does not address how Arter was vetted or promoted, and it does not show that anyone in the force knew of a risk to his wife. The accountability question rests on narrower ground: whether a forces' duty of scrutiny ends when an officer is dismissed, and whether a long suspension on full pay is a sensible way to handle serious allegations.

The next test is whether the IOPC or the Met reviews its handling of the case, and whether the coroner's findings prompt any wider action. A prevention of future deaths report has been published on the Rebekah Arter case on the judiciary website, which suggests the official record is still being built.
