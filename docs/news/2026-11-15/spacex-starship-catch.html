SpaceX catches part of Starship on return to Earth

SpaceX's Starship returns from orbit and is captured by the launch tower's arms at Starbase, Texas. — AP Photo/Eric Gay
In one sentence
SpaceX caught a Starship ship, not just a booster, after 14 orbits, a first; the booster failed, and whether the ship can fly again cheaply is still open.

SpaceX has caught a returning Starship upper stage in the arms of its launch tower, the first time a spacecraft of this size has been recovered at its launch site after a trip to orbit. The ship landed back at Starbase, Texas, about 22 hours after launching on Friday evening. The booster that launched it did not make it back. The headline's wording, "part of Starship", undersells what happened. This was the ship, not the booster, and that is the reusability step that had not been done before.

What was caught, and what was not

Starship has two stages. The Super Heavy booster does the first few minutes of the climb. The ship on top carries on into orbit. SpaceX first caught a booster with the tower's mechanical arms, nicknamed "chopsticks", on 13 October 2024 during Flight 5. (Triage notes for this piece gave the year as 2025. Contemporary reports put it in 2024.) Catching boosters has since become something SpaceX can repeat: the Associated Press counts three earlier successes.

The ship is a harder problem. It comes back from orbital speed through the atmosphere and has to survive the heat on re-entry. It then has to arrive at a precise spot, above a tower, under control. On Flight 5 the ship splashed down in the Indian Ocean as planned and was not recovered.

This flight was the 15th full-scale Starship launch since 2023 and only the second to reach orbit. According to AP, the ship circled Earth 14 times at about 265 km (165 miles) altitude, against one or two laps on the previous orbital mission. It also released 26 advanced Starlink satellites. SpaceX said everything on the ship, including its heat shield, appeared to work well. Commentator Dan Huot greeted the catch with "Look at her! Baby, she's back!" Elon Musk called it an "epic day". NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman said it was "almost magic".

The booster failure

The day was not a clean sweep. SpaceX had planned to catch both stages and fell short. The booster's engines shut down too early on the way down. Its guidance system judged it was out of position and triggered a self-destruct over the Gulf of Mexico. Wreckage remains in the water, cordoned off by buoys, and engineers are still investigating the cause.

That matters for the cost argument. Reuse only lowers costs if recovery is routine. A booster that has worked three times and failed once is still a hardware programme, not an airline schedule.

New step or incremental?

The sources do not settle how large a step this is, and that was the open question going into the flight. There are two readings.

The first is that it is a genuine new step. SpaceX officials say the catch demonstrates full reusability, and that is plausible on its face. Full reusability means both stages return and fly again, and the ship is the stage that had never come back. A ship that survives 14 orbits and re-entry, then lands within reach of the tower arms, answers the largest technical doubt about the design.

The second is that one catch proves a capability, not an economy. Nothing in the coverage says the caught ship will be refurbished and relaunched, or how quickly. The cost advantage comes from turnaround: how much inspection and repair the heat shield needs, and how many flights each vehicle can make. None of those figures has been published. The booster failure on the same flight shows the recovery process is not yet dependable.

The market angle in the original candidate rests on investment commentary rather than reporting. A Simply Wall St piece is not evidence about launch costs. What can be said is that cheaper launch is the main lever for Starlink deployment, and a ship that can be reused is the route to it. The prices and cadence that would show it are not yet visible.

What to watch

The next test is whether this ship flies again, and how long the gap is. SpaceX expects to begin launching Starships from Kennedy Space Center in Florida within the next few months. The findings on the booster shutdown will show whether the stage that had looked routine has a design flaw.

NASA's plans also depend on the vehicle. Artemis III is now slated to stay in Earth orbit and practise docking with Starship and Blue Origin's Blue Moon lander. NASA is targeting Artemis IV, as soon as 2028, for the first crewed lunar landing since Apollo. Those dates depend on how quickly Starship can go from a spectacular catch to a repeatable one.
