Bank of England's Pill says further rate rises likely in 2027

In one sentence
Pill's checkable remarks leave further 2027 hikes conditional on energy-driven second-round effects and reject a 2022-style cycle; the FT framing is unconfirmed.

The Bank of England's chief economist has been cast in two ways this week: as a policymaker who expects more rate rises in 2027, and as one who rules out a repeat of the 2022 tightening cycle. The reporting that can be checked points firmly to the second reading. The first, which rests on an interview with the Financial Times, could not be confirmed, and the Financial Times text was not retrievable.

What the Bank did

On 6 November the Bank raised Bank Rate to 4.00% from 3.75%. That ended a pause of more than three years. Huw Pill, the Bank's chief economist and a member of the Monetary Policy Committee, had been arguing for the move while most of his colleagues preferred to hold. In 2022 the Bank delivered eight consecutive increases, and that history shapes how every new hike is read.

Pill's remarks around the decision, as carried in a Press Association report, addressed that comparison directly.

"There is a lot of focus around whether this is the start of a more prolonged series of rate increases."

His answer was a conditional one. "The magnitude of the shock we've seen is smaller than in 2022," he said, and the 'key message' was that 'as we move into next spring, the future path of rates will become more uncertain.'

Two readings of one message

The headline version, that further rises are likely in 2027, finds only partial support in the record. Pill did say that further hikes could come into next year if higher energy costs feed into wages and prices. He said markets expect three more increases, and that the risks to that outlook run both ways.

The more emphatic quotes cut the other way. investingLive's summary of his remarks has him saying: "We are not going to repeat [the] 2022 rate hiking cycle." It also has him saying there is "nothing baked in for interest rates next year", and that it is "possible that the bank rate would stay on hold or even fall if we saw stalling of growth next year." The same account describes the decision to raise as "preemptive in nature", with "limited evidence of second-round effects" so far.

The two accounts are less contradictory than they look. Both describe a conditional position. Pill sees a risk of tightening if energy-driven inflation becomes embedded, and he declines to commit to a campaign. The unverified FT framing, as relayed in triage, may have emphasised the first half. Without the original text, whether he used the word 'likely' cannot be established, and the piece should not be read as a forecast from him.

The pricing figure that circulates is the market's own, not his. Traders put the chance of a December increase at around 50% and total tightening by the end of 2027 at about 68 basis points. That is roughly the three further quarter-point moves Pill himself acknowledged markets were expecting.

What would settle it

Pill named the evidence he will watch. 'Crucially we will see more direct evidence of whether these second-round effects are beginning to take hold,' he said, and 'it's that dynamic that will be the key driver on whether further rate increases would be necessary or not.' He pointed to pay bargaining, costs and pricing, and to the margin squeeze on companies and how they respond.

That makes the spring of 2027 the decision point he has set himself. Between now and then the data that matter are wage settlements, services inflation and corporate pricing intentions, plus the next MPC decision in December, where a second increase is priced as roughly a coin toss.

For mortgage holders and for banks, which benefit from wider margins when rates are higher, the distinction matters. A signal of a long cycle would reprice fixed-rate mortgages and bank earnings. A signal of a one-off, pre-emptive move with the next step left open does much less. On the evidence available, Pill has offered the latter.
