UK ethics watchdog chief steps back amid misconduct claims

In one sentence
Stuart Sterling, chief executive of the UK's Ethics and Integrity Commission, has stepped back while the government examines decades-old sexual misconduct claims, which he denies in part.

The chief executive of the UK government's Ethics and Integrity Commission has stepped back from his post while ministers try to establish the facts behind allegations of sexual misconduct dating from his time as a police officer. Stuart Sterling, who has led the body since October 2025, remains in the role in name but is not exercising it. No interim head had been named in the reporting available on Sunday.

The allegations were first reported by the Sunday Times. They concern Sterling's earlier career as a detective constable at Northamptonshire Police. The complainants have not been named.

What is alleged

According to the reporting, Sterling was accused in 2002 of grabbing a female colleague's breasts at Corby police station. He was suspended and later returned to duty after the force decided against criminal proceedings. Shortly afterwards, it is alleged, he telephoned the woman and visited her home to discourage her from pursuing the matter. He strongly denies that.

A second allegation, from 2008, relates to an incident said to have taken place two years earlier, involving another colleague. Sterling denies that the incident occurred. He had left the force by January that year.

His representative says a full disciplinary process was held over the 2002 complaint, that he complied with it fully, and that it ended in a sanction. He says Sterling was never the subject of a criminal investigation and left the force voluntarily rather than under compulsion.

Northamptonshire Police confirmed it holds a brief digital record of a 2002 misconduct process. Supporting documents would have been kept on paper, and may no longer exist under data-retention rules. That limits how much can now be established independently, and it is likely to shape the government's inquiry.

The government's position

A government spokesperson said: "These are serious allegations. Stuart Sterling has stepped back from his role whilst we swiftly establish the facts."

The government has also said it will examine whether "appropriate and correct disclosures of information" were made. That is a separate question from the allegations themselves. Appointments to senior public roles ordinarily involve vetting and declarations about past conduct, and the issue is whether what was disclosed at the time matched what the record shows. A finding on disclosure could be reached even if the underlying claims remain unresolved, because the evidence for them is thin and old.

Why it matters

The Ethics and Integrity Commission is described as the body that upholds integrity, honesty and accountability in the public sector. A watchdog of that kind relies on its leadership being beyond question when it examines others. Stepping back is the conventional way to protect that standing while an inquiry runs. It is not a finding of wrongdoing, and Sterling disputes the central claims.

The arrangement does leave practical questions open. The commission's day-to-day direction during the inquiry has not been set out publicly, and the government has given no timetable beyond "swiftly". The accounts so far are also partly second-hand: the allegations rest on the Sunday Times's reporting, and the complainants have not spoken publicly.

What to watch

Three things will settle the next stage: whether the government names an acting head for the commission, how long the fact-finding takes, and whether it publishes its conclusions on the disclosure question. If the review finds that the record and Sterling's account diverge, his position would become much harder to sustain. If it finds no discrepancy, he faces the harder task of returning to lead a body whose authority depends on public trust.
