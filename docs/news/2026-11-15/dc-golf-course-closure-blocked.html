Judge temporarily blocks Trump from closing Washington DC golf course

In one sentence
A federal judge has barred further tree-cutting at East Potomac Golf Links and required notice before closure, as Trump's 15 November start date arrives and an appeal looms.

A federal judge in Washington has stopped the Trump administration from cutting more trees at East Potomac Golf Links, the public course on a spit of land south of the National Mall. She has also ordered the government to give notice before it closes the park or makes irreversible changes to it. The order is an injunction issued before trial, so the underlying case is undecided. It lands as the president's stated closure date, 15 November, arrives.

What the court ordered

U.S. District Judge Ana Reyes issued a 70-page opinion on 26 October granting a preliminary injunction. It bars the government from cutting, substantially pruning or otherwise damaging trees at East Potomac Park without a court order. It also requires at least ten business days' written notice before the park is closed or any "irreversible modification" is made.

The plaintiffs are the D.C. Preservation League and two local golfers, represented by the group Democracy Forward. Their case is mainly environmental-procedural, not a claim about the president's finances. Reyes found the Interior Department likely violated the National Environmental Policy Act by "impermissibly segmenting" its review of the renovation, assessing it in pieces rather than as a whole. She also rejected a new government argument that the Administrative Procedure Act bars review of the work.

The trigger was a single day over the Columbus Day weekend, when contractors felled roughly 430 trees with less than a day's notice. The government said the clearing followed vegetation recommendations in a 2019 National Park Service report. Reyes called that explanation "pretextual", after a seven-hour hearing with sworn testimony from Interior and Park Service officials. She found the government's statements conflicted with the president's own public posts. She concluded that a renovation plan, which the plaintiffs call the "Washington National Plan", existed well before 15 October. Her order said she could not pin down when it became final.

The president's stake

The course is municipal and open to the public. Mr Trump wants to turn it into a championship-level venue capable of hosting events such as the PGA Championship or the Ryder Cup. He has said it would stay open to the public at a reduced rate for D.C. residents. Critics point to a rendering he shared that appears to remove the perimeter road used by runners and cyclists, and they fear a less affordable, less accessible park.

The pleadings and reporting reviewed here do not establish that Mr Trump holds a financial interest in the course, and nothing here supports calling the dispute a personal-profit case. The live question is narrower and more conventional: whether an agency can reshape a public park by a route that skips the required environmental review. That is the question a higher court will have to answer.

Appeal and the 15 November date

Mr Trump said on Truth Social that Interior would appeal. The Justice Department filed notice of appeal to the D.C. Circuit shortly after the ruling, but reporting indicates it did not ask for an immediate stay, so the restrictions remain in force. The president has nonetheless said construction begins on 15 November and that the park will close for 15 months. The National Park Service has said it will close East Potomac Park south of Buckeye Drive, including the course, "consistent with the President's directive."

The injunction does not forbid closure outright. It requires notice and bars irreversible steps. Whether the government has given that notice, and whether closure on the announced date is consistent with the order, could not be confirmed from the reporting available. Reyes had asked the government to say whether a mid-November closure plan exists, and she planned a status hearing. How the government answered was not found.

Two things decide what happens next. One is whether the D.C. Circuit is asked to pause the injunction, and how it rules. The other is what Reyes says about the closure notice at her next hearing. The court docket, not the president's announcement, will show whether the gates close on schedule.
