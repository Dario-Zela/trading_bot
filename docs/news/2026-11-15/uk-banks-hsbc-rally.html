UK banks in focus as higher rates support margins; HSBC up 326% in five years

In one sentence
HSBC's roughly fourfold five-year rise has lifted it above its historic book multiples, and valuation models split on whether a 5.4% yield and buybacks justify the price.

HSBC's share price has roughly quadrupled over five years, and a retail-oriented valuation site has asked the obvious follow-up: is there anything left? The question arrives without a fresh corporate trigger. The figure that framed it, a 326% five-year gain, comes from a Simply Wall St headline and could not be matched to an exact article. Simply Wall St's HSBC pieces have cited five-year gains from about 304% to 360%, depending on the publication date. The direction is not in doubt. The precise number is, and it moves with whichever day the window ends.

What the rally rests on

The five-year window starts in late 2021, when UK bank shares were priced for near-zero rates. Higher policy rates since then have widened the spread between what banks earn on loans and what they pay on deposits. HSBC has paired that with buybacks and a stated 50% dividend payout target for 2026 to 2028.

The most recent data point is mixed. In its third-quarter results in late October, HSBC reported profit attributable to ordinary shareholders of $7.09bn, against $4.87bn a year earlier, and announced a buyback of up to $1bn. The shares nonetheless fell by roughly 6% to 7% on the day, as revenue missed expectations and wealth-management growth slowed, according to Khaleej Times and investinglive. A stock that rises on earnings growth and falls on a revenue miss is being priced for execution.

Is it still cheap?

The valuation tools disagree, which is the main finding. Simply Wall St's own articles have variously called HSBC undervalued, on an intrinsic-value estimate about 37% above the market price, and overvalued, on a price-to-earnings multiple of about 12.8x against a fair ratio of 9.3x. The fair-ratio estimates across its pieces run from about 9.1x to 10.2x. These are model outputs resting on different assumptions.

Book value gives a cleaner reference. Hargreaves Lansdown shows a forward price-to-book ratio of 1.25, against a ten-year average of 0.84. Stockopedia shows a trailing price to tangible book of 1.68. Neither snapshot is clearly dated, so both are approximate. Even so, they point the same way: HSBC no longer trades at the steep discount to book that marked most of the past decade. The prospective dividend yield, about 5.4% on the Hargreaves figure, is the remaining support. The third interim dividend of $0.10 a share is payable on 17 December to holders on the register at 6 November.

The rate-path caveat

The margin argument depends on rates staying high. The candidate material says analysts expect the Bank of England's path to point higher, but it names no analyst, forecast or meeting date, and that claim is unverified here. The firmer reference is the Bank's own Monetary Policy Committee, where Huw Pill has said further rises are likely in 2027. If that holds, it helps net interest income at the margin. HSBC also earns most of its profit outside the UK, so sterling rates are only one input.

What to watch

The next test is full-year results, where investors will look for net interest income guidance and signs that wealth-management growth is recovering. Until then, the five-year gain says little about the next five. The stock is no longer cheap against its own history, and it is not obviously expensive against its payouts.
