UK on course to miss even its reduced aid target of 0.3% of income

In one sentence
The Guardian reports UK aid is on course to miss even the government's reduced 0.3%-of-income target, raising questions over the budget, partners and the cut's purpose.

The government's plan to cut Britain's aid budget to 0.3% of national income by 2027 is reported to be slipping. The Guardian says spending is on course to fall short of even that reduced target. The article's underlying figures could not be checked independently, so the exact size of any miss is not given here. The policy was meant to release money for defence. A shortfall would mean the cut is not delivering on its own terms.

How the target got here

The International Development (Official Development Assistance Target) Act 2015 commits the UK to spending 0.7% of gross national income (GNI) on aid. That commitment was suspended in 2021, when the government reduced the target to 0.5% citing the pandemic's effect on the public finances. In February 2025 the Prime Minister announced a further reduction to 0.3% by 2027, to fund higher defence spending. According to the House of Commons Library, 0.3% of GNI in 2027 would total an estimated £9.2bn, the lowest cash figure since 2012, against about £15.4bn had aid stayed at 0.5%.

As a result, 0.3% is a policy target rather than the statutory one. Missing it would not obviously be a breach of the 2015 Act, which the government has already set aside. The more immediate question is whether the cut raises the money it was meant to raise.

Where the pressure comes from

The aid total counts more than money sent abroad. Under OECD rules, the first year of costs for hosting refugees and asylum seekers in the donor country can be classed as aid. The Independent Commission for Aid Impact (ICAI) notes that such spending rose from 3% of UK aid in 2016 to a peak of 29% in 2022, and was still about 18% in 2025. These costs are driven by asylum numbers and hotel and accommodation spending, so the Treasury cannot easily control them.

The ICAI estimates that around £1.5bn of the roughly £9.4bn aid budget in 2027/28 will go on in-UK refugee costs. An earlier ICAI estimate suggested that aid spent overseas would then be only about 0.24% of GNI. The headline figure may therefore be hit while the money reaching aid-dependent countries falls well below it. Any reported miss of the 0.3% total would sit on top of that.

What follows

The fiscal arithmetic has two routes. Either the overspend is tolerated, which dents the savings that the cut was meant to fund. Or departments are told to find further reductions in programmes abroad, which would deepen the cuts to the Foreign Office's own budget. The Commons Library notes that an underspend on in-UK costs does not automatically pass to the Foreign Office, so the system is not symmetrical.

Diplomatically, a second round of squeezing would land on partners already adjusting to the 0.3% plan, and would weaken what remains of the case that Britain still honours a development pledge. It also lands as the government prepares Wednesday's Budget, when headroom is already tight.

The next fixed points are that Budget and the Foreign Office's allocations for 2027/28, which will show whether ministers hold the 0.3% line or move it again.
