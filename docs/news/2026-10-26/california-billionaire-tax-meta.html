California's billionaire tax vote draws in Zuckerberg and Meta

In one sentence
California's Proposition 40 would levy a one-time 5% tax on billionaires' net worth, and Meta has already built a share-sale mechanism to help Zuckerberg pay it and stay.

California voters decide on 3 November whether to levy a one-time tax on the net worth of the state's billionaires. The measure, Proposition 40, is billed as a world first. It has already produced one unusual corporate response: Meta has agreed a mechanism that would help its chief executive, Mark Zuckerberg, pay a bill that does not yet exist.

What the measure does

Proposition 40, the 2026 Billionaire Tax Act, imposes a single 5% tax on the net worth of California residents worth $1bn or more. Residency is fixed as of 1 January 2026. Net worth is measured on 31 December 2026. A married couple counts as one taxpayer. It needs a simple majority to pass.

The design differs from a conventional marginal wealth tax. According to the Hoover Institution's summary, the rate applies to the whole fortune, not only the slice above the threshold. A narrow phase-in ramps it from 0% at $1.0bn to 5% at $1.1bn. The base covers most personal property, including shares, bonds and other financial interests, but excludes directly held real property. Retirement accounts are reportedly excluded, and certain trusts a billionaire has funded are reached.

The revenue is earmarked. Of the proceeds, 90% would go to health care and 10% to education, food assistance and enforcement. Proponents have put the take at roughly $100bn. That is their projection and has not been independently verified. Payment could be made upfront or spread over five years with deferral charges.

Critics dispute the arithmetic. The Tax Foundation argues the effective rate could be well above 5% because of design choices and possible drafting errors. Hoover projects a net $25bn loss in permanent income-tax revenue if billionaires leave. Both sets of figures are advocacy-adjacent estimates, and neither replaces the state's own fiscal analysis.

Why founders are at the centre

The base is mostly paper. A founder's wealth sits in company stock, which is illiquid in the sense that selling enough to pay 5% can mean diluting control or moving the share price. The test is residency on a single date, which also means the tax is a bet on mobility: those already resident on 1 January 2026 are caught whether or not they later leave, though departure still affects future income-tax receipts.

The money behind the campaigns is lopsided. The opposition committee, Building a Better California, has raised more than $333m, about 30% of it from Google co-founder Sergey Brin. The Yes on 40 campaign has raised about $32.7m.

Zuckerberg and the Meta arrangement

Zuckerberg had told Meta he was weighing a move of his primary residence out of state, according to reports on the company's disclosure. The board's answer is a new class of non-voting stock, Class C shares, due to be issued in January 2027. He may sell them to cover a tax bill. In return he must commit to remaining a California resident for eight years. He can sell up to 20% of the Class C shares immediately, enough to pay the tax without touching his majority voting control.

The scale is large. Bloomberg's index puts his fortune near $247bn, and the tax would apply to roughly $12.4bn of it. The shape of the deal is notable: the company, not the individual, is absorbing the structuring problem, and it is doing so before the law exists. For a listed company, that raises its own questions about the cost to other shareholders of a perk tied to one executive's tax liability. The terms reported so far do not settle that.

One caution on residency. At least one outlet has reported that Zuckerberg decided to move to Florida, and another that he is buying a Miami waterfront property. These conflict with the Meta filing and the Reuters-reported commitment to stay, and they could not be verified.

What to watch

The first data point is the vote on 3 November. If Proposition 40 passes, the Class C issuance in January 2027 is the next test of whether the Meta structure holds. The year-end valuation date then sets the actual liability. Disputes over valuing private-company stakes, and over the drafting questions the Tax Foundation raises, would probably follow. If it fails, the Meta deal will stand as a record of how seriously a company treated the risk. Either way, other boards with founder-heavy shareholder bases now have a template to copy or reject.
