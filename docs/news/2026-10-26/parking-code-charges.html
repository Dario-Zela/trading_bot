New private parking code aims to curb 'rip-off' charges

In one sentence
A statutory private parking code keeps the £100 cap but adds grace periods, mandatory appeal cancellations and a DVLA-access sanction; recovery fees and a single appeals body are left open.

Private parking firms in England and Wales issue charges on land that is neither council nor police territory: supermarket and retail car parks, hospital sites, residential estates. A statutory code of practice laid before Parliament on 20 October now sets the rules for how those charges are levied and challenged. The test is whether it changes enforcement or only adds paperwork.

The code was laid by the Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government. The BBC has asked whether it will work. Several of the headline measures are real changes. Some of the contested ones were left out.

What the code says

The maximum charge stays at £100. Consumer groups had wanted a general cap of £50, the figure in an earlier version of the code that was withdrawn in June 2022 after some operators began legal proceedings against it. That cap has not returned.

There is a lower tier for minor mistakes. Where a driver enters the wrong registration or forgets a permit, the charge is capped at £20 after appeal. Drivers also get a grace period: five minutes to decide whether to park, and ten minutes to leave after a paid session ends.

The appeals rules are the other main change. Under an Appeals Charter, a charge must be cancelled where a listed circumstance applies, such as a medical emergency, a breakdown or a faulty ticket machine. Until now, operators have had wide discretion over such cases.

Why the enforcement question matters

The legal footing explains why the code matters. Under the Protection of Freedoms Act 2012, operators can pursue the registered keeper of a vehicle for an unpaid charge only if they belong to an accredited trade association, principally the British Parking Association (BPA) or the International Parking Community (IPC). Operators also need access to DVLA keeper data to find the keeper.

That access is the lever. Operators that breach the code could lose it, which for most of them would end the business model. A new independent Scrutiny and Oversight Board is to examine charge and appeal data. Work to set it up begins once the code takes effect.

A sanction that exists on paper is not the same as one that is used. The industry has had codes before. In 2024 the BPA and the IPC published a single industry code. The RAC called it conveniently silent on the biggest issues, and said it was not backed by law. A code with statutory force and a credible penalty is a different instrument, but only if the penalty is applied.

What is left out

Critics point to gaps. Debt recovery fees, which can add up to £70 to a disputed charge, are not banned. The government says it will consult on them, including whether to cut the cap or scrap the fees. The RAC also notes that the code does not create a single independent appeals body. The existing appeal routes, first to the operator and then to an independent appeals service, have long been criticised as slow and uneven, and the code does not replace them.

A drivers' lobby would say a £100 ceiling and unaddressed recovery fees leave much of the pressure on motorists intact. The industry can point to the grace periods and cancellation rules as concessions it has accepted.

What to watch

Three things will settle the matter. The first is the date the code takes effect, and how quickly the Scrutiny and Oversight Board starts publishing charge and appeal data. The second is the debt recovery fee consultation. The third is whether the DVLA sanction is ever used against a named operator. Until one loses access to keeper data, the code's force is untested.
