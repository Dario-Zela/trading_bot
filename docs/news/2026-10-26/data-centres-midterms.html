Data centres become a US midterm issue

In one sentence
Power prices and local opposition to AI data centres have become midterm campaign themes, with candidates in both parties promising to keep the cost off household bills.

Rising electricity bills are now turning up in US midterm campaigns, and AI data centres are the villain of choice. A subject that spent years in utility-commission hearings and county zoning meetings has become material for television ads, with candidates in both parties promising to keep the cost of the AI buildout off household bills.

Ads and moratoriums

The clearest example is the Ohio Senate race. Democrat Sherrod Brown has put more than $3.5m behind three TV spots attacking Republican Jon Husted over data centres, according to the ad-tracking firm AdImpact. Husted has answered with a "Ratepayer Protection Act", legislation meant to limit the effect of data centres on residential bills.

The pattern is not confined to one party. In Michigan, Republican Senate candidate Mike Rogers has said he backs a one-year state moratorium on new data centres. In Florida, Republican Byron Donalds is running ads promising to protect families from data centres that push up utility rates. In Wisconsin, state Rep. Francesca Hong backed a moratorium during the Democratic primary. In Pennsylvania, Gov. Josh Shapiro signed an order requiring developers to cover electricity costs and win local approval. President Trump has said new data centres should supply their own power.

Analysts cited in US coverage say almost every candidate now favours some form of ratepayer protection. The difference is in the remedy: Democrats lean more towards pauses and moratoriums, Republicans towards making developers pay.

What the numbers say

Public opinion supplies the incentive. A Gallup poll in March found 71% of Americans opposed to data centres in their own community. A Pew poll found 44% of Democrats and 33% of Republicans thought data centres were "mostly bad" for household energy costs. Local politics has already bitten: in Festus, Missouri, four council members reportedly lost re-election after approving a facility.

The cost evidence is real but contested. A Bloomberg analysis found wholesale electricity prices in areas with heavy data-centre activity rose by as much as 267% between 2020 and 2025. Goldman Sachs has projected that AI data centres could add about 6% to national electricity bills over the next year. Yet the Brookings Institution notes that experts disagree on what is driving bills up, and many candidates simply lump power costs in with housing, food and petrol. A rise in bills near data centres does not prove the data centres caused it, and that gap is where the campaign claims are weakest.

What to watch

The practical consequences will land in statehouses and utility commissions, not Washington. Moratorium proposals, rules making large users pay for new grid capacity, and local permitting fights will all be shaped by who wins in November. The Ohio Senate result will be an early read on whether attacking data centres works as a message. For technology and energy investors, the question is whether the politics slows the buildout or only changes who pays for it.
