Board of Deputies declares the Greens a racist party

In one sentence
The Board of Deputies voted 118-1 to call the Green Party racist over its resolution treating Zionism as racism, and urged other parties to avoid deals with it.

The Board of Deputies of British Jews, the main representative body of the UK's Jewish communities, has formally declared the Green Party of England and Wales a racist party. The vote came at the Board's October plenary on Sunday and passed 118 to one. It follows a Green conference decision earlier this month to treat Zionism as a form of racism.

The motion behind the label

On 4 October the Greens adopted a resolution saying Zionism "privileges one ethnic group on the basis of the subjugation of others". It said Zionism would be "treated as any other form of racism", with the party's anti-racism policy working group left to work out how. The resolution also backed "a single democratic Palestinian state in all of historic Palestine", with Jerusalem as its capital and a right of return for Palestinians and their descendants.

The resolution was condemned by Jewish and Israeli leaders, by the European Green Party and by some Green members. The Board's response goes beyond condemnation. It says it will treat the Greens as it treats other parties it regards as racist. It will regard any party that cooperates with them as not taking antisemitism seriously enough. It will also "engage with and support" Green members who reject the resolution and want it reversed.

Board president Phil Rosenberg said the policy "brands much of our community, including our synagogues and communal organizations, with that slur", and that it "gives prejudice against Jews a political license". The Board's statement referred to "a policy which brands a central element of Jewish identity as racist".

The wording matters. The label is applied to the party as a whole, in response to a specific policy, and the Board has set out what would count as a way back: reversal of the resolution. Reporting I have seen does not reproduce the full text of the Board's motion, which is published as a PDF, so finer points of its drafting are not confirmed here.

The Greens' response

The party has rejected the Board's "characterisation of this motion and our party". It says it does not discriminate against people who hold Zionist views and that the policy will not stop it speaking out against the war in Gaza. Leader Zack Polanski neither spoke against the motion at conference nor voted on it, according to the Times of Israel, which cited no other statement from him.

That silence is part of the difficulty. A party can defend a resolution as a statement about a political ideology; a communal body can answer that, for most British Jews, the ideology is bound up with identity. The two sides are talking past each other, and neither has an obvious incentive to give ground.

What it means for the party

The practical test is political. Labour had already set an ultimatum for local Green groups it works with in coalitions: disown the motion or lose Labour's support. The Board is now urging other parties not to make deals with the Greens unless local leaderships reject the policy. That turns a dispute about wording into a question about local-council arrangements and, if the Greens keep rising, about national alliances.

British parties have faced this kind of accusation before, and the pattern is instructive. Labour's antisemitism row under Jeremy Corbyn showed that a party's own account of its intentions carries less weight once community organisations conclude otherwise. Whether the Greens follow a similar path depends on whether the resolution is revisited at their next conference, and on how many of the Jewish and other members who opposed it stay and press for reversal.

The next signals are the Greens' internal response, including any move to review or amend the resolution, and whether Labour's ultimatum to local groups is enforced where coalitions are in place.
