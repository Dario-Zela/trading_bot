Vučić reported to have lost Serbian election to student-led movement

In one sentence
Aleksandar Vučić has conceded that his party lost Serbia's 25 October parliamentary election to a student-backed list, though official results are still pending.

Serbia's long-dominant leader has conceded defeat. In a short televised address on Sunday night, Aleksandar Vučić said that "based on the stratified sample, it is clear that we have lost the election." The winner was the Student List, a slate drawn from the protest movement that has challenged his government since a railway-station canopy collapsed in Novi Sad in November 2024.

The result is not yet official. The figures come from Vučić's own account of exit-poll and sample-based estimates, and the national electoral commission has not published a final count. But Euronews, CNN and CBS News all report the concession, which is the strongest confirmation available at this stage.

What is known about the result

The vote was a snap parliamentary election held on 25 October. Vučić told viewers that the Student List would take between 135 and 140 of the 250 seats in the National Assembly. His Serbian Progressive Party (SNS) would take about 105. He put the Student List at roughly 2.2 million votes and the SNS at about 1.72 million.

He said minority parties would hold the remaining seats. The Alliance of Vojvodina Hungarians would take three, Albanian parties one, and the Party of Democratic Action probably one. Euronews reports the Student List's share at 52 percent against 32.3 percent for the SNS, without explaining how those percentages were calculated. These are projections and should be treated as such. It is also unclear whether every small party cleared the 3 percent threshold, though the outlets that have reported on this do not expect it to change the outcome.

A lead of this size would give the Student List an outright majority. Monitors had expected turnout of around 70 percent, which would be the highest since 2000. Turnout has not been confirmed.

Vučić blamed independent media and alleged interference by unnamed European countries and an "American deep state" for the loss. Ursula von der Leyen, president of the European Commission, congratulated the Student List.

From the street to the ballot

The movement began as a response to a specific tragedy. In November 2024 the concrete canopy of the recently renovated Novi Sad railway station fell, killing 16 people. Students blamed corruption and negligence in public works. They blockaded university faculties and organised marches, and the demonstrations spread across the country. They became the largest sustained challenge to Vučić since he came to power in 2012, first as deputy prime minister, then as prime minister and, from 2017, as president.

The move into electoral politics was a gamble. Protest movements often struggle with it. A movement that had defined itself by being leaderless and non-partisan had to run candidates, agree a programme and campaign against a ruling party that controls much of the media and the public payroll. Reports say the list includes no university students. Its candidates are mostly little-known experts, athletes, farmers and artists, which fits a movement that presented itself as separate from the existing opposition parties.

The election itself followed a political reshuffle. Reports differ on Vučić's exact position going into the vote. Euronews describes him as the outgoing president who resigned to lead his party's list. He was constitutionally barred from seeking a third presidential term, and a presidential election must be held by May 2027. His office, his party's standing and his role in any coming government are now all uncertain.

What it means for Serbia and for Brussels

Serbia has been an EU candidate since 2012, and accession talks have barely moved. Brussels has criticised democratic backsliding, media freedom and the handling of the protests, while also courting Belgrade for its role in Balkan stability and, more recently, for its ties to Russia and China. A government elected on an anti-corruption platform would test whether the EU can offer a more credible path than it has so far.

Other issues will follow quickly: Kosovo, relations with Moscow, and the structure of Serbia's institutions after more than a decade of one-party dominance. Euronews calls the result "a profound rejection" of the political model that has dominated Serbia. The Student List now has to turn a protest coalition into a governing majority. That means choosing a prime minister, writing a programme and dealing with an administration whose senior ranks were built under Vučić.

Similar movements elsewhere in Europe have won seats. Few have won a majority against a government with this much control over the state. If the result holds, it will be studied by opposition movements well beyond the Balkans.

What to watch

The first test is the electoral commission's final tally and any challenge to it. Vučić has conceded, but the SNS has not yet said whether it will accept the commission's figures. Next comes the formal convening of the new assembly and the choice of a government, for which there is no published timetable. The presidential election due by May 2027 will show whether the Student List can win the country's other main office, or whether Serbia is heading for a period in which the presidency and the government are held by opposing camps.
