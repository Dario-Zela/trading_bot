ISRAEL · ELECTION · By C. Tanaka · 2026-10-26 · 5 min read

Deeply divided Israel braces for pivotal election

In one sentence
Israelis vote on Tuesday with final polls showing neither Netanyahu's coalition nor the opposition reaching 61 seats, pointing to prolonged coalition talks or a repeat election.

Israelis vote on Tuesday in the first general election since the Hamas attacks of 7 October 2023. The campaign has been fought mainly over one man, Benjamin Netanyahu, and the final polls suggest the country is as split as it was when it last voted, on 1 November 2022. Most surveys give neither Netanyahu's governing bloc nor the opposition the 61 seats needed for a majority in the 120-seat Knesset. The result could be weeks of coalition bargaining, or a fresh election.

Polling stations open at 7am local time and close at 10pm. Israel counts a single nationwide vote with no constituencies. Seats are allocated proportionally among parties that clear a 3.25% threshold. Results usually emerge within hours, but the final tally takes days.

A referendum on one man

Netanyahu is on trial for corruption, which he denies, and is the subject of an International Criminal Court arrest warrant over Gaza, which he also rejects. He has refused calls for an independent national inquiry into the failures of 7 October, calling the idea a political witch-hunt. Reports that he had advance warning of the attack are, in his words, libellous.

His opponents have sought to make the election a verdict on all of this. His own pitch is blunter. ABC News reports that his campaign slogan is "right or Palestine", a phrase that rhymes in Hebrew and frames the contest as a choice between his camp and everything else.

The security record is mixed and complicated. Israel has been in near-constant conflict since 2023, including fighting with Hezbollah in Lebanon and two rounds of conflict with Iran. ABC reports that Netanyahu wanted to resume strikes on Iran but was urged to hold off by Donald Trump. Few analysts expect the wars themselves to move many votes. No major contender is promising an immediate end to the fighting, and cost-of-living pressures, usually a staple of Israeli campaigns, have been relatively muted.

The challengers

The main threat to Likud comes from a new party, Yashar, led by Gadi Eisenkot, a former chief of the armed forces. Final polls put it first, with 22 seats from Channel 13, 23 from Channel 12 and 22 from Kan. Likud trails by a small margin, on 19 to 21.

Behind them is Beyachad ("Together"), a list headed by the former prime minister Naftali Bennett and, according to ABC, Yair Lapid, polling 14 to 15 seats. Yair Golan's centre-left Democrats are on 8 to 10, and Avigdor Liberman's Yisrael Beiteinu on 8. In a Kan head-to-head question, Eisenkot led Netanyahu 44% to 38% as preferred prime minister, while Bennett and Netanyahu tied at 38%.

Benny Gantz, once a leading rival, withdrew his Blue and White party from the race on 19 October.

The arithmetic

The three main television polls, as reported by the Times of Israel, give a consistent picture:

  • Channel 13: the "change" bloc has 54 seats, Netanyahu's coalition parties 50 and the Arab parties 16.
  • Channel 12: the change bloc has 56, the coalition 50 and the Arab parties 14.
  • Kan: the change bloc has 52, the coalition 50 and the Arab parties 14. Yoaz Hendel's Reservists clear the threshold in this poll only, with 4 seats.

The coalition figures cover Likud, Shas, United Torah Judaism, Otzma Yehudit and Religious Zionism. Other polls differ: i24NEWS gave Netanyahu's side 59, and a Maariv survey put the opposition at 54. Outlets that lean right tend to show the incumbent bloc stronger, though the margins of error (2.3 to 4.4 points in the surveys above) are wide enough to cover most of these gaps.

The central point is that the Arab parties, the Joint List and Ra'am, hold 14 to 16 seats that neither side expects to count on. Arab citizens are about a fifth of the population. Ra'am's leader, Mansour Abbas, has said he wants a role in shaping a government. Most Jewish party leaders keep their distance from both Arab lists in coalition talks, so those seats are close to dead weight in the arithmetic.

Small right-wing and religious lists matter for the same reason. Ofer Winter's People of Israel and Moti Leitner's ultra-Orthodox list fall below the threshold in every poll cited here. Votes cast for parties that miss the line are discarded, and the seats are redistributed among those that pass, so a few thousand ballots can tip a bloc over or under 61.

The issues that decide coalitions

Two disputes shape who can sit with whom. The first is the military draft. The Supreme Court struck down exemptions for ultra-Orthodox men in 2024, and the opposition says it will not form a government with the Haredi parties. That removes a traditional route to a majority for a centrist leader. The second is the judicial overhaul that drew mass protests before the war. It was shelved but remains unresolved.

The West Bank gets less attention in the campaign. Few contenders promise major change on settler violence, though some say they would restore law enforcement against extremists.

What comes next

The result matters beyond Israel because the next government will set policy on Gaza, Iran and the West Bank, and so help determine regional risk. Investors in Israeli assets will be watching for a clear outcome. The polls point to the opposite, and a long period of coalition talks would leave decisions on these questions in suspense.

After the vote, the president consults party leaders and gives one of them the first chance to form a government, with a limited period to do so. Defections between blocs, which polls cannot capture, are possible during that time. If no one assembles 61 seats, Israelis go back to the polls.

The first marker is the exit polls at 10pm local time on Tuesday, followed by the overnight count. The key figures are whether Likud or Yashar finishes first, and which small parties fall below 3.25%.
