Employers warn training-cost changes for older workers could undercut youth jobs plan

In one sentence
Britain's large employers warn that making them pay 25% of training costs for apprentices over 25 could undermine the government's push to cut youth unemployment.

Britain's biggest employers have warned ministers that asking businesses to pay more towards training older workers could cut against the government's own aim of getting more young people into work. The warning, reported by the Guardian on 26 October, lands two days before the Budget and as the government weighs its response to a review of young people who are not in education, employment or training.

What is being proposed

The proposal concerns apprenticeships in England. As reported, employers would be asked to cover 25% of the eligible training costs for apprentices aged 25 and over. Apprentices aged 16 to 24 would continue to be funded in full. Some reports say the change could be phased in from September 2027. Reports differ on details, and the plan is described as not yet final, so the figures are best read as reported rather than confirmed.

A version of the principle already exists. Under changes introduced in August 2026, levy-paying employers must contribute 25% of eligible costs for apprentices aged 25 and over once their levy funds run out. The new proposal would go further. The logic is easy to follow: if public money is squeezed, it is directed first at the young, where unemployment is most politically costly.

That cost is considerable. Youth unemployment reached 16.1% at the end of last year, the highest since 2014, and nearly a million 16 to 24-year-olds are outside education, employment and training. The Milburn review into the problem has been delayed until after the Budget, due on Wednesday 28 October.

The employers' objection

The employers' argument is that the two halves of the policy are not as separable as they look. Many firms use apprenticeship funding to retrain existing staff, and a government report found that most employers use levy funds to upskill current employees rather than to hire new ones. If training older staff becomes more expensive, the worry runs, firms may simply train fewer people overall, and may become less willing to take on apprentices of any age. A saving on older workers would not automatically become a job for a school-leaver.

John Foster of the CBI put it bluntly: "We can't make skills a zero-sum game." He argued that the better route to more youth apprenticeships is to make it cheaper for firms to take on young people, rather than to erect new barriers to training.

That sits within a wider employer complaint. Business groups say higher employer national insurance contributions, minimum wage rises and expanded employment rights have raised the cost of hiring young people, particularly in retail and hospitality. UKHospitality has estimated that the employer NICs changes added roughly £4,000 a year to the cost of employing a young worker. The Recruitment and Employment Confederation has separately called for a more flexible Growth and Skills Levy.

What to watch

Two dates matter. The Budget on 28 October may show whether the training-cost change is confirmed, amended or quietly shelved. The Milburn review, when it appears, will show how far the government's youth strategy relies on shifting costs onto employers of older trainees, or on easing the costs of hiring the young. Ministers are also reported to be considering dropping the Labour manifesto pledge to pay young people the same minimum wage as older workers, a sign that the cost of employing the young is already under review.
