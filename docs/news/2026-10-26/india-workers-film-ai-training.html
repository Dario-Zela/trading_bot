Indian gig workers film their jobs to train AI

In one sentence
Indian gig workers are being paid, reportedly around $2 an hour, to film their daily tasks as AI training video, raising questions about pay, consent and who owns the footage.

Somewhere in Chennai, a 25-year-old woman cuts mangoes while a camera records her hands. If the app can't see them, it tells her to start again. The footage goes to a data company, and she is paid a little over two dollars for each hour of usable video, according to AFP reporting. It is a small, repetitive job. The people who commission it want the footage to teach machines how to do the same task.

A growing number of Indian gig and manual workers now film their own work for this purpose. The recordings are first-person video, which researchers call "egocentric" data, and they are meant to help AI systems, particularly robots, learn how human hands move through ordinary tasks.

What the workers are filming

The tasks are mundane by design. Ravi Shankar, who runs the AI data firm Objectways, told AFP that clients have asked for videos of people folding clothes, making coffee and making sandwiches. In New Delhi, Bloomberg reported on Sunita Rathore, a 43-year-old recycling worker who sorts plastic with an iPhone strapped to her head. She switches the camera off when someone calls to her, and recording people's faces is prohibited.

Some of the work is organised through companies that sell the footage on. TechCrunch reported in May that Human Archive, a Silicon Valley start-up, works with home-services, hotel and restaurant businesses, whose staff wear camera caps while they work. The company says it has more than 1,000 active headsets and has raised $8.2 million from investors including Y Combinator.

Why India? The reporting points to a large workforce, relatively low wages and a ready acceptance of new technology. Together these make it a cheap place to collect the varied, real-world footage that developers say they lack.

The questions underneath

The first is pay. Two dollars an hour may be more than the alternatives for some workers, and the Chennai worker told AFP the work paid better than housework. But it is a rate set by the buyer of the data, for material that may later be worth far more to the AI developers who use it.

The second is displacement. Indian outlets and NPR have framed the trend as workers training the machines that could eventually replace them. A video that went viral in April, showing rows of textile workers wearing headsets, fed that unease. Nothing in the coverage suggests robots are close to taking over these jobs, and capable household or factory robots remain a goal rather than a product. The concern is about direction of travel, not an imminent change.

The third is data. Footage shot inside homes, hotels and workplaces can capture more than the task itself. The coverage describes rules against filming faces, but I found no public detail on who owns the recordings, how long they are kept, or what consent workers give for later uses. Those terms are not visible from outside, and the companies involved have not set them out in the material reviewed.

This piece draws on AFP, Bloomberg, TechCrunch and NPR reporting. I could not retrieve the BBC report that prompted it, so no BBC figures or quotes are used here.

What to watch

The test is whether the arrangements become more transparent as the market grows: published pay rates, clear consent terms for the footage, and rules on retention and resale. Neither India's data-protection regime nor the buyers of this footage have yet said how such recordings should be handled. Until they do, the people supplying the raw material will know less about its use than the companies buying it.
