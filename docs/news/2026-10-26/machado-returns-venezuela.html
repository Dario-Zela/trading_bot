Machado returns to Venezuela after months in exile

Machado arrives at a church in Lara state, Venezuela, on 25 October 2026. — Vente Venezuela Press/Handout via Reuters
In one sentence
Nobel laureate María Corina Machado has returned to Venezuela unannounced, testing whether Delcy Rodríguez's US-backed government will tolerate open opposition without setting an election date.

María Corina Machado, the Venezuelan opposition leader who won the 2025 Nobel Peace Prize, has returned to Venezuela after about ten months abroad. She arrived on Sunday, 25 October, without announcement. Reuters pictures show her at a church in Lara state. "I'm here. With you all," she wrote on social media. She has not said how she crossed the border or where she is now. Her return is the first real test of whether the authorities now running Venezuela will let a prominent opponent operate openly. Accounts of the details vary between outlets, and the BBC, the hinted source, was not among those checked for this piece.

A different Venezuela from the one she left

When Machado left the country in late 2025, Nicolás Maduro was still in power. She slipped out to collect her Nobel prize in Oslo, announced on 10 October 2025 for her work on democratic rights, after spending much of that year in hiding. Her route to power had been closed years earlier. The courts blocked her 2024 presidential candidacy, and she backed Edmundo González as the opposition candidate in the July 2024 election. The opposition published tally sheets it said showed González winning. The electoral authority declared Maduro the winner. González later left for Spain.

Then the ground shifted. US forces captured Maduro in January 2026. Power passed to his longtime ally Delcy Rodríguez, who governs as acting president with Washington's backing. She has stayed in office beyond the period she was first authorised to serve, and no presidential election has been scheduled. Rodríguez has warned that Machado "will have to answer" if she came home.

The backdrop to the return is therefore not a Maduro government that Washington is trying to dislodge. It is a successor administration that the Trump administration has chosen to work with.

The return and the reaction

Machado's earlier attempts to come back were blocked, according to accounts of the episode, and Reuters reported in June that US officials had asked her to delay. Reports differ on how many attempts failed, so the count is unclear. She had said earlier this year that she intended to return before the end of 2026 and to run for president again, and she has argued that a credible vote needs seven to nine months of preparation. Reports say she is seeking an election by next July.

Edmundo González confirmed her arrival and said he would join her "very soon". Her party called for unity behind a democratic, peaceful and orderly transition.

Donald Trump, asked about it in Michigan, said he knew nothing of the return. "I think she's a very nice woman," he said, adding: "She gave me her Nobel Prize". Machado presented him with the medal last week. He went on: "But we're doing really well in Venezuela." He has repeatedly praised his relationship with Rodríguez, citing oil production agreements and deals with American companies. He said in September that it was too soon for democratic elections, with US officials prioritising economic stabilisation.

What the oil link means

The oil connection in this story runs through Washington's policy rather than through anything Machado has done. Trump has promoted access to Venezuela's oil reserves as a benefit of his arrangement with Rodríguez. An opposition leader demanding an election date sits awkwardly with that bargain. How the US responds if Machado is harassed, or if she is left alone, will show how much weight it gives to a vote against the stability it says it wants. This piece does not rely on reporting about sanctions or licensing changes, and none of the sources checked tied her return to either.

Part of the opposition is already negotiating. A faction and Rodríguez's government held talks this month on reforming the Supreme Court and the electoral authorities. Machado's movement has not taken part. Her presence on Venezuelan soil gives the rival camp a competitor for the opposition's mandate, as well as the government.

What to watch

The first signal is whether Machado can travel, hold rallies and meet supporters unimpeded, or whether she is detained or confined as she was briefly in January 2025. The second is González's arrival, which would put the two 2024 figures together on home soil. The third is whether the electoral talks produce a date. A government that lets her campaign but sets no election would leave her with a platform and nothing to run for.
