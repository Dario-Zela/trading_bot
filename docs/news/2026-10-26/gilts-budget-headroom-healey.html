Gilt yields at multi-year highs leave Healey little room for Wednesday's Budget

In one sentence
With 10-year gilt yields near 18-year highs, analysts expect Healey to present only £10bn-£15bn of headroom on Wednesday, pushing him towards tax rises that stay inside manifesto limits.

John Healey will deliver his first Budget as Chancellor on Wednesday with the audience that matters most not in the Commons chamber but in the gilt market. A global bond sell-off has pushed UK borrowing costs to levels last seen before the financial crisis. Analysts now expect the fiscal buffer he will present to be about £10bn-£15bn. That is thin by the standards of recent Budgets, and it leaves little room for error.

The buffer shrinks

"Headroom" is the margin by which the government is forecast to meet its fiscal rules. A larger margin allows more spending or tax cuts, and a smaller one means any new commitment must be paid for. Most economists expect the figure to fall significantly, according to Scottish Financial News, which puts the range at £10bn-£15bn.

Individual estimates fall inside that range. EY puts headroom at £11.3bn, down from £23.6bn in March, and attributes roughly £10bn of the fall to higher yields. It says Healey needs to find at least £12bn to repair the buffer and fund pledges already made. Pantheon Macroeconomics estimates about £13bn, based on higher debt-interest costs. One outlier, a £22.7bn figure attributed to Handelsbanken, looks stale or built on a different basis. The Office for Budget Responsibility has not said which window of market prices it will use for its forecast, so the final number depends on that cut-off.

The arithmetic is mechanical. The OBR's forecasts assume the yields prevailing at a set date. Higher yields raise projected interest payments on a debt stock that is large and partly index-linked. They also raise the cost of each new issue of debt. Borrowing is already running ahead of plan: Trading Economics reports £97.8bn for April to September, about £6bn above the OBR's projection.

What the market is saying

The benchmark 10-year gilt yield touched 5.268% on one morning this month, its highest since June 2008, with five-year yields at their highest since October 2023. Trading Economics put the 10-year at about 5.33% on 23 October, after readings near 5.4% on 21-22 October. The 30-year gilt yield was 5.81% in August, when the 30-year US Treasury yield was 5.27%.

This is not only a UK problem. Trading Economics describes higher oil prices, linked to US strikes on Iranian tankers and Iranian threats near the Strait of Hormuz, as a key driver of the global sell-off. Investors are also uneasy about deficits in major economies and about inflation. UK inflation reached 3.5% in September, above forecasts, and markets expect further Bank of England rate rises.

The UK has a particular exposure to that backdrop. Reports that gilts are underperforming other major government bond markets could not be checked against yield spreads, so that claim should be treated with caution. Demand for UK assets has helped steady yields at times in October, as investors worried about eurozone debt and social unrest.

Investors have been blunt about what they want. Matthew Amis, an investment director at Aberdeen, told the Financial Times that extra debt issuance for investment next year above £10bn would be a red flag to the gilt market, and that the Chancellor should make the Budget "as much of a non-event as possible". Simon French, chief economist at Panmure Liberum, said it was not a time for bravery in global markets.

Room to manoeuvre

The government's stated position is conservative. Allies of Healey say he wants a "straightforward and well-managed" Budget and will stay "well within" his borrowing rules. The Treasury says fiscal discipline is the foundation of economic stability and that the rules will be met "with a buffer against uncertainty". It also says the UK is cutting its deficit faster than any other G7 economy.

The constraint on the revenue side is a manifesto pledge not to raise VAT, income tax or corporation tax. Healey is reported to be sticking to it. That points towards second-tier levers, and the BBC has set out options including changes to thresholds and measures on property and wealth. These are options rather than announced policy. Stealthier measures such as frozen thresholds raise money without a headline rate change, though they do so at the expense of taxpayers drawn into higher bands over time.

On spending, the Prime Minister has backed raising defence spending to 3% of GDP by the end of the decade and hinted at larger commitments later. A pre-briefed £1bn for social and affordable housing is small against a headroom of £10bn-£15bn, and whether it is new money or reallocated has not been established. The BBC also notes that the growth outlook has improved, but oil, inflation and borrowing costs could erase that gain.

What to watch

Two tests come on Wednesday. The first is the OBR's headroom figure, and whether it lands inside the £10bn-£15bn range or below it. The second is the gilt market's reaction in the hours after the speech: a move in the 10-year yield away from its 18-year high would suggest the plan has been judged credible, and a further rise would suggest it has not. After that, the Bank of England's next rate decision and the path of oil prices will matter as much as anything in the Budget red book.
