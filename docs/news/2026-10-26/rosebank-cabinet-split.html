Cabinet split over Rosebank as the oilfield becomes a test of energy security versus climate goals

In one sentence
Ministers are reportedly split over approving the Rosebank oilfield, a decision that tests whether North Sea energy-security arguments can be squared with UK climate commitments.

Ministers are divided over whether to approve Rosebank, the large oilfield west of Shetland. The argument is a practical test of whether the government can hold two commitments together: that North Sea production serves energy security, and that Britain is cutting emissions on a legally binding path. The reported split suggests it has not yet found a way to do so. The discovery reporting, from the Guardian and the BBC, names no ministers and quotes none, so the lines of division below rest on earlier reporting and on the public record.

A field with a long paper trail

Rosebank lies about 130 km north-west of Shetland, in roughly 1,100 metres of water. Equinor operates it with an 80% stake, and Ithaca Energy holds the other 20%. The partners took a final investment decision on the first phase in September 2023, committing $3.8bn between them. Recoverable resources are put at about 300 million barrels, with some 245 million targeted in phase one. The plan is to tie subsea wells back to a redeployed floating production vessel, the Petrojarl Knarr, with start-up pencilled in for 2026–27.

That timetable was disrupted in January 2025, when the Court of Session quashed the consent for Rosebank and for the Jackdaw gas field. The court found that the environmental assessment had not properly considered downstream, or scope 3, emissions: the carbon released when the oil is eventually burned, which typically dwarfs what is emitted in extracting it. The operators have since resubmitted their assessment with that material included. It awaits a ministerial decision, and the status of the project has stayed open throughout.

Where the cabinet divides

The fault lines are familiar from earlier reporting, which should be read as background and not as a description of the present discussions. Climate-minded ministers, with the energy department the usual home of that view, have been reported as opposed to new fields. Treasury-minded colleagues have been reported as favouring approval, citing investor confidence and jobs. Keir Starmer was reported in 2025 as signalling he would not stand in the way of a project that already held a licence.

The arguments on each side are not frivolous. Supporters say a field that is already licensed, and in which billions have been committed, sends a damaging signal if blocked. They also say that Britain will keep using oil and gas for years, and that domestic output displaces imports. Opponents answer that a new field of this size locks in production and emissions beyond the point at which the government's own climate advice says supply should be shrinking. They add that Rosebank's output would be sold on international markets, so it does little to protect British consumers from price shocks.

The court ruling matters here because it narrows the government's room to manoeuvre. Ministers must now weigh scope 3 emissions explicitly, so they cannot treat the climate cost as a technicality. Whatever they decide, the reasoning will be visible and open to challenge, and campaigners have shown they will test it in court.

What the people and the economics add

The debate in Westminster tends to leave out the field's workforce and the finances behind it. For the offshore and supply-chain workers in Aberdeen and Shetland, Rosebank is a source of work at a time when older fields are declining. Polling reported by Aberdeen business bodies has suggested a majority of Scots back the project, and even the SNP has been reported to have softened its opposition. Against that, the economics of a high-cost, deepwater development depend on oil prices and on the fiscal regime, which are themselves political choices.

What to watch

The immediate catalyst is the decision itself, and the reasoning that accompanies it. The government has said a call on Jackdaw would come first, and an approval there would be an early indication of how it treats scope 3 emissions in practice. Rosebank was described as taking longer. Producers and investors will read the outcome as a guide to the future of North Sea policy more broadly. Campaigners will read the justification for signs of a legal opening. Whichever way ministers go, they will need a coherent account of how the decision fits the climate commitments, and the reported split suggests they do not yet have one.
