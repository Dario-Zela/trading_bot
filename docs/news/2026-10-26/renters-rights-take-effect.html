Renters' rights reforms take effect as landlords warn of higher rents

In one sentence
Six months after England's Section 21 ban, rents are up about 4% a year but there is no clear sign yet of the surge or supply collapse landlords predicted.

England's private rental market has now lived under the Renters' Rights Act for almost six months. Landlords said the reforms would push rents up and shrink supply. The first national figures show a market that is tight but not breaking. They do not yet show the damage landlords predicted, and they are too early to rule it out.

What changed

The Act received Royal Assent on 27 October 2025. Its main tenancy provisions took effect in England on 1 May 2026. The central change was the abolition of Section 21 "no-fault" evictions, which had let landlords end a tenancy without giving a reason. Rent in advance is now capped at one month's rent. Rent rises are limited to once a year, through a formal Section 13 notice, and are meant to track market rates.

Some parts of the Act have yet to arrive. The private rented database, the Housing Ombudsman and the Decent Homes Standard for private rentals are due at a later date.

What the data shows so far

The latest Office for National Statistics figures put the average private rent in England at £1,466 a month in the 12 months to September, up 4.0% on a year earlier, according to Property118's summary of the release. Across the UK, annual growth was 3.8%, unchanged from August, at an average of £1,406. The North West recorded the fastest rise in England at 5.7%. London and the South East were slowest, both at 3.5%. The figures are provisional and may be revised.

That is firm growth, but it is not a spike. The data do not show a break at 1 May. Rents are also influenced by mortgage costs, wage growth and demand, so the ONS series cannot isolate the effect of the Act from those pressures.

Supply is harder to read. Industry commentary on the ONS release, such as Mortgage Solutions' report, points to landlords having sold up. I could not confirm volumes, and I found no official count of properties leaving the sector or of rental listings since May. A SpareRoom survey of almost 4,500 English tenants, conducted in April, found 30% of those who had stayed put had faced a rent increase. The same source reported that flatshare supply held up better than expected, though growth slowed in the year to January.

There is a further sign of how landlords have behaved. Government data cited by the National Residential Landlords Association showed 28,112 Section 21 possession claims in 2025, the lowest since 2022. That is consistent with a slow wind-down before the deadline, but it says little about what happens to rents afterwards.

What would settle it

Two things would turn the landlords' warnings into evidence. The first is a sustained gap between rent growth in England and in Scotland and Wales, which sit under different rules. The latest ONS release shows Scotland at 0.7%, Wales at 4.1% and England at 4.0%, which is not a clean comparison. The second is a fall in the stock of homes to rent, visible in listings data and in landlord sales. The next ONS private rent release, due next month, will add a further data point. The Act's second phase, with its database and standards, will test how far landlords' costs rise.
