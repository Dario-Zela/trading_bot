Burnham pledges £900m on homelessness, aiming to clear rough sleeping by Christmas

In one sentence
A £900m pledge promises to get rough sleepers off the streets by Christmas, but the baseline, timetable and what the money buys are unclear, and the target is testable within weeks.

A pledge to spend £900m on homelessness, with the stated aim of getting rough sleepers off the streets by Christmas, was announced on Monday. Funding for the existing plan is set to double, according to early reports from the Guardian and the BBC. The deadline is about two months away, so the claim can be tested quickly against published counts.

The reporting available so far leaves several things open. It does not make clear what the £900m is measured against when funding is said to double, or whether the money is new or partly re-announced. It also does not say over what period the money is spent, or whether it covers England alone or the wider UK, since housing is largely devolved. Those details decide whether this is a rescue operation or a multi-year budget with a seasonal slogan attached.

What a Christmas target means

Governments have set rough-sleeping goals before. They tend to hit the same obstacle: the problem is a flow as well as a stock. Someone placed in a hostel or flat this week is counted as housed, but others lose tenancies, leave prison, flee abuse or exhaust a friend's sofa in the same week. Any official count shows only a snapshot, taken on a single night in autumn in England's case, so a target of "no one on the streets" is hard to confirm or to refute.

The wording matters, then. "Clear rough sleeping" could mean offering everyone a bed, or it could mean a measured fall in the count. The first is achievable with emergency accommodation. The second depends on people taking offers, and on offers that suit them, which is harder for those with addiction, mental illness or no recourse to public funds.

What the money can buy

Emergency capacity can be bought quickly: hotel rooms, hostel places, outreach teams. Durable housing cannot. Councils and charities have long warned that temporary accommodation is already stretched, with large numbers of households housed in it, and that move-on options are scarce. Money that turns rough sleepers into temporary-accommodation residents by December would improve the street count without ending homelessness.

The pledge also lands as new tenancy rules take effect, which landlords say could push up rents (see related coverage), and as councils face tight budgets. Whether the doubled funding is ringfenced, and how fast it reaches local authorities, will determine how much of it can be spent by Christmas.

What to watch

Three things will show whether the target is credible. The first is the funding detail: the baseline, the timetable and the split between emergency beds and permanent homes. The second is the response of councils and homelessness charities, who hold the data and will say quickly whether the money has arrived. The third is the next official rough-sleeping snapshot and any winter-period counts, which will give the first independent measure of progress.

Until those details are published, the £900m is a commitment of intent. Its value will be judged by the number of people still sleeping outdoors in the new year.

Figures in this article come from early reporting by the Guardian and BBC and have not been independently verified.
