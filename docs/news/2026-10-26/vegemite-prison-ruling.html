Australian murderer loses bid to eat Vegemite in prison

In one sentence
Victoria's Supreme Court has dismissed a convicted murderer's claim that the state's prison Vegemite ban breaches his right to enjoy Australian culture.

A court in the Australian state of Victoria has turned down a convicted murderer's attempt to have Vegemite returned to prison menus. The ruling leaves in place a ban that has stood since 2006.

Andre McKechnie, who is serving a life sentence for murder at Barwon Prison, had argued that being denied the salty yeast spread breached his human right to enjoy Australian culture. He also said he needed it for his wellbeing. Justice Claire Harris of the Supreme Court of Victoria dismissed the challenge in a judgment released on Monday, according to ABC News and other Australian outlets. The full judgment text was not reviewed for this article, so the reasoning below follows press accounts of it.

The case for toast

Mr McKechnie's suit, filed in 2025, rested on two legs. The first was the Charter of Human Rights and Responsibilities Act, which protects the cultural rights of Victorians. The second was the Corrections Act, under which he said the state had failed to provide food adequate to maintain his wellbeing.

The argument had a certain appeal. Vegemite is as close as Australia comes to an edible national symbol, spread thinly on buttered toast in kitchens across the country and carried abroad in the luggage of homesick expatriates.

Why the jar is banned

Corrections Victoria has kept Vegemite out of prisons since 2006, giving two reasons. The spread can interfere with narcotic detection dogs, and inmates were reported to have smeared it on packages of illicit drugs to mask the scent. Its yeast also carries the potential to be used to brew alcohol.

Justice Harris accepted that Vegemite might be regarded as part of Australian culture. But she found that a prisoner's cultural rights are necessarily limited by imprisonment, and that the ban was a proportionate response to the security concerns. On the Corrections Act, she held that the duty to provide adequate food does not stretch to supplying foods of cultural significance to enhance a prisoner's wellbeing.

What comes next

The ruling is narrow. It does not say Vegemite is unimportant, only that a prison may restrict it for good security reasons. Whether Mr McKechnie appeals is not yet known. For now the ban stands, and the inmates of Barwon will have to find their toast toppings elsewhere.
