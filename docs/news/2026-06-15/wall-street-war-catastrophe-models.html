GEOPOLITICAL RISK · By M. Halloway · 2026-06-15 · 6 min read

Wall Street builds catastrophe models to price military conflict

The New York Stock Exchange trading floor. Systematic geopolitical risk modeling is changing how financial institutions price and hedge military conflict. — Eduard Hueber / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Catastrophe modeling firms best known for pricing hurricanes are adapting their methodology to quantify military conflict risk for Wall Street — with early results that impress and limitations that should concern.

In early January, Verisk Maplecroft ran a back-test on a model it hadn't yet shipped to clients. The question was simple: what probability would the system have assigned to war breaking out in Iran within twelve months? The answer was 66%. The model was trained on political, economic, and social data from 1995 to 2022 and had never seen the events that followed. US and Israeli strikes on Iranian nuclear sites came roughly six weeks later.

That number — not a qualitative judgment from a geopolitical desk, but a machine-learning output derived from measurable inputs — is the product that Wall Street has been waiting for. As military conflict has become an increasingly tangible line item in corporate losses, investors, banks, and insurers are turning to an unlikely source: the firms that have spent decades modeling hurricanes and earthquakes.


The floor of the New York Stock Exchange. Wall Street is increasingly treating geopolitical risk as a structured, modelable liability.

From Hurricane Tables to Conflict Scenarios

Catastrophe modeling has been the backbone of property insurance pricing for three decades. When a major insurer needs to price exposure to a hurricane in the Gulf of Mexico, it runs a probabilistic simulation — drawing on physics, historical storm tracks, and engineered loss functions — to produce an expected loss distribution. That distribution, not any individual forecast, drives the premium.

The same firms are now attempting something structurally similar with military conflict. Verisk Maplecroft, best known for its work on natural catastrophe models for insurers and cat-bond investors, released its Predictive War Index to clients in late May. The model uses machine learning to generate a twelve-month conflict probability for countries around the world, drawing on a Geopolitical Relations Index that tracks tension between country pairs by examining variables such as past military clashes, regime similarity, and geographic proximity.

"Instead of looking back, insurers and investors increasingly want to know what might happen and where," said Sam Haynes, head of data and analytics at Verisk Maplecroft.

Gordon Woo, a catastrophe risk specialist at Moody's, describes the framing: modeling experts are now treating conflict scenarios as they would a terrorist attack, where a relatively low-cost action — a missile strike, a port blockade — can generate losses that are wildly disproportionate to the physical damage. That asymmetry is exactly what catastrophe bonds and parametric insurance products are designed to price. The toolbox, in other words, travels.

Why Now: The Numbers Are Forcing the Issue

The abstract case for systematic geopolitical risk modeling has existed for years. What changed is the concrete ledger.

The number of countries engaged in external conflicts has nearly doubled since 2008, reaching 103 in 2026, according to the Institute for Economics and Peace. The global economic impact of violence stands at $21.81 trillion — 10.5% of world GDP — in 2025, the highest figure on record. Business assets located inside conflict zones have grown from 29,500 in the first quarter of 2021 to 36,000 today.

For insurers, the shift is most visible in marine premiums. Before the Iran conflict, war risk coverage for vessels transiting the Strait of Hormuz cost a fraction of a percent of the vessel's insured value per voyage. After US and Israeli strikes forced Iran to declare the strait effectively closed to Western-allied shipping, Lloyd's market premiums rose to between 1% and 1.5% — in some cases touching 2.5% before easing. That is not an abstraction; it is a pricing problem demanding a methodology.

The Allianz Risk Barometer 2026 — a survey of risk management professionals across nearly 100 countries — found that war had overtaken civil unrest as the political violence exposure companies feared most, drawing 53% of respondents, up from 48% in 2025. Political violence as a category reached its highest position ever in the barometer, ranking seventh overall among corporate concerns.

"It changes the distribution entirely," said Krishan Sharma, senior vice president for model risk management at Citigroup. His bank has warned publicly against relying on "rear-view mirror" models built on historical data. Morgan Stanley has called for a fundamental rethink of how geopolitical risks are integrated into standard frameworks.

Tina Fordham, co-founder of Fordham Global Foresight and former chief global political analyst at Citigroup, put the institutional mood plainly: "2025 saw the acceleration of the supercycle and it marked a wake-up call for the C-suite."

Early Evidence, Early Wins

Verisk's war model isn't the only system attracting attention. A separate government collapse model, launched by the firm in October 2023, has correctly predicted six of the seven government collapses that have occurred since then — including the ouster of Bashar al-Assad in Syria in late 2024 and the abrupt removal of Venezuela's Nicolás Maduro in January 2026.

The RAND Corporation has developed its own system — the Integrated Strategic Forecasting model, completed in February 2026 — which converts uncertain geopolitical scenarios into explicit probability estimates using AI. Anthony Vassalo, who leads RAND's Forecasting Initiative and previously served as a senior official at the US Office of the Director of National Intelligence, put the chance of Iran's regime failing to survive into 2027 at 20%. The model produced its estimate without access to classified intelligence, and its outputs were not reviewed by human analysts before publication — limitations RAND disclosed alongside the number.

That disclosure points toward the central tension in this new field.

The Precision Problem

Hurricanes obey physics. Wars obey decisions.

Natural catastrophe models have three decades of empirical calibration behind them, a stable underlying process to model, and quantified uncertainty bounds that underwriters have learned to interpret. The war models being launched today are working from training data that predates most of the current conflicts, in a geopolitical environment that practitioners describe as structurally different from the post-Cold War baseline.

Verisk's Predictive War Index was trained on data through 2022. It does not incorporate the current Iran conflict. Its back-test accuracy — 66% on Iran — is compelling, but a single out-of-sample hit is a thin basis for actuarial confidence. Government collapse models that score six of seven are doing well by any forecasting standard, but the seventh miss is a reminder that even high-performing models produce surprises at exactly the moments when surprises are most costly.

The risk is not that the models are wrong — all probabilistic models are wrong in individual cases. The risk is that sophisticated-looking outputs invite false confidence. A portfolio manager who hedges against conflict with the conviction that the model has "priced" the scenario may actually be hedging against a specific probabilistic distribution that has not been empirically validated in live markets.

Financial institutions are alert to this. Citigroup's warning against rear-view mirror models applies with equal force to forward-looking models that extrapolate from a data regime that may no longer hold.

What to Watch

The immediate test for these models is whether they inform actual hedging decisions or remain expensive research outputs with no downstream effect on portfolios. The Hormuz premium spike demonstrates that the insurance market will move in response to real events. The open question is whether systematic, model-driven pricing can get ahead of those events — or whether markets will continue to price war risk reactively, in the days after missiles fly.

Lloyd's chief executive has publicly emphasized the importance of keeping war-risk coverage available, a signal that the market sees its role in this space as something to be managed systematically rather than withdrawn from. If a cat-bond equivalent for geopolitical risk takes shape — parametric instruments triggered by defined conflict thresholds — it would represent the most concrete form of the institutionalization underway.

The next data point comes from the models themselves. The Predictive War Index is now running live, not in back-test. Whether its twelve-month probabilities — across 103 conflict-affected countries — translate into pricing changes that clients act on will determine whether this remains a specialist research product or becomes infrastructure.
