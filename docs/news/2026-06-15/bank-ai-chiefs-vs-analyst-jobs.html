Banks race to appoint chief AI officers as AI erases the analysts beneath them

Canary Wharf, London's financial district, where HSBC, Lloyds and other major banks are reshaping their executive structures and workforce around AI — Ank Kumar / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0
In one sentence
Banks are installing AI chiefs at $1.6m salaries while cutting the junior analysts beneath them — and even those new C-suite executives say the role may not survive its own success.

On April 1, HSBC appointed David Rice as its first-ever chief AI officer. Rice is a near-20-year HSBC veteran who most recently ran operations for the bank's corporate and institutional banking division — not an AI specialist, in other words, but a banker who knows the institution's plumbing. The hire followed Commonwealth Bank of Australia's appointment of Ranil Boteju, poached from Lloyds, to a nearly identical role. UBS had got there in October. The rest of the sector is accelerating: according to IBM's Institute for Business Value, the share of organizations with a dedicated chief AI officer climbed from 26 percent to 76 percent in just over a year.

By compensation, these are serious jobs. Data research firm Equilar puts the median package at roughly $1.6 million annually, with the ceiling approaching $3.5 million. Business schools are rushing to supply credentialed candidates: the University of Chicago's Booth School now offers a ten-month Chief AI Officer program for $28,000. Duke, Cornell, and Michigan have launched similar tracks. In Singapore, AI leadership roles already rank among the fastest-growing job listings on LinkedIn.

What is striking is that practitioners in these new roles are among the most candid about their impermanence.

A role with a sell-by date

Boteju, who left Lloyds to take the Commonwealth Bank job, describes his ambition plainly: "AI will be embedded into everything we do and we won't even know it's there." That is a vision of success, but it is also a description of redundancy. If AI becomes as ordinary as electricity, there is no more need for a chief AI officer than for a chief electricity officer.

Peng Zhao, chief executive of Citadel Securities, is blunter. Such roles "will not be necessary in the long run," he has said, "because AI will become part of the technology stack." David Hardoon, who served as Standard Chartered's global head of AI enablement until departing in March after less than a year, offered the sharpest version of the argument: "Do we have a chief Excel officer? Do we have a chief email officer? No."

That Hardoon lasted under twelve months — at a bank simultaneously planning to cut roughly 7,800 roles by 2030 partly through AI automation — is not an anomaly. It reflects something real about where the industry sits in its thinking. Banks are creating the role because they believe they need to be seen taking AI seriously at the C-suite level, and because they need someone to own governance, strategy, and vendor relationships. But the destination is unsettled. Whether the chief AI officer evolves into a permanent fixture or gets folded into the technology function is genuinely open. HSBC, notably, has simultaneously appointed Rice and expanded CTO Mario Shamtani's remit to include building a central AI platform — two executives, overlapping terrain.

The floor below is emptying

While banks debate the right org chart at the top, the picture one floor down is becoming clearer.

Finance analyst vacancies on UK recruitment site Adzuna have fallen from more than 350 four years ago to around 80 today. White-collar roles now account for only a quarter of London job listings, down from nearly half in 2022. Corporate lawyers, software developers, management consultants, and digital marketing managers have each seen postings shrink to double figures. The decline is broad enough to be a signal, narrow enough — one recruitment platform, one snapshot — to resist being called definitive.

The numbers behind the listings are larger. More than 300,000 London workers in bookkeeping, brokerage, and administrative roles face high displacement risk; a further 748,000 in economics, software development, and accounting face elevated exposure. Standard Chartered has confirmed plans to eliminate roughly 7,800 jobs globally by 2030, framing the exercise as reallocation toward AI investment rather than cost cutting. CEO Bill Winters chose the phrase carefully: the bank is "replacing in some cases lower-value human capital with the financial capital and the investment capital we're putting in." HSBC is reported to be weighing cuts of up to 20,000 — approximately 10 percent of its global workforce — over a three-to-five-year horizon.

At hedge funds, the hollowing out is already operational rather than planned. Junior analyst pools that ran to three or four seats have in some cases contracted to one, with the remaining analyst supervising AI outputs rather than producing analysis independently. "The demand for great portfolio managers has never been higher," said Sean Sweeney of recruitment firm CW Talent Solutions. "But the layer of people underneath them is disappearing fast."

Who feeds the pipeline?

The structural problem is a talent pipeline that has always been fed by entry-level roles. Andrew Hunter, co-founder of Adzuna, asks the question that banks have not yet answered publicly: "If you're not going to take a cohort of young people to train and develop over the next two to three years, how are you going to feed that pipeline of middle management in years to come?"

It is not that institutions are unaware of the tension — it is that it sits on the wrong side of the quarterly earnings cycle. The chief AI officers now being recruited, at packages that can reach $3.5 million, are by their own description building toward a future in which their specific role no longer needs to exist. Whether that transition takes three years or ten, the institutional pressure to have someone in the chair today is strong enough to support a $28,000 university certificate course and a global talent war.

The specific catalyst worth watching: whether any major bank publishes a workforce plan that addresses pipeline replacement alongside its automation targets. So far, the cuts have been announced without the sequels.
