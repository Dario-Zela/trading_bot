Trump claims Iran deal complete and Hormuz blockade lifted

The Strait of Hormuz, the narrow chokepoint between Iran and Oman through which roughly one-fifth of global seaborne energy transits. — Perry–Castañeda Library / University of Texas at Austin / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
Trump's Truth Social claim of a completed Iran deal has been confirmed by major outlets; oil fell ~5% as Hormuz blockade terms were revealed.

President Donald Trump declared on Sunday that a peace deal with Iran is "now complete," posting on Truth Social that he was authorizing "the toll free opening of the Strait of Hormuz" and ordering the immediate removal of the U.S. naval blockade. What began as a single-source alert from Benzinga rapidly filled in across every major wire: CNN, NBC News, PBS, Al Jazeera, NPR and CNBC all confirmed the agreement within hours. Iran's deputy foreign minister Kazem Gharibabadi separately confirmed the deal on state television. This is no rumour.

Pakistan's Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif, who served as the deal's principal broker alongside Qatar, announced that "both sides have declared the immediate and permanent termination of military operations on all fronts, including in Lebanon." A formal signing ceremony is scheduled for Friday in Switzerland.

What the deal actually says

The headline terms cover both the waterway and the nuclear file. On Hormuz: shipping resumes toll-free immediately, and the U.S. naval blockade — imposed in April after the Islamabad talks collapsed — is lifted in parallel. On sanctions: Washington will release $25 billion of frozen Iranian assets, impose no new sanctions before a final agreement, and waive oil export penalties currently on Tehran. On the nuclear side, Iran has agreed not to produce or acquire nuclear weapons, to halt new uranium enrichment, and to dilute its existing highly enriched stockpile inside the country. Iran currently holds 440.9 kilograms of uranium enriched to 60% purity — not weapons-grade, but uncomfortably close. A 60-day window opens for broader final-deal negotiations.

U.S. Vice President JD Vance described it as potentially the start of "a new era" in the Middle East. UK Prime Minister Keir Starmer called for ensuring the strait "remains fully and permanently open." French President Macron urged "swift and full implementation."

Oil's instant verdict

Markets had already been pricing the possibility of a resolution. Brent crude had been trading near $140 a barrel — up roughly 40% since the conflict began in earnest on February 28, when U.S. and Israeli airstrikes killed Iran's supreme leader and triggered the closure of the strait, through which about one-fifth of global energy flows. On Sunday, the announcement sent WTI down 4.85% to $80.76 a barrel; Brent fell 4.35% to $83.51. The war premium that had embedded itself in every cargo, airline ticket and manufacturing cost estimate began unwinding in real time.

What remains open

The deal leaves several threads loose. Fighting in Lebanon has not fully ceased, and the text merely commits both sides to "immediate and permanent termination" — enforcement mechanisms are unspecified. Israel, which has its own equities in the Iranian nuclear programme, has not endorsed the agreement and its reaction remains a live variable. Iranian state media framed the deal as a U.S. capitulation, a domestic narrative that may constrain Tehran's flexibility in the 60-day negotiation window.

The formal signing on Friday in Bern is the next hard checkpoint. If Iran's parliament and the U.S. Senate each resist ratification — different legal frameworks, similar political dynamics — the implementation timeline could slip even as the shooting stops.
