BP reshuffles board as North Sea windfall-tax dispute clouds strategy

The substructure of BP's Clair Ridge platform, North Sea — the basin BP may now exit entirely. — Grahame C Anderson / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
BP has confirmed Ian Tyler as its third chair in a year just as its chief executive weighs quitting the North Sea outright over a 78% windfall tax.

BP named Ian Tyler as permanent chair on Tuesday, closing out a boardroom crisis that has run since May and installing the company's third chair in less than a year. The appointment lands as the company's chief executive, Meg O'Neill, weighs walking away entirely from the North Sea — the basin BP has produced from for more than sixty years — after a windfall tax regime that now takes 78% of upstream profits.

Tyler, a non-executive director since April 2025 and former chief executive of Balfour Beatty, had been holding the chair on an interim basis since 26 May, when BP's board removed Albert Manifold with immediate effect over what it called "serious concerns" about "governance standards, oversight and conduct." Manifold, who had the backing of activist investor Elliott Management, disputed the board's account and took legal advice; BP has declined to elaborate on the specifics. He had lasted eight months in the job.

A board rebuilt in a year

Dame Amanda Blanc, the senior independent director who ran the search for Tyler's permanent replacement, said he "brings significant experience providing challenge and support to executive teams, while maintaining strong governance and oversight." Blanc herself will not stand for re-election at BP's 2027 annual meeting and will leave once a successor as senior independent director is found. At April's AGM, non-executive directors Melody Meyer, Karen Richardson and Simon Henry all departed, and Satish Pai took over the safety and sustainability committee. BP now says the majority of its ten-strong board has been appointed within the last five years — a turnover rate the company's own governance disclosures flag as a risk in its own right, quite apart from the reasons behind any individual exit.

The reshuffle has coincided with, rather than been publicly blamed on, the tax dispute. But the timing is difficult to separate from the substance: Tyler takes over a board that must sign off on whatever O'Neill decides about the company's oldest production region, at the moment that decision has become existential rather than routine.

The North Sea calculation

The Energy Profits Levy, layered on top of ordinary and ring-fence corporation tax, now pushes the headline rate on North Sea oil and gas profits to 78% — among the highest effective tax rates applied to domestic hydrocarbon production in the OECD. BP's most recent annual report said Labour's decision to raise the levy by three percentage points and extend it from 2028 to 2030 cost the company an extra £539 million, and warned the policy had "created significant uncertainty for the UK's oil and gas industry."

O'Neill, who took over as chief executive on 1 April after nine years running Australia's Woodside Energy, ordered a review of BP's UK offshore business shortly after arriving. That review has since escalated: BP held advanced talks to sell its entire North Sea production portfolio to Ithaca Energy for close to £2 billion, and although those talks stalled, the company says it remains open to other buyers as part of a wider $20 billion divestment programme running to the end of the year. O'Neill has put the logic plainly, saying the North Sea business "will be better positioned as part of another company" as BP concentrates capital on its "highest-value opportunities" — language that reads less like a temporary retreat than an admission the basin no longer fits the portfolio at this tax rate.

What it signals

Taken together, the two threads point the same direction. A board rebuilt three times over in a year gives O'Neill a governance structure freshly installed and, in Tyler, a chair with no personal history tied to the North Sea strategy he now oversees. A tax regime that erases most of the upside from mature offshore fields gives her the commercial case to leave. Neither BP nor Tyler has said the appointment was made because of the tax dispute, and the board changes trace to a conduct issue with Manifold that predates the North Sea review. But the practical effect is that BP now has a governance apparatus unencumbered by the assets it may be about to sell.

The next marker is the divestment programme's year-end deadline. If BP lands a North Sea sale before then, it will be one of the clearest instances yet of a UK sector-specific tax reshaping which parts of the country's oil and gas base a major is willing to hold — with implications for Aberdeen's supply chain, and for how the Treasury weighs windfall levies against investment in the fields it is trying to tax.
