Maltese tycoon acquitted over murder of journalist Daphne Caruana Galizia

A memorial to Daphne Caruana Galizia on Tower Road, Sliema, Malta. — Otter / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
A Maltese jury acquitted tycoon Yorgen Fenech of ordering journalist Daphne Caruana Galizia's 2017 murder, leaving the case's central question unanswered.

A Maltese jury on Wednesday acquitted the businessman accused of ordering the murder of investigative journalist Daphne Caruana Galizia, closing — without resolving — the most consequential criminal case in the country's history.

Yorgen Fenech, heir to the Tumas Group property and casino empire, had stood accused of masterminding the October 2017 car-bomb killing that ended Caruana Galizia's life and, within months, brought down a Maltese government. Jurors in Valletta cleared him of both complicity in murder and criminal association to commit murder by an 8-1 margin, after eight hours of deliberation at the close of a trial that had run since July 1.

A killing that toppled a government

Caruana Galizia, 53, was blown up in her car near her home on 16 October 2017, months after her reporting on the Panama Papers tied Malta's prime minister, his chief of staff and a cabinet minister to secret offshore companies allegedly set up to receive payments from Azerbaijan. She had also been investigating Fenech's own offshore vehicle, 17 Black, and its links to a lucrative state gas-power concession awarded to a consortium in which his family group held a stake. The murder triggered mass street protests and, in 2019, the resignation of prime minister Joseph Muscat, whose chief of staff and a minister were both named in her final, unpublished stories.

Five men have already been convicted over the killing itself. Brothers Alfred and George Degiorgio, who planted and detonated the bomb, were sentenced to 40 years each in 2022; an accomplice, Vincent Muscat, pleaded guilty in 2021 and received 15 years; two further men linked to supplying the explosives were sentenced to life terms in 2025. Fenech was the one defendant accused of standing behind the triggermen — the case's central, unanswered question of who ordered the hit and why.

The case against him, and the doubt that undid it

Prosecutors alleged Fenech paid roughly €150,000 through a middleman, Melvin Theuma, to arrange the killing, motivated by fear that Caruana Galizia's reporting would expose his ownership of 17 Black and imperil the Tumas Group's business. Theuma, a taxi driver who brokered the deal, was granted a presidential pardon in exchange for testifying and became the prosecution's central witness.

It was Theuma's credibility that the defence spent the trial dismantling — the terms of his pardon, gaps in how his account was corroborated, and the failure to preserve or independently secure other evidence. Fenech testified in his own defence, rejecting the prosecution's account and pointing instead to Muscat's former chief of staff, Keith Schembri, as the real architect of the murder, casting his payments to Theuma as extortion rather than a contract killing. Jurors, by their own lawyers' account, evidently were not persuaded beyond reasonable doubt that Fenech gave the order — while apparently suspecting someone else did.

Outrage, and an appeal to come

The reaction was swift and unified in its dismay. Caruana Galizia's family said the verdict meant that "the institutional failures that enabled her murder remain unaddressed and unreformed," nine years on. Reporters Without Borders called itself "beyond outraged," noting that Maltese authorities have now failed to convict anyone identified as the mastermind of a murder that a 2021 public inquiry found the Maltese state itself had contributed to, through a culture of impunity that left journalists exposed. Prosecutors are expected to appeal.

For Malta, a state whose EU credentials have already been strained by the case — the European Parliament passed a resolution on rule-of-law concerns tied to the murder in 2023 — the acquittal reopens rather than closes the question of accountability. The appeal will determine whether the case returns to court; either way, the identity of who ordered Caruana Galizia's death remains, officially, unresolved.
