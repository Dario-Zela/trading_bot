Amgen's tezepelumab posts positive phase 3 results in eosinophilic oesophagitis

In one sentence
TEZSPIRE met both primary goals in a 368-patient Phase 3 oesophagitis trial, but topline-only data leave its edge over Dupixent unproven.

Amgen and AstraZeneca said on 27 August that tezepelumab — sold as TEZSPIRE — hit both co-primary goals in a Phase 3 trial testing it against eosinophilic oesophagitis, a chronic allergic condition in which white blood cells inflame the oesophagus and make swallowing difficult. It is the drug's third disease target, after severe asthma and chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps, and the companies now plan to take the data to regulators.[^1][^2]

The headline is easy to state and hard to weigh without the numbers, which the companies have so far withheld pending a medical conference presentation. What is public is the trial design and the shape of the result — enough to say this is a real, well-powered readout, not a routine incremental update.

What the trial actually measured

The Phase 3 study, called CROSSING, enrolled 368 patients aged 12 to 80 with symptomatic, biopsy-confirmed eosinophilic oesophagitis, randomised 1:1:1 to low-dose TEZSPIRE, high-dose TEZSPIRE, or placebo, all given as a subcutaneous injection every four weeks.[^2] It used two co-primary endpoints, a stricter bar than a single-endpoint trial: histologic remission, defined as an eosinophil count of six or fewer per high-power field of oesophageal tissue, and improvement on the Dysphagia Symptom Questionnaire, a validated measure of how often and how severely patients struggle to swallow. Both were met at 24 weeks, for both doses, and the companies say the effect held through 52 weeks — a detail that matters more than the initial result, since durability is what distinguishes a treatment patients will stay on from one that fades. All key secondary endpoints, including endoscopic and inflammatory remission measures, were also met. Amgen's R&D chief, Jay Bradner, said the drug "improved both underlying inflammation and swallowing difficulties that make eosinophilic esophagitis disruptive for patients," while the trial's lead investigator, Arjan Bredenoord, called the sustained 52-week results "impressive."[^1] Safety was described only as "generally consistent" with the drug's existing approvals — a phrase that, absent an adverse-event table, this correspondent treats as reassuring but not yet verifiable.

The competitive stakes

Eosinophilic oesophagitis is not a niche curiosity: Amgen and AstraZeneca cite more than 470,000 diagnosed cases in the US, a prevalence that has risen roughly fivefold since 2009, and note that about half of patients are inadequately controlled on existing first-line treatments such as proton-pump inhibitors and topical steroids.[^1] Until now the only approved biologic for the disease has been Sanofi and Regeneron's Dupixent, which worked from a different angle, blocking interleukin-4 and interleukin-13 further downstream in the inflammatory cascade. TEZSPIRE instead blocks thymic stromal lymphopoietin, an upstream signalling molecule the companies describe as sitting "at the top" of several inflammatory pathways — a mechanistic distinction that may or may not translate into a clinical edge once head-to-head or comparative data exist. Dupixent's scale gives a sense of what is at stake: it took in $17.8bn globally in 2025, against TEZSPIRE's $1.9bn, up from $1.3bn in 2024.[^3] A successful entry into oesophagitis would not close that gap on its own, but it would be the first real crack in Dupixent's monopoly on an approved biologic for the disease.

What is still missing

The release is a topline summary — the kind pharmaceutical companies issue to satisfy disclosure obligations ahead of a scientific conference, not a data package. There are no absolute remission rates, no effect sizes, no confidence intervals, and no comparison to Dupixent's own trial results in the same disease, all of which will matter for prescribers weighing one biologic against another. The companies say full results will be presented at an unspecified upcoming medical meeting, and only then will it be possible to judge whether this is a meaningfully better option for patients or merely a second entrant with similar efficacy and a different injection schedule.

Amgen shares have already had a reasonably strong August, with several banks — including Piper Sandler, Morgan Stanley and BMO Capital — lifting price targets after the company's second-quarter earnings, though those revisions predate this specific readout and cannot be attributed to it alone.[^4] The clearer catalyst is the regulatory filing: eosinophilic oesophagitis is a smaller, better-defined population than asthma, which should make for a comparatively fast review once Amgen submits the full data package. Watch for the conference presentation of complete results, and for the FDA filing that would start the clock on a possible label expansion.
