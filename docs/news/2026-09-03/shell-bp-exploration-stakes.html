Shell takes exploration stakes in BP's Brazil and Gulf of Mexico prospects

Shell's Perdido platform in the deepwater Gulf of Mexico, where the company already operates alongside BP. — Mike Duhon / Bureau of Safety and Environmental Enforcement, via Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
Shell is taking minority stakes in BP-operated exploration blocks off Brazil and in the US Gulf, a modest but telling bet on long-cycle deepwater oil even as BP retreats from the high-tax North Sea.

Shell has agreed to buy into two of BP's flagship deepwater exploration prospects — one off Brazil, one in the US Gulf — deepening a partnership between the two majors just as BP retreats from higher-tax, lower-return acreage closer to home.

Under the deal, announced this week, Shell takes a 50% stake in the Tupinambá block in Brazil's pre-salt Santos Basin, roughly 400km offshore in water around 2,300 metres deep. BP keeps the other half and stays operator. In the Gulf, Shell buys 30% of five leases covering the Conifer prospect in the Keathley Canyon area, about 250 miles southwest of New Orleans and near BP's Kaskida development; BP retains 70% and operatorship there too. Neither company has disclosed what Shell is paying.

Two basins, one calculation

The two prospects sit in geology that has rewarded patience and punished it in roughly equal measure. Tupinambá lies in the same pre-salt trend where BP made what it called its largest discovery in 25 years last year, at the nearby Bumerangue block, after winning Tupinambá as the sole bidder in a December 2023 licensing round. A first well is expected to spud soon. Conifer sits in the Gulf's Paleogene formation — ultra-deep, high-pressure rock that BP has spent much of the past decade learning to drill economically, most recently at Kaskida, a $5bn, 20,000-psi hub sanctioned in 2024 with output expected to start in 2029. Conifer drilling isn't due until 2027.

For BP, bringing in a partner does what farm-downs are meant to do: it spreads the cost of drilling wells that may or may not find anything, while letting BP keep the operatorship, and the option value, if they do. For Shell, it buys exposure to acreage in basins where it already has a presence, without the capital outlay or the drilling risk that comes with operating.

Expansion here, retreat there

The timing is pointed. BP's new chief executive, Meg O'Neill, who took over in April after Murray Auchincloss's departure, has been unwinding the low-carbon push of the Bernard Looney era and refocusing spending on oil and gas — while simultaneously moving to quit the UK North Sea altogether, citing an effective 78% tax rate on production there under Labour's extended windfall levy. In June, BP said it was also exploring selling down stakes in Kaskida and Tiber, the very Gulf projects that sit near Conifer. The pattern is capital discipline rather than retreat: BP is shedding acreage where the tax and returns don't work, and inviting partners into acreage where the exploration upside might.

Shell's calculus points the same way. Chief executive Wael Sawan has spent the past three years trimming Shell's portfolio toward fewer, higher-margin deepwater and LNG positions rather than spreading capital thin. A minority, non-operated stake in two frontier exploration blocks fits that template: modest outlay, optionality on a discovery, no obligation to run the rig.

What it signals for the sector

None of this is a bet that oil demand accelerates from here. It is a bet that whatever demand exists in the 2030s and beyond will still reward barrels that are cheap to produce once found — and that deepwater pre-salt and Paleogene reservoirs, despite years-long lead times, still clear that bar better than high-tax, mature basins like the North Sea. Both companies continue to talk up renewables and LNG in investor presentations, but capital is flowing toward exploration wells that won't produce a barrel before the end of the decade. That is a longer-cycle wager than most of the transition rhetoric assumes.

The next markers are mechanical rather than dramatic: the Tupinambá well result, whenever BP confirms spud, and Brazilian regulatory sign-off on the transaction, which remains outstanding. Conifer drilling, and any read-through for the wider Kaskida-Tiber sale process, is a 2027 story.
