MARKETS · CHINA · By M. Halloway · 2026-09-03 · 3 min read

China's factory and services activity both stay in contraction

Workers assemble electronics on a factory line in Shenzhen — manufacturing PMI rose slightly in August but stayed below the 50-point expansion threshold. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
China's factory PMI edged up to 49.8 and non-manufacturing held at 49.0 — both still in contraction, with construction the main drag.

China's two largest engines of growth are both misfiring at once. Official data released this week show factory activity contracting for a second straight month while the services and construction sector — measured separately as the non-manufacturing PMI — sits at the same contractionary level it held a month earlier. Neither gauge has crossed back above the 50-point line that separates expansion from decline, and the overlap matters more than either number alone.

The manufacturing purchasing managers' index, published by China's National Bureau of Statistics, rose to 49.8 in August from 49.2 in July — a touch better than the 49.7 economists polled by Reuters had expected, but still below the 50 threshold for the second month running. The non-manufacturing PMI held at 49.0, unchanged from July and also in contraction.

Two sectors, one direction

Coverage of Chinese data often reaches for a "mixed signals" framing — manufacturing improving, services softening, take your pick. August doesn't support that reading. Both indices sit below 50. Both have done so for at least two consecutive months. The direction of travel is the same even where the details diverge.

Under the manufacturing headline, the composition was less bleak than the topline number implies: the production sub-index rose to 50.4 and new orders to 50.6, both back into expansion, while new export orders also flipped positive. What dragged the aggregate down were raw-materials inventories and employment, both still contracting. NBS chief statistician Huo Lihui has attributed part of the manufacturing softness to unusually hot weather curbing outdoor and energy-intensive activity earlier in the summer.

Non-manufacturing told a more lopsided story. Within it, the services sub-index came in at 49.3, also flat versus July, though pockets ran hot — postal, telecoms, broadcasting and IT services all posted readings above 55. The drag came from construction, where the business-activity sub-index fell 0.1 point to 46.9. Huo pointed to extreme weather — heavy rain and typhoons — disrupting site work in several regions, layered on top of a property sector that has been shrinking for years.

Why the composite reading matters

A composite PMI output index, which blends manufacturing and non-manufacturing activity, ticked up only marginally, from 49.3 to 49.5 — itself still below 50. That is the cleanest single number for the state of Chinese demand right now, and it says growth momentum has not turned a corner, even as individual sub-indices show occasional green shoots.

The broader backdrop: China's GDP growth slowed to 4.3% in the second quarter, the weakest pace since late 2022, as soft domestic demand and the prolonged property downturn continue to weigh on activity. A synchronized sub-50 reading across factories and services is consistent with that slowdown rather than a contradiction of it — it's confirmation that weakness has broadened rather than concentrated in one sector.

For markets, the read-through runs through the usual channels: CNY pairs, Chinese equities, and commodities exposed to Chinese construction and manufacturing demand — iron ore, copper, steel — all key off signs of durable stabilization in Chinese demand, and August's data doesn't provide one. Economists cited alongside the release expect some improvement later in the year as the weather effects fade and local governments accelerate fiscal spending, but that is a forecast, not yet a data point.

What's next

The next official PMI reading lands in early October, covering September, and will show whether August's construction weather effects — and the still-negative employment sub-index — are transient or the start of a longer slide. Beijing's fiscal-spending pace over the next quarter, and any fresh property-sector support measures, are the more direct levers to watch for signs the contraction is bottoming out.
