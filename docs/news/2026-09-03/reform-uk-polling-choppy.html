Reform UK's polling turns choppy as Farage's personal ratings sink

Nigel Farage, whose personal favourability rating has fallen to -44 even as Reform UK's poll numbers whipsaw between first and third place. — Laurie Noble / UK Parliament, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
Reform UK's poll standing has swung from a record 35% to third place within months, while Farage's own favourability has collapsed to -44.

Reform UK has spent the summer as the party everyone loves to poll — and pollsters can no longer agree on what they're measuring. In May, BMG Research put Nigel Farage's party on a record 35% for The i, a 15-point lead over Labour that implied a landslide of more than 400 seats. By the last weekend of August, More in Common had Reform down to 22%, squeezed into third place behind Labour on 26% and the Conservatives on 23% — the first time Kemi Badenoch's party has out-polled Reform in eighteen months. YouGov's most recent tracker, taken the same weekend, showed Labour and Reform locked together on 23% apiece, with the Tories trailing on 20%. PollCheck's seven-poll moving average as of 2 September splits the difference: Labour 25.6%, Reform 23.6%, Conservatives 20.1%.

That is a wide spread for parties supposedly being measured by the same electorate in the same fortnight, and it matters beyond Westminster's scoreboard. Sterling and UK equities have spent months pricing in some probability of a Reform-shaped government, or at least a Reform-shaped veto over whoever leads one. A party whose support swings by double digits depending on which pollster rings that week makes that probability much harder to price.

Farage's own numbers are worse than his party's

The more striking figure sits with the leader rather than the party. YouGov's August favourability tracker put Farage's net rating at -44 — 23% of Britons view him favourably against 67% unfavourably, with 55% saying "very unfavourably." That is down sharply from -27 in May, a 17-point fall in three months that came as Reform was still riding high in the seat-share polling. Leader and party, in other words, have decoupled: Farage's personal brand is souring faster than voting intention for Reform is falling, which is unusual for a party this closely built around one man.

The proximate cause is money. Parliament's standards commissioner is investigating an undeclared £5m gift Farage received before the 2024 election from Christopher Harborne, a Thailand-based cryptocurrency entrepreneur. Separately, police are examining a £500,000 donation from the mother of George Cottrell — a long-time Farage associate convicted in the US in 2016 of offering to launder drug-trafficking proceeds — over whether it breached rules on permissible donors. Reform says none of the money required declaration under the rules as they stood at the time; the scrutiny has nonetheless dragged on for weeks.

What the divergence means

Some of the gap between pollsters is house-effect noise — methodology, weighting, turnout models — that has always existed and always will. But a swing from a 15-point Reform lead in May to third place in one late-August poll is larger than house effects alone typically explain, and it lines up with the donations story and Farage's collapsing favourability. The more plausible read is that Reform's coalition, built fast on protest-vote energy, is now testing how much of that support was tied to Farage's personal standing rather than to the party as an institution.

The next test is less about a single poll than about whether the trend holds: further favourability trackers from YouGov and Ipsos are due this month, and any findings from the standards commissioner's inquiry would give markets a firmer signal than the polling noise has so far provided.
