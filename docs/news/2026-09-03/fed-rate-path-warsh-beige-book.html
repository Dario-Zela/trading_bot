Fed's Beige Book points to a stalling economy as Warsh's hawkish turn muddies the rate outlook

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C., headquarters of the central bank. — AgnosticPreachersKid / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
The Fed's Beige Book shows flat hiring and sticky prices at once, and Chair Warsh's hawkish Jackson Hole pivot makes Friday's jobs report a pivotal test for rate-hike odds.

Kevin Warsh has been Federal Reserve chairman for less than four months, and he has already managed to unsettle a market that spent most of the summer betting on rate cuts. On Wednesday the Fed's Beige Book — the central bank's periodic read on economic conditions across its twelve districts, compiled from interviews with businesses, bankers and community contacts — described an economy that is neither collapsing nor accelerating. It is stalling, quietly, while prices keep climbing anyway. That combination is precisely the one a central bank dreads, and it now collides with a chairman who has told markets, in no uncertain terms, that inflation is his priority.

What the Beige Book actually said

The report, covering conditions from early July through late August and published September 2, found that ten of the twelve Federal Reserve districts logged growth in the slight-to-moderate range, with two reporting no change at all. Employment growth was, in the Fed's own phrasing, "very slight": three districts saw modest hiring gains, four saw slight gains, and five reported no change whatsoever. Wage growth held at a modest-to-moderate pace — enough to keep workers roughly even with the cost of living, not enough to signal a labor market running hot.

Prices told a different story. Eight of the twelve districts reported moderate increases, one saw a robust rise, and manufacturers in particular flagged mounting input costs tied to energy, raw materials, tariffs and transportation. Contacts singled out the run-up in oil prices — a consequence of the collapse of the Iran ceasefire that has pushed crude above $91 a barrel — as a growing strain on both business costs and household budgets heading into the winter heating season.

Put plainly: hiring has gone flat and inflation hasn't. That is the textbook definition of a stagflationary squeeze, and it leaves the Fed's two mandates — maximum employment and stable prices — pulling in opposite directions. A soft labor market normally argues for lower rates. Persistent price pressure argues for higher ones. The Beige Book doesn't resolve that tension; it sharpens it.

Warsh's inflation fixation

Into that tension stepped Kevin Warsh, confirmed as Fed chair by a bruising 54-45 Senate vote in May after President Trump nominated him to succeed Jerome Powell. At the Jackson Hole symposium on August 28, Warsh used his first major address as chair to correct what critics called a muddled performance at his first post-meeting press conference in July. There was nothing muddled this time. Warsh recommitted the Fed to its 2% PCE inflation target, argued that recent encouraging readings don't yet amount to "meaningful" progress, and said plainly that price stability — not the labor market — is where the Fed's attention belongs right now.

Markets took the hint. Odds of a rate hike at the September 15-16 FOMC meeting, as tracked by CME Group's FedWatch tool, jumped from roughly 35% before the speech to more than 66% after it — nearly double, and a sharp reversal from early August, when a dismal July jobs report (payrolls fell 23,000 against a consensus forecast of a 83,000 gain) had pushed hike odds down toward the mid-30s. The 10-year Treasury yield, already grinding higher for weeks, pushed to its highest levels since early 2025 on the repricing, adding to the pressure on equity valuations that has rattled AI-linked stocks in recent sessions.

What makes Warsh's stance notable is less the hawkishness itself than the fact that it runs directly against the grain of the data his own Beige Book just published. Flat hiring across half the Fed's districts would, in a normal cycle, be grounds for easing. Warsh is arguing the opposite case, and doing so as a chairman whose confirmation was itself contentious — a signal, some analysts say, of a Fed board more internally divided on priorities than at any point in recent memory.

Friday's jobs report as the tiebreaker

That argument now has an immediate test. The Bureau of Labor Statistics releases the August employment report on Friday, September 4, and the usual reaction function has effectively inverted. Where a soft print once meant rate relief and a market rally, this time a weak August number would undercut Warsh's hawkish case and could push hike odds back down — while a strong print, confirming his inflation-first read, would likely cement a September move. Economists' forecasts vary widely, from a gain of roughly 58,000 jobs to as much as 110,000, with the unemployment rate expected to hold near 4.1%. Given July's outright miss, the range of plausible outcomes — and market reactions — is unusually wide.

What it means for positioning

The throughline across the Beige Book, Warsh's Jackson Hole speech and Friday's looming jobs number is that the Fed's rate-cut path, which traders spent much of the spring and early summer pricing in with some confidence, has effectively been shelved. A chairman focused on "sticky" inflation, an economy generating almost no new jobs, and energy costs rising on the back of a collapsed Middle East ceasefire together describe a policy environment that looks far closer to "higher for longer" than to imminent easing — regardless of which way Friday's payrolls print breaks. Even a soft number is more likely to stall a hike than to revive cut expectations, given how explicitly Warsh has now nailed his colors to the inflation mast.

For risk assets, that argues for continued pressure on rate-sensitive valuations — the same dynamic already visible in wobbling AI-related equities and multi-year-high Treasury yields — until either inflation data genuinely rolls over or the labor market deteriorates badly enough to force the Fed's hand regardless of Warsh's stated priorities. The next data points worth watching after Friday's payrolls: the August CPI print in mid-September, which lands just days before the FOMC meets, and the University of Michigan's inflation-expectations survey, which will show whether Warsh's rhetoric is itself feeding the price pressures he says he's fighting.
