Anthropic weighs near-$1trn IPO as Lutnick signals thaw with Trump administration

Dario Amodei, Anthropic's chief executive, pictured in 2023. — Simon Walker / No 10 Downing Street, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Anthropic is reportedly weighing an IPO near $1trn after $80bn in new compute deals, but the sourcing is thin and the real story is AI's ballooning capital intensity.

Anthropic's public listing is turning into one of the more confused stories in tech finance. Depending on which outlet you read this week, the AI lab is heading for a valuation anywhere from $965 billion — the level of its last private funding round — to $2 trillion or more, with a listing pencilled in for "late September," "September or early October," or simply "fall." Stratechery and Tech Startups have both floated the trillion-dollar figure, tying it to a $35 billion compute agreement Anthropic just signed with the cloud provider Lambda. None of it has been confirmed by Anthropic, by a financial regulator, or by the banks reportedly running the deal.

What is confirmed, and rather more interesting, is the money actually going into the ground, and a separate thaw in Anthropic's relationship with the White House that had been anything but warm.

The compute bill

Anthropic signed the Lambda deal on 31 August, committing $35 billion over several years for roughly 350 megawatts of Nvidia GPU capacity at a data centre in Nueces County, Texas, being built by Hut 8, a former crypto-mining firm now pivoting to AI infrastructure. Nvidia both supplies the chips Lambda deploys and is reported to hold the lease on the facility itself — a structure that keeps Nvidia's silicon, capital and customer relationships knotted together across the deal. It follows a $45 billion commitment Anthropic made days earlier for capacity at an Nscale campus in West Virginia. Two contracts, one week, $80 billion of forward compute spending.

That is the real story sitting underneath the IPO chatter: frontier AI labs are now locking in compute years ahead of demand, at sums that dwarf what most public companies spend on capital expenditure in a decade. Anthropic's revenue run rate reportedly reached roughly $65 billion by late July, more than doubling in a single quarter — but even that growth curve strains to justify $80 billion in fresh cloud commitments made in a week. A public listing, on this reading, isn't primarily about giving early investors an exit. It's about opening a channel to the kind of capital that private funding rounds, however large, can't sustain indefinitely.

Reading the sourcing carefully

The trillion-dollar IPO figure deserves more scepticism than it's getting. Anthropic's confidential S-1, filed with the SEC on 1 June, has not become public — confidential filings don't. The reported plan is a prospectus release after Labor Day, an investor day in mid-September, and a Nasdaq listing in late September or early October, with underwriters said to include Morgan Stanley, Goldman Sachs and JPMorgan. But the valuation floated alongside that timeline ranges wildly depending on the outlet: some cite the $965 billion mark from Anthropic's last funding round: others report bankers pitching investors on $2 trillion, or even $3 trillion. None of these numbers comes from Anthropic itself. They come from people briefed on private conversations, filtered through tech blogs and newsletters rather than confirmed by the company or SEC filings. Treat the "near $1 trillion" figure as a plausible midpoint of investor chatter, not a settled number.

The politics catch up

The IPO push is landing just as Anthropic's fight with Washington cools. In February, President Trump directed federal agencies to stop using Anthropic's technology after the company refused Pentagon demands to lift usage restrictions on its models for mass domestic surveillance and fully autonomous weapons — a standoff Anthropic met with two federal lawsuits alleging illegal retaliation. It won the first round on 28 August, when a judge ruled the administration's "supply-chain risk" label against the company was unlawful.

Four days later, Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick told Axios's Mike Allen that the administration now trusts Anthropic. Asked directly whether he trusts chief executive Dario Amodei, Lutnick said: "We trust Anthropic. They've done what we asked. They're back on the right side. So the answer is: Yes." He offered no detail on what, specifically, changed — the comment arrived alongside Anthropic co-founder Tom Brown taking a more visible role at this week's G20 Innovation Ministerial, but Lutnick didn't connect the dots for reporters, and nobody has yet pinned down whether a policy concession, a court loss for the government, or simple political convenience did the repairing.

What ties it together

Neither thread stands up alone. The IPO reporting is speculative and inconsistently sourced; the Lutnick quote is a soundbite with no policy substance behind it. But together they describe a company trying to lock in two different kinds of legitimacy at once — financial-market access to fund an $80 billion compute buildout, and political cover in Washington after a genuine legal fight with the Pentagon. Whether the listing lands in late September, October, or later, and whether it prices at $965 billion or $2 trillion, the more durable signal is that Anthropic — and by extension its frontier-lab rivals — now treat locking in gigawatts of compute years in advance as a precondition for staying competitive, not a bet on future demand materialising. The next real data point is the prospectus itself, whenever Anthropic actually files it publicly; until then, the valuation numbers in circulation are guesses dressed up as reporting.
