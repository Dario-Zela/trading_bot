Olympic champion Davis-Woodhall says she's 'lucky to be alive' after car crash

In one sentence
Olympic long jump champion Tara Davis-Woodhall says she's "lucky to be alive" after a head-on crash left her concussed and in doubt for next week's Ultimate Championships.

Tara Davis-Woodhall, the reigning Olympic and world long jump champion, says she is "lucky and blessed to be alive" after a head-on car crash last week has left her doubtful for athletics' newest global championship, just over a week away.

The 27-year-old American was pulling into a parking lot on her way to a training session when another driver turned into her path. "We were both going about 20 miles per hour, so both of our cars were totalled," she said. Neither driver suffered life-threatening injuries, but Davis-Woodhall came away with a severe concussion — the kind of injury that offers no clean timeline for recovery.

"I didn't have any life-threatening injuries. I have a severe concussion," she said. "There's not much more I can say about the accident, but it's very scary." Physically, she says she has come through relatively intact. The bigger unknown is upstairs. "I'm physically OK. Mentally, something's going on in there," she said. "I don't know where my head's at right now. I'm just making it day by day."

A championship debut, and a cruel decision

The timing is bad in a specific way. Davis-Woodhall was heading into the crash at what she describes as career-best form — "I'm at the peak of my training right now. I'm as fast as I've ever been. I'm as strong as I've ever been" — with the inaugural World Athletics Ultimate Championships due to open in Budapest on September 11. It's a new event on the calendar, gathering the top performers from the year's Diamond League and championship circuits, and Davis-Woodhall was expected to headline the long jump field as both Olympic and world champion.

Instead, she says she hasn't trained since the crash. "I've seen my doctors and my physios, and everyone's just trying to get me back into it," she said, without committing either way on Budapest. "This decision is very difficult for me. I don't know where I am right now. So, day by day."

That leaves organisers, sponsors and fans in a holding pattern of their own. A concussion diagnosis doesn't come with a fixed recovery window — return-to-training decisions in cases like this are typically driven by symptom resolution rather than a calendar date, which is precisely why Davis-Woodhall isn't yet able to say whether nine days is enough.

What's next

Davis-Woodhall is expected to give a clearer update on her participation closer to the Budapest championships, which run September 11-13. Until then, her camp's messaging has been consistent: a physical recovery that seems to be going to plan, and a mental one that isn't on anyone's schedule.
