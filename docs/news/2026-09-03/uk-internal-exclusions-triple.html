England secondary schools' use of 'internal exclusion' nearly triples in three years

An English secondary school building — internal exclusion keeps pupils on site but out of mainstream classrooms. — Trevor Fowler / Geograph, CC BY-SA 2.0
In one sentence
Internal exclusion of English secondary pupils rose from 5% to 13% in three years, a practice invisible in official exclusion statistics.

The proportion of secondary school pupils in England removed from ordinary lessons and placed in isolation rose from 5% in the 2022/23 academic year to 13% in 2025/26 — a rise of 160% in three years, according to data shared with the Guardian by Arbor, a school management information system used by roughly 13,000 schools across the country.

The practice is known as "internal exclusion." A pupil stays on the school roll and on the school site, but is pulled out of mainstream classes and placed in a separate room — sometimes called an isolation booth, a reflection room or a reset unit — often working alone or in silence for part or all of a school day. Unlike a suspension or permanent exclusion, the pupil never leaves the building, and the incident never appears in the exclusion figures the Department for Education publishes each year.

That distinction matters more than it sounds. Formal exclusions and suspensions are counted, published and scrutinised — they can trigger safeguarding reviews and count against a school's performance record. Internal exclusion sits outside that system entirely. The government does not collect national data on it at all; the Arbor figures, drawn from real school records rather than a survey, are among the first attempts to quantify a practice that has expanded largely out of public view.

Who is being isolated

The rise is not evenly spread. Among disadvantaged pupils — those eligible for free school meals in the past six years — 22% were internally excluded at least once in 2025/26, up from 8% three years earlier, a 175% increase. That compares with 10% of their non-disadvantaged classmates. Eighteen percent of pupils with an education, health and care plan, which covers many of the most vulnerable children in the school system, were removed from class at least once over the same period. Primary schools barely feature in the trend — fewer than 1% of primary pupils were internally excluded, around 5,000 children nationally — which marks this out as a secondary-school phenomenon tied to the pressures of GCSE-era behaviour management.

A study published this year in the British Educational Research Journal, drawing on the #BeeWell survey of more than 34,000 pupils across 121 mainstream secondary schools in Greater Manchester, found one in twelve pupils reported being placed in isolation at least once a week, often for a full school day at a time. The same research found internal exclusion was disproportionately applied to marginalised groups, including LGBTQ+ pupils and those with special educational needs, and linked it to weaker pupil-staff relationships, a diminished sense of belonging at school, and — specifically among girls — worse mental wellbeing. In a related survey cited by researchers, two-thirds of parents said their child felt distressed or unhappy while in isolation.

The defence, and the gap it exposes

School leaders argue the practice keeps children who would otherwise be suspended or expelled inside the building and, in principle, still learning. Pepe Di'Iasio, general secretary of the Association of School and College Leaders, has described internal exclusion as "a step short of an external exclusion," designed to keep pupils in school rather than send them home. Tom Bennett, a behaviour management adviser to the DfE, has gone further, calling it "a form of inclusion" that allows disruptive pupils to keep working on site under closer staff supervision than a mainstream classroom allows.

Arbor's co-founder and chief executive, James Weatherill, was more circumspect about what the trend means, saying schools "clearly need effective ways to respond when behaviour disrupts learning," but that the scale of the rise "should prompt us to look more closely at what is driving it." The Children's Commissioner for England, Dame Rachel de Souza, has raised a narrower but pointed concern: that there is no guarantee a pupil sitting in an isolation room is receiving anything resembling a "high-quality education" while they are there.

That uncertainty is the structural problem. There are no national rules governing how long an internal exclusion can last, what a pupil is meant to be doing while isolated, or what oversight — if any — a school must apply to its own use of the practice. Formal suspension data lets a parent, an inspector or a journalist see which schools exclude often and why. Internal exclusion, recorded only in each school's own systems if it is recorded at all, offers no equivalent trail. A school under pressure to keep its suspension numbers low has an obvious incentive to lean on a tool that does not show up in anyone's spreadsheet.

What comes next

Schools Week has reported that ministers are examining whether to require schools to record internal exclusions nationally, which would be the first step toward making the practice visible in the same way suspensions already are. Until that happens, the picture is being built from the outside — through management-software data like Arbor's and academic surveys like the Greater Manchester study — rather than from any official return. Whether the growth in isolation reflects worsening classroom behaviour, tighter scrutiny of formal exclusions since the pandemic, or simply a more attractive way to manage the numbers, is a question the current data cannot answer. A national reporting requirement would begin to.
