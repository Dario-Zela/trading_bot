UK FISCAL SQUEEZE · By M. Halloway · 2026-09-03 · 5 min read

Bond selloff halves UK's Budget headroom as gilt yields hit fresh highs

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, at the centre of the gilt-market repricing squeezing the Treasury's fiscal room. — DAVID ILIFF / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
A global bond selloff has driven UK gilt yields to their highest since 1998, roughly halving Chancellor Healey's fiscal headroom before October's Budget.

Chancellor John Healey has spent the summer promising a Budget without drama. The bond market did not get the memo.

A global sell-off in government debt has driven UK borrowing costs to their highest levels in a generation, stripping away much of the fiscal room Healey was counting on for his first Budget on 28 October. The 10-year gilt yield climbed above 5.25% this week, the highest since 2008, while the 30-year yield touched 5.89% — a level last seen in March 1998, before the Bank of England had independent control of interest rates. Britain recorded the sharpest jump in bond yields anywhere in the G7 on Tuesday, according to traders, as markets zeroed in on a government that has yet to say how it will pay for its plans.

The number that matters: £26bn to £13.8bn

The headline damage is fiscal. At the Office for Budget Responsibility's spring forecast, Rachel Reeves — Healey's predecessor — had roughly £26bn of headroom against the government's self-imposed borrowing rule. Run the same calculation off this week's yields and that cushion shrinks to about £13.8bn, according to Sanjay Raja, Deutsche Bank's chief UK economist. Bloomberg Economics puts the erosion at a similar scale — around £12bn wiped out by the combination of higher debt-servicing costs and firmer inflation. Capital Economics and Pantheon Macroeconomics both independently arrive at the same rough conclusion: Healey's room to manoeuvre has been cut roughly in half before he has announced a single new policy.

That matters because headroom is the buffer between what the government plans to spend and what its own fiscal rule allows. A government with £26bn of space can absorb a bad quarter of growth data, fund a modest giveaway, or cover a spending overrun without breaching its rule and triggering a market reaction. A government with £13.8bn has none of that slack. Raja's assessment is that Healey will want to preserve at least £10bn in reserve simply to reassure investors — leaving barely anything for the new commitments Prime Minister Andy Burnham has been signalling since taking office in July.

A budget preview, delivered by the bond market

The timing has embarrassed the government's political rollout as much as its economics. Burnham faced his first Prime Minister's Questions this week having spent his Commons address blaming Brexit for the state of the public finances — only for gilt yields to spike further in the hours that followed. Kemi Badenoch pressed him repeatedly to rule out tax rises on the middle classes to fund defence spending; Burnham declined, saying he was not about to "write the Budget" at the despatch box. It was a technically correct answer that will do little to calm a bond market already pricing in the possibility that Healey has to raise revenue to hold the fiscal line.

Healey, for his part, has been trying to draw a contrast with his predecessor by talking up fiscal discipline as "the bedrock" of the government's approach and pledging a buffer against economic shocks. Markets have taken the message but are testing its credibility in real time: a chancellor who has publicly committed to caution has far less room to quietly let borrowing rise than one who never made the promise.

Not just a UK problem

What distinguishes this episode from a routine gilt wobble is that it isn't confined to Britain. Germany's 30-year bond yield has pushed to a fresh 15-year high, and France's 30-year touched levels last seen in 2008 — moves that trace back to a shared cause rather than domestic political noise. Renewed fighting following the collapse of the Iran ceasefire has pushed oil above $91 a barrel, reviving inflation fears just as investors were bracing for the US Federal Reserve and the Bank of Japan to diverge on rate policy. European equities have felt the squeeze mechanically: the FTSE 100, DAX and CAC 40 have all traded near one-month lows this week as higher discount rates weigh on valuations across the board.

That context cuts both ways for Healey. It means the gilt move is not simply a verdict on UK fiscal credibility — Britain is being swept up in a global repricing of duration risk rather than singled out. But it also means the Chancellor has no lever to pull that would reverse it. He cannot out-communicate an oil shock or a Bank of Japan tightening cycle. Sterling's muted reaction underscores the point: normally a sharp rise in yields would support a currency by widening the interest-rate advantage on offer, but the pound has drifted lower against the dollar this week, evidence that investors read the move as compensation for risk rather than a reward for holding gilts.

What the Budget now has to solve

The practical consequence is that Healey heads into 28 October needing to close a gap that did not exist five months ago, without the option of borrowing his way past it. UK shop-price inflation has already accelerated to a two-year high, and markets are now pricing a roughly 70% chance of a Bank of England rate rise by November — a combination that narrows his choices to spending restraint, tax increases, or some blend of both. Any of the three carries political cost for a government less than two months into a new premiership.

The next real test comes with the OBR's updated forecast, due alongside the Budget itself, which will either validate the market's math or offer Healey a technical reprieve. Until then, every gilt auction between now and late October effectively previews the Budget's arithmetic — and this week's has left less room than the Chancellor was banking on.
