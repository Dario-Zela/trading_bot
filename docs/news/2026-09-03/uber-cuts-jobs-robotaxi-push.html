Uber cuts 3,300 jobs as it doubles down on robotaxis with London Wayve launch

Uber CEO Dara Khosrowshahi, who told staff the company had become too complex and is flattening management to fund its robotaxi push. — The Presidential Office of Ukraine / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
Uber is cutting 3,300 jobs and flattening management the same week it launched driver-monitored robotaxis with Wayve in London, redirecting savings into its autonomous-vehicle push.

Uber spent Tuesday delivering two messages that only make sense side by side. The first: it is cutting roughly 3,300 jobs, about a tenth of its global workforce, and stripping out layers of management. The second: it just put its first self-driving cars on the streets of London. Taken together, they describe a company betting its future growth on machines that drive themselves — and trimming the human org chart to pay for it.

The cuts

Chief executive Dara Khosrowshahi told staff on September 2 that the company had grown too complicated for its own good. "That growth has also brought complexity: more layers, more coordination, more fragmented ownership, and in some cases structures that made sense when businesses were smaller but no longer serve us well at our current scale," he wrote in a memo to employees. Uber's revenue has nearly tripled over the past five years, he noted — the layoffs are the price of pruning back what that growth built.

The restructuring is aimed squarely at middle management. Uber has cut the number of staff sitting seven or more reporting layers below the CEO by 20%, and eliminated close to half of its "micro-teams" — units with only one or two direct reports, a structure that tends to multiply management overhead without multiplying output. Remote work has also been curtailed, with fewer than 1% of staff now permitted to work outside an office. Delivery operations spanning restaurants, retail and direct sales are being folded into a single unit.

Crucially, Khosrowshahi has said the ride-hailing, delivery and robotaxi divisions are being shielded from the cuts — the money saved by flattening management is being redirected into those three businesses, robotaxi chief among them. It is a familiar move in tech — Amazon, Meta and Google have all run similar "delayering" exercises since 2023 — but the timing, landing the same week as a flagship autonomous-vehicle launch, makes Uber's version of it a statement of intent rather than routine belt-tightening.

The London launch

That statement showed up on London's streets the same day. Uber and British start-up Wayve began offering robotaxi rides across the city, using fewer than 20 Ford Mustang Mach-E electric SUVs fitted with Wayve's camera-based driving software. It is the first robotaxi service open to the public in London, arriving ahead of both Waymo and a Baidu-Lyft partnership, which are also circling the city.

The service is not yet driverless in any meaningful sense — a licensed safety operator sits behind the wheel throughout, ready to brake or steer, much as Tesla's robotaxi pilots in the US still require a human minder. Sarfraz Maredia, who heads Uber's autonomous mobility business, acknowledged that UK approval to remove the safety driver entirely is still pending: "That process is still playing out." Under the UK's current pilot framework, operators need explicit regulatory sign-off before they can take the human out of the car, and neither company would put a date on when that might happen.

Riders cannot request a Wayve car directly. Instead, anyone hailing an UberX, Comfort or Electric trip may simply be matched with one, at no extra cost, with the option to decline — and no tipping prompt appears for the ride. London is only the second European city, after Zagreb, where Uber offers this kind of service, and Zagreb also still requires a safety driver. Wayve's co-founder and chief executive, Alex Kendall, a Cambridge-trained engineer whose company builds self-driving software using an end-to-end AI approach rather than the sensor-heavy stacks favoured by rivals, called London the natural place to start: "We're proud to introduce the Wayve AI Driver to the public for the first time right here in London, our home city." Uber first invested in Wayve in 2024, and the London deployment follows a $1.2 billion funding round in February that also drew in Nvidia and several carmakers.

The bet underneath both moves

Set next to each other, the two announcements read less like coincidence than strategy. Uber's core ride-hailing and delivery businesses are large and mature; growing them further mostly means fighting for share in markets it already leads. Autonomous vehicles are the one lever left that could genuinely reshape its cost base — removing the driver removes Uber's single biggest recurring expense on every trip. Waymo already runs fully driverless cars commercially across 14 US cities, and Uber has spent years watching that business build a lead it could not match by fielding its own fleet. Partnering with Wayve, and with other robotaxi developers in other markets, is Uber's way of getting into that race without the capital burden of building the technology itself — hence the emphasis on freeing up cash and management bandwidth to fund exactly this kind of partnership.

For now, London's Wayve cars still have a human in the driver's seat, and Uber's flatter org chart is barely a day old. The next marker to watch is regulatory: whether UK authorities clear the removal of the safety driver, and how quickly, will determine whether this launch is a genuine commercial pivot or another long pilot. Uber's next earnings call, due in the coming weeks, should also show whether investors are willing to reward the trade-off Khosrowshahi is asking them to accept — fewer people, more machines, and a bet that autonomy arrives before the savings run out.
