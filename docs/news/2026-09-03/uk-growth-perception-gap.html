Nearly half of UK households say they don't feel the benefits of growth

Prime Minister Andy Burnham, whose government faces a widening gap between growth rhetoric and household sentiment ahead of the October Budget. — Alecsandra Dragoi / No 10 Downing Street (Open Government Licence v3.0)
In one sentence
A PwC report finds 46% of UK households see no lift in living standards from economic growth, complicating Burnham's pitch ahead of the October Budget.

Nearly half of British households say the economy's headline growth numbers might as well be describing another country. A new report from PwC, published this week, finds that 12.5 million households — 46% of the total — live in parts of the UK where economic growth is not translating into higher living standards, a gap researchers describe as "stark" enough to constitute a North-South divide in everything but name.

The finding lands at an awkward moment for Prime Minister Andy Burnham, who is trying to build a Budget narrative around growth just as gilt yields climb and fiscal headroom shrinks. If voters do not believe growth reaches their pay packets, the political cover for painful choices — tax rises, spending restraint, wage discipline in the public sector — thins considerably.

What the numbers show

PwC's measure is household spending power — income after tax and housing costs, adjusted for household size — rather than raw GDP. By that yardstick, every region across the north of England, the Midlands and Wales sits below the national average of £23,371 a year. Yorkshire and the Humber fares worst, at £1,917 below average, followed by Wales (£1,754 down) and the North East (£1,542 down). The South East sits £2,154 above average, London £1,883 above, with the South West and East of England also ahead.

The divide is not purely regional, either. Within London itself, PwC found Richmond's average disposable income running at nearly double the £18,384 recorded a few miles away in Hammersmith and Fulham — evidence, the firm argues, that prosperity varies "not only between regions but on each other's doorstep," in the words of Rachel Taylor, PwC's government and health leader.

Why it matters for the Budget

Burnham has staked his early premiership on a pledge to close regional inequalities and lift living standards, framing growth as the route out of austerity-era stagnation. Kemi Badenoch, the Conservative leader, has already seized on the report to attack that framing, arguing Burnham's "theory of growth is completely wrong" and that higher public spending does not automatically make households richer.

The timing compounds an existing fiscal squeeze. Thirty-year gilt yields have pushed above levels last seen in 1998, and Bloomberg Economics estimates the resulting rise in debt-servicing costs, combined with inflation, has already eaten roughly £12bn of the government's £23.6bn Budget headroom ahead of the October 28 statement. A government that cannot credibly claim voters are feeling the benefit of growth has less room to ask for patience — and more incentive to reach for visible, near-term relief rather than the kind of consolidation the bond market may be pricing in.

The next test comes with the Budget itself. Whether Burnham and Chancellor John Healey lean toward redistribution — regional investment, targeted tax credits — or toward the spending restraint that tighter headroom implies will show which side of that tension wins out.
