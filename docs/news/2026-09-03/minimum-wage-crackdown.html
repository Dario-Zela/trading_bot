B&Q and Five Guys named in minimum-wage underpayment crackdown

A B&Q store at a UK retail park. B&Q was the largest single case in the latest minimum-wage naming round, underpaying 4,530 workers a combined £457,000. — Eric Jones / Geograph, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Government named 658 employers, including B&Q and Five Guys, for underpaying minimum wage — £4m in back pay, £7m in fines.

The Department for Business and Trade has named 658 employers found to have paid staff less than the National Minimum Wage, ordering £4m in back pay to more than 27,000 workers and issuing £7m in penalties. Among the businesses on the list: B&Q, Five Guys, Tesco, Whitbread and Serco.

B&Q was the largest single case: it underpaid £456,934.72 across 4,530 workers, which it attributed to miscalculated geographical allowances. The retailer says the shortfall was unintentional and that affected staff were repaid in full in July 2025. Five Guys JV Limited, which operates the burger chain's UK arm out of Kensington and Chelsea, underpaid £54,642.47 to 3,699 workers, blaming what it called technical differences in how payroll regulations were applied to its workforce.

The rest of the list ranges from the trivial to the systemic. Tesco owed a single worker £227.84, which it called an isolated administrative error. Whitbread underpaid 342 staff a combined £4,200. Two McDonald's franchises owed £29,000 between them to 797 workers. Serco pointed to a technical fault on one contract more than two years ago, since corrected. G4S also appears on the register.

A tougher enforcement posture

The naming round is the first since the Fair Work Agency began consolidated operations in April, merging HMRC's minimum wage unit, the Employment Agency Standards Inspectorate and the Gangmasters and Labour Abuse Authority into a single regulator. HMRC continues to run day-to-day minimum wage enforcement during a transition expected to run until April 2027, but ministers have used the merger to push more frequent, higher-volume naming rounds — a March round named 389 employers and recovered £7.3m for around 60,000 workers, with £12.6m in penalties.

Business Secretary Jonathan Reynolds framed the exercise as deterrence: "Short-changing your staff isn't a shortcut to success, and we are determined to stamp it out." Employment minister Kate Dearden added that workers would not "foot the bill for their boss failing to follow the rules," while Fair Work Agency chair Matthew Taylor said paying the minimum wage "is not optional — it is the law."

For employers, the pattern across recent naming rounds is instructive: most breaches stem from payroll mechanics rather than deliberate wage theft — deductions for uniforms, unpaid working time, misapplied allowances, apprentice-rate errors — but the penalty regime does not distinguish intent from error. Fines run up to 200% of arrears. With naming rounds now arriving roughly twice a year rather than annually, and full enforcement powers due to land with the Fair Work Agency next April, high-street employers with complex shift and allowance structures face a shorter runway to find payroll errors before the government finds them first.

The next test comes when HMRC's enforcement function fully transfers to the Fair Work Agency in April 2027, a point ministers have signalled will bring wider powers and, potentially, more frequent disclosure.
