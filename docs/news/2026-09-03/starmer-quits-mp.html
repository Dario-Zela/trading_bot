Starmer to quit as MP, triggering Holborn and St Pancras by-election

Keir Starmer, who is stepping down as MP for Holborn and St Pancras after 11 years in the seat. — Simon Dawson / No 10 Downing Street (Open Government Licence v3.0)
In one sentence
Keir Starmer is quitting as an MP after 11 years, just six weeks after Andy Burnham replaced him as Labour leader and PM, forcing a seventh by-election of this Parliament.

Keir Starmer will step down as the MP for Holborn and St Pancras, ending an eleven-year run in the House of Commons that took him from a backbench lawyer-turned-politician to Downing Street and back to the backbenches, and now, in a single announcement, out of Parliament altogether.

The resignation triggers the seventh by-election of this Parliament, and the first in London since Boris Johnson quit Uxbridge and South Ruislip in 2023. It also lands just six weeks after Starmer handed the keys to Number 10 to Andy Burnham, the former Greater Manchester mayor who now leads a Labour Party that has, in the space of ten months, gone from a landslide election winner to a party running through leaders and legislators at a pace that unsettles its own MPs.

"Now is the right time to step down as MP for Holborn and St Pancras and hand the baton over to a successor," Starmer said, adding that he intends to focus on international affairs — defence, security, trade and technology — and continue campaigning against violence toward women and girls.

A resignation that closes a longer story

Starmer's departure completes an exit that began in June, when he agreed to step down as Labour leader after more than 95 of his own MPs had called for him to go or to set a timetable for leaving. The pressure came from both flanks at once — critics on the right attacking his record on immigration and tax rises, critics on the left attacking his handling of Gaza, welfare reform and the absence of a wealth tax. A wipeout in May's local elections, in which Labour lost more than 1,000 council seats, hardened the mood. Polling at the time found most of the public, and even a majority of 2024 Labour voters, thought he was right to go.

Burnham's route into Downing Street was its own preview of the disorder now surrounding his predecessor's old seat. Barred by Labour's National Executive Committee in January from contesting a vacant Commons seat — a rule meant to stop the party fighting two campaigns at once — Burnham only returned to Parliament in June, after a sitting Makerfield MP resigned to clear a path for him. He was confirmed as leader on 17 July and became prime minister three days later, the UK's seventh in a decade.

Why Holborn and St Pancras won't be a formality

Labour's majority in the seat, Starmer's since 2015, stands at 11,572 — comfortable on paper, but Camden's political geography has shifted. The Green Party took 10.4% of the vote there in 2024 and made further gains in May's local elections, and party leader Zack Polanski is being talked up as a possible candidate. LabourList editor Emma Burnell credited Starmer with steering the party from opposition to a 2024 landslide but cautioned that "this will not be an easy by-election" given the local drift toward the Greens and Liberal Democrats.

What it signals

Taken alone, an ex-prime minister quitting the Commons is a familiar rhythm of British politics — Johnson and Liz Truss both eventually did the same. What makes this one worth pausing on is the compression: a leader forced out in June, a scramble to find his successor a seat, a new premier by July, and by September the vacancy left by the old one still needs filling. For a party trying to convince voters and markets it has settled into a stable governing rhythm, the calendar tells a different story. The by-election date has yet to be set; watch for the writ to be moved when Parliament returns in the coming weeks, and for whether Reform UK or the Greens make it Labour's next test of how far its London base has eroded.
