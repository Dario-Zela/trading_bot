Wall Street slides as Treasury yields hit multi-year highs and AI stocks wobble

The trading floor of the New York Stock Exchange, where AI and mega-cap tech names led losses as Treasury yields climbed to multi-year highs. — NIST / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
The 10-year Treasury yield's climb to 4.818%, a near three-year high, is forcing a repricing of debt-funded AI stocks like Oracle even as the 30-year's longest stretch above 5% since 2006 signals the shift is structural.

The number that rattled trading desks this week was 4.818% — the yield on the 10-year Treasury note, briefly touched in intraday trading, a level not seen since November 2023. It sounds like a rounding error. It is not. That yield sits underneath every discounted-cash-flow model on Wall Street, and when it rises, the present value of profits promised five or ten years from now falls — hardest of all for the artificial-intelligence stocks that have done most of the work hauling the S&P 500 to records this year.

The result was two straight losing sessions for the S&P 500 and Nasdaq Composite spanning the final day of August and the first of September, snapped only on September 2 when both indexes edged back into the green — the S&P 500 up roughly 0.5%, the Nasdaq up about 0.2% to 0.45%. The bounce did not undo the diagnosis. Yields are still near a three-year high, the 30-year bond has spent more of this year above 5% than in any year since 2006, and the market's most expensive stocks are the ones now doing the explaining.

Why a bond number moves a chipmaker's stock

Thierry Wizman, global FX and rates strategist at Macquarie, put the mechanism plainly to CNBC this week: higher yields "force analysts to discount higher earnings more aggressively, thus forcing [price-to-earnings] multiples downward." That is the textbook version. The real-world version has a name: Oracle.

Oracle shares fell 4% to $142.82 on September 1, extending a slide that has left the stock down 23% year-to-date and trading well below its 200-day moving average of roughly $170.66. The company financed $55.7 billion in fiscal 2026 capital spending — up from $21.2 billion the year before — largely by raising $43 billion in new debt, on top of $5 billion in stock sales. Long-term debt now stands at $122.3 billion against shareholder equity of just $42.5 billion. Every basis point the 10-year adds makes that balance sheet more expensive to carry, and the market has started pricing it accordingly: the cloud infrastructure ETF SKYY fell roughly 2% against a 0.9% decline in the tech-heavy QQQ over the same stretch, a sign that debt-funded AI builders are absorbing the rate shock harder than mega-cap tech as a whole.

Oracle is not alone. A chip-stock selloff in late July wiped out more than $1 trillion in combined market value across the sector; Nvidia lost as much as $153 billion of capitalisation in a single session during the recent volatility, and names like Micron and Sandisk have each shed more than 5% in a day at various points since. None of these are companies in financial distress. They are companies whose valuations were built on the assumption that money would stay cheap long enough to justify paying today for earnings that arrive years from now. That assumption is the one now being repriced.

The long end has already made up its mind

If the 10-year yield explains the week, the 30-year explains the year. The long bond closed above 5% on 55 trading days between January and the start of September 2026 — the most such closes in any calendar year since 2006, two decades ago. It touched 5.34% in mid-August, the highest since 2007, before Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent's announcement of expanded bond buybacks — an attempt to contain the move — which analysts have since described as "a drop in the bucket" against the structural forces pushing yields higher.

Those forces are not mysterious. Corporate borrowers are expected to issue roughly $215 billion in debt in September alone, adding to already-record August volumes and competing with the government for buyers. The federal deficit continues to widen, with analysts noting long-end yields are unlikely to retreat meaningfully "until entitlement reform changes the deficit picture." And the Federal Reserve's own posture has hardened: traders were pricing roughly 70% odds of a rate move at the mid-September meeting, and the two-year yield — the maturity most sensitive to Fed policy — reached 4.41% this week, its highest since January 2025. A separate flare-up in oil prices, after renewed US-Iran strikes pushed West Texas Intermediate above $90 a barrel, has added an inflation worry on top of the rate worry, giving bond bears two reasons to sell rather than one.

What the reset means

Taken together, the short-end story and the long-end story describe the same shift from two directions. The 10-year's lurch to 4.818% is the market's immediate reaction to fresh rate-hike risk and fiscal supply; the 30-year's 55-day streak above 5% is the market's settled view that this is not a blip to be waited out. For algorithmic and systematic strategies built on years of falling or flat rates, that settled view matters more than any single day's headline: correlation models tuned to a low-rate regime, and hedges priced off a cheaper cost of duration, both need recalibrating if 5% is the new normal rather than a spike.

The next test lands quickly. The Federal Reserve meets in mid-September with markets pricing better-than-even odds of a hawkish surprise, and September's corporate debt calendar — that $215 billion in expected issuance — will show whether buyers still show up at these yields without demanding more. Until the deficit conversation in Washington actually changes, the bond market's message to equity investors is likely to stay the same: growth has to be paid for today.
