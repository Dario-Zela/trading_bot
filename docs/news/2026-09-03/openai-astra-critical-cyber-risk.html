OpenAI's unreleased Astra model becomes first to cross 'Critical' cyber-risk threshold

OpenAI CEO Sam Altman, whose company says its unreleased Astra model is the first to cross its 'Critical' cybersecurity risk threshold. — Steve Jurvetson / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
OpenAI says its unreleased Astra model autonomously found and chained two zero-day exploits, the first to cross its 'Critical' cyber-risk threshold.

OpenAI says its next model has done something no AI system has done before, according to the company's own internal yardstick: it found and used two previously unknown software flaws — zero-days, in security jargon — entirely on its own, well enough to clear the highest risk tier the company tracks.

The model, code-named Astra and not yet released to the public, crossed what OpenAI calls the "Critical" threshold on the cybersecurity category of its Preparedness Framework, the internal rubric the company uses to decide whether a model is too dangerous to ship without extra safeguards. It is the first OpenAI model to do so. In one evaluation built around recently disclosed vulnerabilities, Astra didn't just replicate known exploits — it surfaced two zero-days by itself and chained them together, along with a browser sandbox escape, to reach root-level access on a target system. Separately, it posted a perfect score on ExploitBench, an internal benchmark that measures how reliably a model can turn a known vulnerability into a working exploit.

What 'Critical' actually means

OpenAI's framework, first published in 2023 and last revised in April 2025, sorts model capability into tiers. "High" covers a model that can meaningfully speed up existing attack methods — useful to a hacker, but not a new kind of threat. "Critical" is different in kind, not degree: it means a model can independently discover and weaponise unknown flaws across multiple hardened, real-world systems without a human walking it through the steps, or can take a vague, high-level goal and design and execute a full attack chain on its own. OpenAI describes this as a threat vector with no real precedent — the sort of capability the framework was written to catch before it reached the open market rather than after.

That is the case OpenAI is making about Astra. The company says the model declines 91.5% of cyber-related jailbreak attempts in testing, up from 59% for its predecessor, and that it has disclosed the two zero-days it found to the relevant software maintainers rather than publishing them. Sam Altman, OpenAI's chief executive, framed the delay in a post on X: "We do not think it is a good strategy to keep powerful models to a chosen few. Given its cyber capabilities, we need a little longer to do this safely. But hopefully not too long!" Rather than a general release, Astra's fuller offensive capabilities will go first to a small test group and then to vetted defenders inside a program OpenAI calls Daybreak Blue, which counts Accenture, IBM, CrowdStrike, Cisco, Sophos and Cloudflare among its members.

It's worth being clear about what's being claimed and by whom. This is OpenAI's own framework, scored on OpenAI's own evaluations, with no independent replication published alongside the announcement — and the company has an obvious commercial interest in demonstrating that its newest model is powerful. The zero-days themselves have not been detailed publicly, so their real-world severity is hard to judge from outside. Still, the Critical designation triggers OpenAI's own internal review and deployment restrictions, which is a meaningful thing for the company to impose on itself even before outside scrutiny catches up.

Why trading desks should care

The timing puts this squarely in front of financial regulators. Just two days before OpenAI's announcement, Andrew Bailey — Bank of England governor and chair of the Financial Stability Board — wrote to G20 finance ministers ahead of their end-of-August meeting in Asheville, North Carolina, warning that AI-driven cyber risk was now the most immediate threat to the global financial system. "Frontier AI may have the ability materially to alter the speed, scale and economics of cyber risk, which could undermine market confidence system-wide, especially due to highly concentrated third-party service providers," Bailey wrote. He urged firms to prepare for scenarios in which multiple institutions are hit simultaneously through shared technology dependencies, and to be ready to rebuild critical systems from bare metal if needed.

The IMF has been making a related point for months: AI lowers the cost and skill threshold for finding and exploiting vulnerabilities, which raises the odds that the same weakness gets discovered and hit across many institutions at once rather than one at a time. That matters more for markets than for most other sectors, because trading infrastructure is unusually interdependent — execution engines, risk systems, clearing links and market-data feeds from a small number of vendors sit underneath a large share of daily volume. A model that can autonomously chain vulnerabilities the way Astra reportedly did in testing doesn't need to individually target a hedge fund's order-management system; it needs to find one flaw in a widely used piece of infrastructure underneath many of them.

The defensive gap

Most existing market-infrastructure security still assumes a human attacker: rate-limited reconnaissance, phishing campaigns that take days to run, exploit development that takes a skilled team weeks. Autonomous discovery compresses that timeline and removes the manual steps that give defenders a chance to notice something is wrong before damage is done. OpenAI's own answer — restrict full access to vetted defenders through Daybreak Blue rather than a general release — is effectively a bet that the same capability is more useful for hardening systems than for breaking them, provided access is controlled. Whether that holds depends on adoption elsewhere: rival labs are racing on similar capabilities, and Astra's approach to zero-day discovery, once demonstrated as possible, does not stay unique for long.

None of this is abstract for firms running algorithmic execution or automated risk management. It argues for treating AI-capable adversaries as a distinct line item in threat models, rather than a faster version of the same attacker security teams have always planned for.

Astra's staged rollout through Daybreak Blue is the next thing to watch — how quickly OpenAI widens access, and whether any of the zero-days it found turn out to sit inside infrastructure that trading firms actually rely on once patches are public.
