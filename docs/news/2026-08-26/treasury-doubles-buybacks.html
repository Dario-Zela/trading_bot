US Treasury doubles long-bond buybacks as fiscal deficit concerns mount

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, who said there is a "very good chance" the federal deficit has peaked even as long-bond yields hit multi-decade highs. — U.S. Department of the Treasury / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Treasury doubled long-bond buybacks to at least $4bn per operation after 30-year yields hit a 19-year high, easing yields without addressing the underlying deficit.

The US Treasury will more than double the size of its bond buyback operations at the long end of the yield curve, a move that took effect in market pricing this week even before the formal start date of September 9. The maximum size of liquidity-support buybacks in the 10-to-20-year and 20-to-30-year sectors rises from $2 billion to at least $4 billion per operation, and stays in place through November 4.

The timing was not subtle. The 30-year Treasury yield had just touched 5.323% on August 18 — a 19-year high — before Treasury's announcement pulled it back to 5.196%. The 10-year eased six basis points to 4.647%. Treasury said the change reflects "the significant volume of high-quality offers" it routinely receives in longer-dated buyback operations, bureaucratic language for a market that needed a buyer of last resort.

"It is not an accident, in my view, so the more important part is the signaling from it," said John Briggs of Natixis North America. "If yields go too far, Treasury will try and fight it — and now we know where some pain points are."

A liquidity tool, not a deficit fix

Buybacks work by having Treasury repurchase older, less-liquid bonds and refinance the position through fresh issuance, mostly short-dated bills. That smooths dysfunction in a market where dealers had been struggling to move large blocks of long-dated paper without moving prices against themselves. It does not shrink the pile of debt outstanding, and it does not touch the deficit that keeps generating fresh supply.

That deficit is the real story sitting behind the yield spike. The Congressional Budget Office has raised its fiscal 2026 estimate to roughly $1.9 trillion, and July's monthly shortfall came in above $432 billion, the highest in years. Federal debt outstanding has passed $40 trillion. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent told CNBC on August 20 there is a "very good chance" the deficit has peaked, pointing to fiscal-consolidation work with President Trump and OMB director Russell Vought that he said could save "several hundreds of billions of dollars." He has not yet said which spending or revenue lines would deliver those savings.

Strategists were more circumspect about what the buyback expansion actually achieves. It counters a specific symptom — thin liquidity and outsized term premium at the long end — without altering the supply-demand arithmetic that produced it: heavier Treasury issuance, persistent above-target inflation, and a wave of corporate debt from the AI capital-spending boom competing for the same pool of buyers.

What it doesn't fix

The mechanics matter here. Because Treasury funds the buybacks by issuing more bills rather than tapping any reserve, it is effectively shortening the average maturity of federal debt — trading duration risk for rollover risk. That eases pressure on the long end for now but leaves the government more exposed if short-term rates move against it later, and does nothing to close the gap between spending and revenue that keeps pushing issuance higher.

Markets took the announcement as a modest positive, with equities catching a bid alongside the yield relief. But the message from the bond market itself was consistent with the diagnosis: buybacks bought time, not resolution. The next test comes with the Treasury's quarterly refunding announcement, expected in early November, when the size and composition of new issuance — and any further escalation of the buyback programme — will show whether this was a one-off liquidity patch or the start of a recurring intervention every time long yields threaten to break higher.
