UK 30-year gilt yields hold near 1998 highs ahead of the autumn Budget

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, whose gilt-sale programme has added to pressure on long-dated yields. — George Rex / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
UK 30-year gilt yields are stuck near 5.72%, their highest since 1998, squeezing Chancellor Healey's room ahead of the 28 October Budget.

UK borrowing costs at the long end of the curve are stuck near territory not seen since John Major was in Downing Street. The yield on 30-year gilts stood at 5.72% on Wednesday, having touched 5.79% in May — the highest level since 1998 — and it has spent the months since grinding sideways in a tight band just below that peak rather than retreating.

That matters because the 30-year gilt is the benchmark long-dated pension funds and insurers use to price liabilities decades into the future, and because the government has to keep selling debt into a market that is charging it more for the privilege with every auction. Ten-year yields, the more widely watched gauge of the broader borrowing picture, are holding around 5% — themselves elevated by the standards of the past two decades, even with the Bank of England's base rate down at 3.75% after a gradual easing cycle.

Why the long end won't come down

Three forces are keeping 30-year yields pinned near the top of their range. Persistent inflation concerns are one — investors want a bigger premium for locking money away for thirty years when price growth looks unanchored. A run of unsettling fiscal news out of Westminster is another: the Debt Management Office is planning to raise roughly £275bn in gross financing for 2026-27, a sum that keeps supply heavy just as demand from traditional buyers — pension funds moving toward buyout, life insurers matching liabilities — has become less reliable than it once was.

The third factor is the Bank of England itself. Its quantitative tightening programme, under which it actively sells gilts bought during years of quantitative easing rather than simply letting them mature, has continued even as the pace has eased — the annual reduction target fell from £100bn to £70bn between September 2025 and September 2026. Critics, including some rate-setters, argue the Bank's decision to sell rather than run off its book has itself added supply at the worst possible moment, pushing 30-year yields higher than peers in the US, Germany or Japan have experienced over the same stretch.

The Budget backdrop

Chancellor John Healey — installed by Prime Minister Andy Burnham in July after he replaced Rachel Reeves — will present his first Budget on Wednesday 28 October against this backdrop. Investors have already warned him publicly that further loosening of fiscal discipline risks pushing yields higher still; analysts have urged that any additional borrowing for investment stay under £10bn next year, paired with visible restraint on day-to-day spending. Some forecasters see room for optimism: falling yields in the run-up to the Budget could restore £10-12bn of the fiscal headroom that rising borrowing costs stripped away earlier this year. That headroom is thin enough that a single bad inflation print or a wobbly gilt auction could erase it.

For pension schemes running long-dated liability-driven investment strategies, the elevated-but-stable yield picture is a mixed blessing — higher yields improve funding ratios for schemes hedged against them, but the volatility around 2022's LDI crisis has left trustees and the Bank alike watchful for any repeat of forced selling into a falling market.

The next real test comes with the Budget itself on 28 October, and before that, September and October gilt auctions will show whether demand is holding up at these yields or whether the DMO needs to sweeten terms to get the books covered. Markets will also be watching whether the Bank trims its QT pace further when it reviews the programme in September.
