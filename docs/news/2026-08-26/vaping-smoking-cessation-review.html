Nicotine vapes remain the best route to quitting smoking, major review finds

A regulated nicotine e-cigarette in use — the category Cochrane's review found most effective for quitting smoking. — Ecig Click / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
A Cochrane review of 80 trials finds regulated nicotine vapes beat patches and gum for quitting smoking, days before a new UK vape tax lands.

The Cochrane Collaboration, whose evidence reviews sit near the top of medicine's pecking order, has updated its living review of e-cigarettes as a smoking-cessation aid — and the conclusion, published on 25 August, is the strongest yet in vaping's favour. Regulated, nicotine-containing e-cigarettes now have what Cochrane rates as high-certainty evidence behind them: they help more smokers quit than nicotine patches or gum, the standard treatment offered on the NHS for the past three decades.[^1]

The scale of the effect is modest but real. Pooling 80 randomised controlled trials and 29,861 participants — nine of them added since the previous update — the reviewers, led by Dr Nicola Lindson at Oxford and Dr Jamie Hartmann-Boyce, now at the University of Massachusetts Amherst, found roughly four more successful quitters per 100 smokers using nicotine vapes compared with nicotine-replacement therapy, and about two more per 100 compared with non-nicotine e-cigarettes.[^2] "Quitting smoking is hard," Lindson said. "It's important that there are different options available." Hartmann-Boyce went further, calling regulated nicotine vapes "a proven and viable option" that is "substantially less harmful than smoking."[^2] Against behavioural support alone, or no help at all, the evidence is present but less certain — the trial designs there are thinner.

A policy collision

The timing is awkward for Westminster. The Tobacco and Vapes Act received Royal Assent on 29 April, phasing in a rolling sales ban for anyone born on or after 1 January 2009 and tightening vape marketing generally.[^3] Ministers have simultaneously leaned on vaping as a quitting tool — NHS England's "Swap to Stop" scheme has handed out free starter kits to smokers since 2023 — while the Treasury prepares to make vaping markedly more expensive. A new Vaping Products Duty takes effect on 1 October, adding £2.20 per 10ml of e-liquid (£2.64 with VAT), and free distribution and vending-machine sales are banned from 29 October, ahead of a full advertising ban in June 2027.[^4] A review concluding that vapes are the single most effective route off cigarettes lands just as the products are becoming pricier and harder to give away — a contradiction public-health bodies and the vaping industry are both likely to press the Department of Health and Social Care to address.

Cochrane's caveats matter as much as its headline. The findings apply only to regulated, nicotine-containing devices bought through legitimate retailers — not the unlicensed disposables that flooded the UK market before June 2025's ban, which carry their own risks from unstable batteries and unverified e-liquid contents. Serious harms have not shown up in the trial data, but Cochrane notes that follow-up rarely extends beyond two years, so nothing here speaks to decades-long exposure. Cancer Research UK, which funds the ongoing review, continues to caution against vaping by anyone who has never smoked.[^2]

The next data point is less scientific than fiscal: whether the October duty measurably dents uptake of vaping as a cessation tool, a question the DHSC has yet to say it plans to monitor.
