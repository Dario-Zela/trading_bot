Canada fires back with 50% 'dollar-for-dollar' tariffs on $20bn of US goods

The Dofasco and Stelco steel mills in Hamilton, Ontario — ground zero for the doubled 50% steel tariff. — Ken Lund / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Canada will impose 50% tariffs on $20bn of US goods from September 8, doubling steel and aluminium duties after trade talks with Washington collapsed.

Ottawa's response to Washington arrives with a number attached and a date circled. Prime Minister Mark Carney confirmed on Monday that Canada will impose tariffs of up to 50% on roughly $20 billion of American goods starting September 8, doubling existing counter-duties on US steel and aluminum and extending fresh levies across more than 700 product lines — from dairy powder and household appliances to agricultural equipment and pulp and paper.

The package is Ottawa's answer to a weekend that saw formal trade talks between the two governments collapse and Washington's own 50% tariffs on roughly $20 billion of Canadian goods take effect at midnight Friday. Carney has cast the arithmetic as deliberate. "Canada will match those tariffs dollar for dollar," he said, framing the response not as escalation for its own sake but as parity — rate for rate, dollar for dollar, sector for sector.

From talks to tariffs

The breakdown came fast. Negotiations between Ottawa and Washington, which had been grinding toward some kind of framework through the summer, fell apart Friday night over what Carney described as last-minute, "uneconomic" demands from the US side. Within hours, the Trump administration's 50% tariffs on Canadian goods — covering everything from automobiles and dairy to hockey sticks and toiletries — took hold. Carney did not mince the framing at a subsequent press conference: "You're at war when you get attacked. We got attacked."

Trade Minister Dominic LeBlanc, who had led Canada's side of the negotiations, struck a similarly unsentimental tone, saying Ottawa was "not waiting by the phone." The retaliatory package his ministry unveiled Monday is built to hit the same dollar value the US tariffs extract from Canadian exporters — a structure Ottawa has used before, in the 2018 steel-and-aluminum dispute, but never at this scale or breadth.

What's in the package

The headline number is the doubling of Canada's steel and aluminum counter-tariffs from 25% to 50%, matching the US rate exactly. Beneath that sits a tiered structure: dairy products, appliances, cheese and fish face 25% duties, while a broader tail of goods — rubber molds, machinery parts, and other industrial inputs — draws 15%. Aluminum items alone span 29 separate categories, from raw ingots to finished door and window frames. Machinery and appliance lines add another 50-plus product codes, including air-conditioning units and household refrigerators.

All of it takes effect at 12:01 a.m. on September 8 — giving markets and manufacturers a two-week runway to reprice exposure before the duties are actually collected. Carney has been candid that the measures cut both ways. "Some of these measures will raise costs and reduce choice for Canadians," he said, adding that the government takes the step reluctantly.

The market read

The immediate financial reaction has been more measured than the political rhetoric. The Canadian dollar slipped to roughly US$0.7227 from US$0.7267 the prior session as traders priced in a longer, costlier standoff, but the S&P/TSX Composite still closed higher — up nearly 94 points to 36,714 — as gold's rally to record levels offset weakness in autos and industrials. Materials stocks broadly benefited from the same haven bid pushing gold toward US$4,732 an ounce, even as steel and aluminum producers on both sides of the border face a genuinely awkward two weeks: US buyers of Canadian metal already pay the 50% Trump tariff, and Canadian buyers of US metal will shortly pay the same rate in reverse.

That symmetry is the trade to watch into September 8. Cross-border supply chains in autos, appliances and packaged food don't unwind in two weeks, which means the tariffs land as a straight cost increase for importers on both sides rather than a redirection of trade flows — at least in the near term. Input costs for manufacturers reliant on Canadian aluminum, and for Canadian producers reliant on American machinery parts, move first; consumer prices follow with a lag.

What's next

September 8 is now the fixed point on the calendar: the date Canada's countermeasures take legal effect, and the date against which everything between now and then — a possible last-minute deal, a further escalation from Washington, an exemption request from an affected industry — will be measured. Neither side has signaled an imminent return to the table. Until one does, steel, aluminum and the currency pair between them remain the cleanest read on how the dispute is pricing.
