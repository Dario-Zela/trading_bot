US Navy clears Strait of Hormuz of mines as Trump warns Iran off new deployments

USS Essex and USNS Wally Schirra transit the Strait of Hormuz in formation, the chokepoint through which roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil passes. — Sgt. Alexis Flores / U.S. Marine Corps, via Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
Trump says the US Navy has cleared all mines from the Strait of Hormuz, unwinding a war premium that briefly pushed Brent crude past $100 this year.

The Strait of Hormuz has been the fault line running through the global oil market for most of this year. On Tuesday, President Trump said the fault has been sealed shut — for now.

"I have just been informed by the United States Navy that all mines have been removed and/or detonated from within the International Waters of the Strait of Hormuz," Trump wrote on Truth Social. He paired the announcement with a warning: any vessel caught laying new mines would be "immediately and systematically destroyed," and the U.S. Space Force would now watch "every square inch" of the strait under what he called a "Zero Tolerance policy."

It is the clearest sign yet that Washington considers the acute phase of a conflict that began in February — when U.S. and Israeli strikes on Iran triggered a retaliation campaign across the Gulf — to be over, even as the diplomatic and economic fight with Tehran enters a new phase.

How the strait got mined

Hormuz carries about a fifth of the world's seaborne oil and a comparable share of its liquefied natural gas, funneled through a channel barely two miles wide at its narrowest shipping lanes. When Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps began laying mines there in March, in response to the U.S.-Israeli campaign, the effect on markets was immediate: Brent crude, which had been trading in the $70s, jumped to around $100 a barrel within days, and briefly threatened $150 as fighting intensified through the spring.

U.S. Central Command opened a mine-clearance operation in April, deploying destroyers, mine countermeasures ships and unmanned underwater drones to sweep the channel and re-establish safe transit corridors. It has taken four months. OPEC+ tried to blunt the price shock in the interim by raising output quotas — a modest 206,000 barrels a day from May — but the real ceiling on prices was always going to be set by whether tankers could move through Hormuz without hitting a mine, not by how much crude Gulf producers were willing to pump.

The premium comes out

The market's answer arrived in real time this week. The Murban crude premium to Brent — a proxy for how much extra insurance traders demand for oil that has to physically pass through Hormuz — stood above $11 a barrel on Monday and had roughly halved by Tuesday afternoon as word of the clearance spread. Brent itself settled near $87 on Tuesday, down for a second straight session, as what traders had been calling the "war premium" continued to unwind.

That is still well above the pre-crisis baseline in the $70s, and a long way from the triple-digit spikes of March and April. But the direction is the point: a persistent, months-long tail risk that had been priced into every barrel of Gulf crude is being repriced downward, barrel by barrel, as the physical threat recedes. For a global economy still nursing above-target inflation, one fewer supply shock vector running through energy markets is a genuine, if modest, disinflationary data point.

The threat hasn't fully gone quiet

Trump's announcement did not mean the Gulf has gone calm. Hours before the mine-clearance statement, an oil tanker was struck by an unidentified projectile roughly nine nautical miles off Oman's coast, disabling its engine room; the UK Maritime Trade Operations agency, which tracks such incidents, said it could not identify who fired it. It was the latest in a string of projectile strikes on commercial vessels near the strait that have continued even as the mine threat has been cleared, underscoring that demining removes one hazard to shipping, not all of them.

The timing also coincided with a sharp escalation on the sanctions front. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent unveiled what the administration is calling "Operation Economic Outcast," targeting nearly 60 individuals, entities and vessels and opening five sectors of Iran's economy — digital assets, technology, gold, aviation and shipping — to broader secondary sanctions. "We are launching an economic onslaught against Iran's financial connections around the globe," Bessent said, adding that any entity found laundering money on Iran's behalf would be cut off from the U.S. dollar system.

China, Iran's largest trading partner and the top buyer of its oil, condemned the move within hours. Foreign ministry spokesperson Lin Jian said Beijing opposed "illegal, unilateral" sanctions and warned the measures would "only further intensify tensions" — a reminder that Washington's demining announcement and its sanctions expansion are two halves of the same strategy: physically secure the strait while squeezing Tehran's revenue everywhere else.

What the clearance actually changes

For energy markets, the practical effect is straightforward: insurers, shippers and refiners no longer have to price in a live minefield across the world's most important chokepoint. That lowers the floor under freight and war-risk premiums that had been added to every Gulf cargo since March, and it removes one of the clearer arguments for crude staying persistently elevated regardless of what OPEC+ does with quotas.

What it does not do is resolve the underlying standoff. Iran has not accepted the sanctions escalation quietly, and the projectile strike off Oman shows the campaign against shipping has other tools besides mines. Trump's "zero tolerance" language is as much a deterrent bet as a victory lap — it commits the U.S. to treating any future mining as an act warranting immediate retaliation, which raises the stakes of the next incident even as it lowers the odds, in the administration's telling, that one occurs.

The next test is less likely to be military than economic: whether China, still buying the bulk of Iran's oil exports, alters that behavior under the threat of secondary sanctions, and whether Brent's slide toward its pre-crisis range holds if it doesn't. Markets will be watching the Murban-Brent spread for the rest of the week as the more reliable gauge of whether Hormuz is actually calm, or just quiet.
