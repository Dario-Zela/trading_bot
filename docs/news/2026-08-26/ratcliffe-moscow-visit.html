CIA director Ratcliffe makes unannounced Moscow visit amid stalled Ukraine talks

The Moscow Kremlin, where CIA Director John Ratcliffe held unannounced meetings with Russian officials this week. — Pavel Kazachkov / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
CIA director Ratcliffe's unannounced Moscow trip, timed to a US-brokered pause in Ukrainian strikes, leaves unclear whether it signals diplomacy or a warning in the Burns 2021 mould.

The C-17 that touched down at Moscow's Vnukovo airport on Tuesday carried no flag, no press pool and, for several hours, no confirmed passenger list. By evening, US officials had confirmed what flight trackers and a parked diplomatic motorcade had already suggested: CIA Director John Ratcliffe was in the Russian capital, his first known visit to the country since taking over America's premier intelligence agency last year.

No photographs. No statement from Langley. No acknowledgment from the Kremlin, whose spokesman Dmitry Peskov told reporters he had "no such information" about a US military plane landing in Moscow — a denial issued, awkwardly, on the same day multiple US outlets confirmed the trip through independent sourcing. The mismatch between what Moscow was saying publicly and what was plainly happening on the tarmac has become something of a signature of this stage in the Ukraine war: both sides talk past each other in public while, evidently, still talking to each other in private.

A pause on the battlefield to make room for a visit

What is not in dispute is the choreography around the trip. US officials asked Ukraine last week to hold off on long-range drone and missile strikes against Moscow, St. Petersburg and Russia's northern regions between Monday and Wednesday, telling Kyiv only that a senior American official would be travelling to Russia during that window. Ukraine agreed. Kyiv's compliance — and a cryptic Telegram post from a channel linked to Volodymyr Zelensky's office noting that "the Ukrainian side is aware of certain aspects" discussed in Moscow — suggests this was not a rogue intelligence errand but a coordinated, if carefully unpublicised, diplomatic move with Kyiv's foreknowledge.

That matters for how the visit should be read. A CIA director travelling to meet foreign counterparts is not itself unusual; intelligence chiefs maintain back channels that survive when formal diplomacy breaks down. What is unusual is the scale of disruption arranged around it — a three-day pause in one of Ukraine's most politically significant strike campaigns — which points to something more deliberate than a routine liaison meeting.

An echo of November 2021

The closest precedent is not reassuring. The last CIA director known to have visited Moscow was Ratcliffe's predecessor, William Burns, who flew to the Kremlin in November 2021 on Joe Biden's instructions to warn Russian officials, including security council secretary Nikolai Patrushev, over troop buildups near Ukraine's border. Three months later, Russia invaded. That visit was a warning delivered through the intelligence channel because the diplomatic one had already grown too brittle for the message to land any other way.

Ratcliffe's trip lands in a similarly strained diplomatic environment, though pointed in the opposite direction. Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner, the administration's civilian envoys, have shuttled between Moscow and Washington for months without reaching Kyiv, and talks aimed at ending the war have stalled since the US and Israel's confrontation with Iran pulled attention elsewhere this summer. Zelensky said on 12 August that Ukraine had sent Washington a joint pressure plan against Vladimir Putin, developed with European partners, and has spoken of a window for US mediation that he sees closing by the summer of 2027. Russia, for its part, continues to insist any settlement requires Ukraine to cede the four regions Moscow claims outright and address what it calls the conflict's "root causes" — language that has changed little in over a year of talks.

Why the ambiguity itself is the story

For markets watching the war for signs of a durable de-escalation — in energy, in credit spreads tied to European exposure, in the general pricing of geopolitical risk — an intelligence chief's visit is a weaker signal than a envoy's. Burns-style trips can precede either breakthroughs or ultimatums; they are read after the fact, not during. Ratcliffe has separately built a channel with Moscow through prisoner diplomacy — he personally negotiated the release of dual national Ksenia Karelina in April 2025 — so the visit could plausibly concern detainees, deconfliction, or narrower security matters rather than the broader shape of a peace deal. CBS News has reported that US intelligence assessments now flag a shift in Putin's risk tolerance, including greater willingness to test NATO members directly, which would make this a warning trip in the Burns mould rather than a negotiating one.

Energy traders have reason to watch regardless of which reading is correct: any hint that Washington and Moscow are managing overlapping files — Ukraine, sanctions enforcement, and Iran's position around the Strait of Hormuz — in parallel rather than in isolation would be relevant to crude pricing even without a Ukraine breakthrough attached to it. But as of Tuesday night, neither the CIA nor the Kremlin had said anything on the record, and the substance of the meetings remains entirely unconfirmed.

What comes next

The strike pause requested of Ukraine runs through Wednesday, after which resumed long-range attacks on Moscow — or their continued absence — will be the clearest tell of whether anything concrete emerged. Beyond that, attention turns to whether Witkoff and Kushner finally make the long-deferred trip to Kyiv, and to Zelensky's self-imposed deadline: his statement that Ukraine's window for US-brokered mediation closes by mid-2027 gives Washington a rough clock against which any outcome of this week's visit will be measured.
