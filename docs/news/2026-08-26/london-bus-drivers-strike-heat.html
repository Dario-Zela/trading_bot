London bus drivers strike again over 40C cab heat

An Arriva London bus — most of the fleet carries air-cooling units rather than full air conditioning. — Dan Sellers / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Arriva North London bus drivers resumed strikes after rejecting a pay-and-conditions offer, with cab temperatures reported up to 40°C and no firm date for air conditioning.

Arriva's north London bus drivers are back on picket lines this week after rejecting a company offer they say does nothing to fix cabs that have measured up to 40°C in this summer's heatwaves. The dispute, which began in mid-August, is shaping up as a test case for how UK transport operators handle labour costs in a warming climate — and how far they can defer capital spending before workers stop accepting promises.

Around 1,500 Unite members at eight Arriva London North garages — Barking, Edmonton, Enfield, Grays, Palmers Green, Stamford Hill, Tottenham and Wood Green — walked out for four days from 14 August, hitting more than 40 routes including the 19, 29, 66, 141 and 150. Unite paused the action a week later so members could vote on a revised offer from management. On 24 August they rejected it, and strikes resumed the following day, with further walkout dates now booked in blocks through mid-October.

"Arriva can fully afford to make meaningful changes our members can accept," a Unite regional officer said, adding there was "still time for Arriva to stop this disruptive action."

The heat problem, in numbers

Most of the affected buses carry air-cooling units rather than full air conditioning — systems Unite says cut cab temperature by only two to three degrees, leaving drivers working in conditions that can exceed 40°C during peak summer heat. One driver was signed off for five days after heatstroke during a late-June heatwave; others have reported fatigue, dizziness and near-misses they attribute to cab conditions. Drivers describe radioing operations control for relief during shifts and getting little response.

Arriva's managing director, Mo Wasim, has said the company takes driver welfare seriously and is "investing in longer-term fixes, including cab air conditioning upgrades." The dispute is over the definition of "longer-term." Arriva's UK bus division reported an operating profit of €127 million on the group's most recent published figures, a number Unite general secretary Sharon Graham has cited directly, arguing "a profitable company such as Arriva could easily invest in improving conditions."

Why this is a cost story, not just a labour story

Retrofitting full air conditioning across a fleet of hundreds of double-deckers is a real capital line item, not a rounding error, which is presumably why Arriva has so far offered a roadmap rather than a date. But the calculus cuts both ways: five strike weekends across August, September and October mean lost fare revenue, contract penalties from Transport for London for missed mileage, and reputational cost with a regulator that sets the terms of London's bus contracts. Extreme-heat working conditions are also becoming a more explicit line in union bargaining generally, not just at Arriva — this is London's fifth heatwave of the summer, according to Unite, and cab temperature is no longer being treated as an incidental gripe alongside pay.

For investors in UK transport operators, the read-through is that heat-driven claims — traditionally a footnote next to pay disputes — are turning into a recurring, quantifiable cost line as summers get hotter. A single retrofit programme is a one-off capex decision; a pattern of repeat, seasonal strike action across the sector is closer to an operating-margin problem.

The next date to watch is 4-5 September, the first of the newly announced walkout blocks, and whether Arriva returns to the table with firm retrofit dates rather than an investment commitment. TfL's response — and whether other London operators face similar demands as this heatwave-heavy summer continues — will show whether this stays a single-company dispute or becomes an industry-wide bargaining point.
