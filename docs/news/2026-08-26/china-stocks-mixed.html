Chinese stocks split as tech weakness offsets robotics and AI optimism

The Shanghai Stock Exchange building in Pudong — the city's blue-chip index has outperformed Shenzhen's tech-heavy board this week. — Baycrest / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.5)
In one sentence
Shanghai edged higher while Shenzhen hit a near one-month low, as traders split over whether China's robotics mania can outrun weak retail spending.

Chinese equities split cleanly along a fault line this week: Shanghai up, Shenzhen down, and the gap between them says more about trader psychology than about the economy itself.

On Tuesday the Shanghai Composite added 0.19% to close at 3,889.4, while the Shenzhen Component slipped 0.35% to 13,745.9 — its lowest level in roughly a month. The Shenzhen board, heavier in technology and growth names, has now given back 6.2% since August 17, when it stood at 14,704.27. The ChiNext board, home to many of China's smaller AI and semiconductor listings, is down 8.2% over the same stretch. Shanghai's blue-chip, state-enterprise-heavy index has been comparatively insulated.

The proximate trigger is nerves ahead of Nvidia's earnings, due Wednesday, which traders across Asia are treating as a referendum on whether the AI capital-spending boom still justifies current valuations. Chip and optical-component names that posted strong results still fell: Eoptolink dropped 1.7% despite revenue more than doubling and net profit up 91%; SMIC and Hygon Information Technology each lost 1.7%; Zhongji Innolight fell 2.3%. That pattern — good numbers, falling share price — is usually a sign that a sector was priced for perfection and is now being marked down on sentiment rather than fundamentals.

Retail sales versus robot IPOs

Underneath the index moves sits a genuine divergence in the real economy. China's retail sales grew just 0.6% year-on-year in July, badly missing forecasts of 1.5% and decelerating from June's 1%. Auto sales fell 17%, building materials 14.2%, jewellery 10.1% — all categories tied to property and big-ticket discretionary spending. Fixed-asset investment is down 6.7% for the January-July period. Services consumption has held up better than goods, but the headline is unambiguous: household spending is cooling.

Against that backdrop, the robotics trade has behaved as though the economy were booming. Unitree, the humanoid-robot maker known for backflipping demonstrations, closed its Shanghai debut up 460%, valuing the company near $50 billion after retail investors oversubscribed the offering roughly 8,000 times. The Solactive China Humanoid Robotics Index has gained about 75% over the past year — four times the CSI 300's return. Unitree's own chief executive, Wang Xingxing, has cautioned that robotics' "ChatGPT moment" is still two to ten years away, a rare instance of a founder talking down his own sector mid-mania.

That combination — soft consumption, frothy single-stock listings, a broader tech index sliding — reads less like conviction than like a market unable to agree on a story. Retail money is chasing individual robotics names on the theory that AI hardware can decouple from a slowing consumer; institutional flows into the Shenzhen-listed tech complex more broadly say the opposite. Both camps can be right about different companies and wrong about the index.

Beijing's Standing Committee of the National People's Congress is meeting from August 25 to 28 and could yet signal fresh stimulus, which would matter more to Shenzhen's consumer and property-adjacent names than to Shanghai's state banks and energy majors. Wednesday's Nvidia print is the nearer catalyst: a miss or cautious guidance would test how much of China's AI-linked rally was riding on borrowed optimism from the American chip cycle, rather than on Beijing's own numbers.
