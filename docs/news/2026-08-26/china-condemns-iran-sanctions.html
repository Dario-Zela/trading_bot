China condemns 'illegal' new US sanctions targeting Iran's trading partners

US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent unveiled the sanctions package, calling it a campaign of "economic asphyxiation" against Iran. — US Department of the Treasury / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Beijing has branded fresh US sanctions targeting Iran's trading network 'illegal,' layering new friction onto a US-China relationship already strained by tariffs.

The United States and China spent much of 2026 arguing about tariffs. This week the argument acquired a second front.

On Monday, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent unveiled what officials are calling "Operation Economic Outcast" — a sweeping sanctions package aimed at cutting off Iran's remaining sources of revenue. By Tuesday, Beijing had called it illegal, and the two largest economies on earth found themselves sparring over a third country's oil exports on top of everything else.

In one sentence: Beijing has branded fresh US sanctions targeting Iran's trading network "illegal," layering a new source of friction onto an economic relationship already strained by tariffs.

Nearly 60 targets, five sectors, one message

The Treasury designated close to 60 individuals and entities spread across roughly a dozen jurisdictions — Iran itself, China and Hong Kong, Singapore, the UAE, Switzerland, Malaysia, the Marshall Islands, Britain, France, Syria, Ukraine and Greece. Bessent framed the campaign as an attempt at "economic asphyxiation," targeting what he called Iran's five vital lifelines: digital assets, technology, gold, aviation and shipping.

A handful of Chinese firms were named directly, including Shenzhen Sweet Ocean Technology, described by Treasury as a procurement front for Iranian buyers of test instruments, along with two Shenzhen logistics operators, Huamei Lianyun and Bositong, and a shipping company, Lilimoon Navigation. Notably absent from the list: any of the Chinese banks that clear payments for Iranian oil. Asked why, Bessent demurred — "Why would I want to blow up the global financial system?" — a line that reads as much as a warning of what could still come as an explanation for what was left out.

Beijing's response

China's position had already been staked out days earlier, when Washington asked Beijing to join the pressure campaign. Foreign ministry spokesperson Lin Jian told reporters that China "firmly opposes illegal unilateral sanctions" that lack a basis in international law or United Nations Security Council authorization, and that sanctions and military pressure "will not help resolve the issue" but risk escalating tensions that serve no one. After Monday's announcement, Beijing repeated the charge in sharper terms, vowing to take the steps necessary to protect its companies and interests.

The stakes for China are concrete rather than symbolic. Chinese refiners — many of them the small, independent "teapot" plants clustered around Shandong province — buy an estimated 90% of Iran's oil exports, arriving via a shadow fleet of aging tankers that transfer cargo ship-to-ship and relabel it, often as Malaysian crude, to obscure its origin. Shandong and Dalian ports alone have taken in more than 1.5 million barrels a day this year. Four teapot refineries have already been sanctioned since last year; Monday's round stopped short of touching the banks and refiners that keep that trade running, which is itself a signal of how much leverage Washington still has in reserve.

Why this compounds, rather than replaces, the tariff dispute

For a trading relationship already absorbing tariff escalation and retaliation on both sides, sanctions enforcement opens a second, less predictable channel of friction. Tariffs are negotiated, scheduled, and — however painfully — priced in. Sanctions lists are not: they can expand overnight, and the threat of secondary sanctions on Chinese banks hangs over energy markets specifically because Bessent chose, for now, not to pull that lever. Any move against Chinese financial institutions handling Iranian oil payments would ripple beyond crude prices into the broader dollar-clearing system that underpins global trade — which is precisely the kind of escalation Beijing's statement was warning against, and precisely the kind Washington has so far held back.

What comes next

Watch two things: whether the Treasury follows through on secondary sanctions against Chinese banks — the step Bessent explicitly declined to rule in or out — and how Beijing's promised "necessary measures" materialize, whether through retaliatory designations, trade measures, or simply continued defiance at the pump. Iranian oil flows into Shandong are the most direct gauge of whether either side is prepared to escalate further, or whether this settles into the kind of managed friction that has come to define the broader US-China relationship this year.
