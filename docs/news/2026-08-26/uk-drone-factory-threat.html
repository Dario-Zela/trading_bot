Former Russian minister warns UK drone factories could face attacks from 'unknown sources'

A Storm Shadow/SCALP cruise missile, the weapon at the centre of the UK-Ukraine technology transfer that prompted the Russian warning. — Ank Kumar / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
A former Russian deputy foreign minister warned UK drone factories could be hit by 'unknown sources', offering no evidence, timeline or method.

A former Russian deputy foreign minister has raised the prospect of attacks on British factories that build drones for Ukraine — though he was careful, several times over, not to say who might carry them out.

Speaking to BBC Newsnight, Andrei Fedorov, who now advises the Kremlin and directs a Moscow political research foundation, said he could not "exclude 100 percent that there might be some 'semi-military' action against Britain." Pressed on what that might look like, he offered: "something might happen with enterprises, with the factories which are producing drones for Ukraine." He mentioned hacking too — carried out, he said, by "unknown source, not official level, of course" — and vaguer "political steps." Asked directly whether Russia itself would strike, he said no: any attack would come "not by Russia, but from unknown sources."

It is a carefully hedged form of threat — deniable by design, attributable to no one, and delivered by a man who left government office years ago. That does not make it noise. It lands amid a broader pattern of sabotage and hybrid attacks that MI5's director general, Ken McCallum, has already linked to Russian state intent, and at a moment when Britain's drone industry has just become considerably harder for Moscow to ignore.

The trigger

The remarks followed Prime Minister Andy Burnham's announcement in Kyiv this week that Britain would declassify blueprints for UK-made components of the Scalp missile — the French-built cousin of Storm Shadow — clearing the way for Ukraine to manufacture the long-range cruise missile domestically. Scalp variants have already been used to strike military and industrial sites deep inside Russia; handing over the technology to build them locally is a significant escalation in the UK's material support for Kyiv.

Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov followed with his own complaint, saying the UK was "participating in this war" and adding "fuel to the fire." Burnham's reply, when asked about it by reporters, was blunt: "Who started the fire? That's the question, isn't it?" — a pointed reference to Russia's 2022 invasion.

A factory sector with something to lose

The warning targets an industry that barely existed at this scale two years ago. Under a £752 million contract announced in April, three British manufacturers — Tekever, Windracers and Malloy Aeronautics — are committed to supplying at least 120,000 drones to Ukraine by the end of 2026, the largest single-nation UAV commitment of the war. Tekever alone is opening a fourth UK site, a large facility in Swindon expected to create around 1,000 jobs, alongside expansion in Suffolk. The government has pitched the build-out explicitly as dual-purpose: supporting Ukraine while establishing Britain as a European centre of drone manufacturing.

That is precisely what makes the factories a plausible symbolic target, and precisely why Fedorov's comments, however vague, were picked up rather than shrugged off. Sites tied to war supply chains have already featured in the pattern of sabotage attributed to Russian intelligence networks across Europe — arson at a Warsaw shopping centre, damage to undersea cables in the Baltic — which McCallum has described as an effort to generate "sustained mayhem" on British and European streets using operatives who are not, on paper, Russian state actors. That precedent is what gives "unknown sources" its edge: it is the exact operating model security officials say Moscow already uses.

What isn't there

What the warning does not contain is anything close to specifics. No factory was named, no timeframe given, no method beyond a shrugged-off mention of hacking. Fedorov is a private adviser now, not a serving official with operational knowledge, and his framing — "sooner or later, there might be not only verbal but maybe some visible reaction" — reads as an attempt to signal resolve to a domestic audience as much as a warning to London. Russian state media and commentators have been pushing Putin to respond more forcefully to Western arms support, and Fedorov himself has previously been willing to publicly needle the Kremlin's own conduct of the war. Rhetoric of this kind serves a purpose in Moscow independent of whether anything follows it.

None of that rules out the risk. It simply means investors and manufacturers are being asked to weigh a real vulnerability — under-defended commercial sites now central to a NATO ally's military supply chain — against a threat with no operational content attached to it yet.

The next marker to watch is less rhetorical: whether the UK's National Protective Security Authority updates guidance for defence contractors, and whether Tekever, Windracers or Malloy disclose any change to site security as their new facilities come online later this year.
