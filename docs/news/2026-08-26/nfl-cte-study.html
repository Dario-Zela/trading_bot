Study finds at least 25% of former NFL players had CTE brain disease

Cross-sections showing a normal brain (left) next to a brain with advanced Stage IV chronic traumatic encephalopathy (right). — Boston University CTE Center / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A study finding 24.5%-plus CTE prevalence in former NFL players lands amid live litigation over the league's concussion settlement and betting-platform liability.

The finding itself reads like something out of a coroner's report, because in a sense it is: Mass General Brigham and Boston University researchers examined the brains of 878 former NFL players who died between 2016 and 2021, and concluded that at least 24.5% of them had chronic traumatic encephalopathy. The real number, the researchers say, is probably a good deal higher — their upper-bound estimate reaches as high as 97.7%, though they caution that both extremes are unlikely and the truth sits somewhere in the wide territory between.

The study, published this week in The BMJ, is a family affair for CTE research: it draws on the same Boston University brain bank that first put the disease on the map with Aaron Hernandez and Frank Gifford, among others. Of the 878 deceased players identified, 235 families donated brain tissue for examination — and of those, 215, or 91.4%, showed pathological signs of CTE. Extrapolating from a self-selected donor pool to the full former-player population is the statistical sleight of hand that produces both the study's headline number and its enormous uncertainty band; families are more likely to donate a brain when a player showed cognitive symptoms before death, which skews the sample toward more severe cases. The researchers built their 24.5% floor by assuming, conservatively, that every non-donating family's player was disease-free — a deliberately implausible assumption meant to establish a lower bound, not a best guess.

What isn't ambiguous is the severity among those who did test positive. More than 91% of the CTE-positive brains were classified as Stage IV, the most advanced form of the disease, and 61.3% of CTE cases met clinical criteria for dementia. Boston University's Ann McKee, the pathologist who has anchored this research for over a decade, called the findings a “wake-up call” in comments to the Boston Globe.

Money follows the science

CTE research has moved from medical curiosity to financial liability with some regularity since the NFL's founding concussion settlement, and this study lands in the middle of an active legal and regulatory moment rather than a quiet one. The league's original 2015 settlement, which covers 65 years of claims, has now paid out more than $1.4 billion, but a Washington Post investigation this year found the NFL routinely uses medical loopholes to deny claims — precisely the kind of gap that a fresh, high-profile prevalence estimate tends to widen in court.

Sports betting operators have their own, more novel exposure. In March, the Public Health Advocacy Institute sued DraftKings, FanDuel, Genius Sports and the NFL jointly, arguing that microbetting and player-prop products are designed to be addictive — a case that ties the health of players' brains to the health of the products built to bet on them. DraftKings shares are already down roughly 50% over the past year on unrelated concerns about prediction-market competition eating into sportsbook volume; a jury verdict linking betting products to gambling harm, layered onto declining engagement, is not a scenario the market has fully priced.

The ownership stakes have gotten bigger

The backdrop that makes all of this newly relevant to capital markets is that NFL teams themselves are now investable assets in a way they weren't a decade ago. Average franchise value hit roughly $9.3 billion this year, up 31%, and the league's 2024 decision to admit institutional capital has brought private equity firms — Arctos has stakes in the Buffalo Bills and Los Angeles Chargers, Sixth Street has a piece of the Patriots — directly onto team cap tables. Those investors are underwriting media-rights growth and stadium economics; a legal environment that reopens questions about the league's historical handling of brain injuries, or that produces a wave of new individual claims outside the settlement's release, sits on their balance sheets as a tail risk that valuation models rarely line-item explicitly.

What to watch

The BMJ study itself changes no court docket today, but it arrives as ammunition for plaintiffs' lawyers ahead of ongoing claims disputes under the 2015 settlement and for the Pennsylvania betting-liability suit working through discovery. The next concrete marker is whether the NFL's settlement claims office, under continued Washington Post-style scrutiny, faces pressure from Congress or state attorneys general to loosen its medical criteria — a move that would raise the settlement's total cost and, by extension, the league's cash calls on owners carrying fresh private-equity partners.
