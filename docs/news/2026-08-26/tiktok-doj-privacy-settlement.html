TikTok nears $400m settlement with DOJ over child-privacy violations

TikTok's former US headquarters in Culver City, California, long the backdrop to the company's regulatory battles. — Coolcaesar / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
TikTok will pay $400m to settle DOJ claims it collected under-13 users' data without consent — a costly but market-irrelevant compliance bill.

TikTok is closing out one of its oldest legal headaches with one of its largest checks. The company and parent ByteDance have agreed to pay $400 million to resolve a Justice Department lawsuit accusing them of harvesting personal data from millions of children under 13 without parental consent — and then ignoring parents who asked for that data to be deleted.

The settlement, confirmed this week, closes a case the DOJ filed jointly with the Federal Trade Commission in August 2024. U.S. District Judge George H. Wu dismissed the underlying action with prejudice on 21 August, based on the parties' own stipulation. TikTok admits no wrongdoing.

What the DOJ alleged

The complaint painted a picture of a platform that knew it had an underage-user problem and chose not to fix it. Investigators said TikTok let children sign up despite knowing their real ages, ran a dedicated "Kids Mode" that itself collected data without proper safeguards, and altered its registration flow in ways that made it harder to catch and remove young users — all while sitting under a standing legal obligation, dating to 2019, to prevent exactly this.

That obligation traces back to TikTok's predecessor, Musical.ly, which paid $5.7 million to the FTC in 2019 — at the time a record fine under the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA) — and accepted a consent decree requiring it to police underage accounts going forward. The DOJ's case argued TikTok inherited that decree and broke it anyway.

The mechanics of the payout

The $400 million splits into two pieces. TikTok pays $300 million immediately. The remaining $100 million is contingent: it falls due only once a court formally vacates the 2019 Musical.ly consent decree, which still carries its own reporting and monitoring obligations pending a hearing set for 21 September. Until then, TikTok remains under both the old decree and the terms of the new settlement — a belt-and-braces arrangement typical of how the DOJ has structured recent COPPA enforcement.

As part of the deal, TikTok has also committed to tighter age-verification controls and expanded tools letting parents see and manage what their children can access on the app.

Context that's shifted since the case was filed

When the DOJ sued in 2024, TikTok's American operations still sat under a Chinese parent facing a legislated ban. That's no longer the picture. In January, ByteDance completed a restructuring that handed roughly 80% of a new US joint venture — TikTok USDS — to a consortium led by Oracle, Silver Lake and MGX, with ByteDance retaining a minority stake and no access to US user data. Oracle now acts as the venture's designated security auditor.

This settlement, in other words, resolves conduct from the pre-restructuring era rather than testing the new ownership structure. It's a legacy bill being paid down just as TikTok's US business tries to present itself as a clean, domestically supervised operation — one more item cleared off the ledger on the way to a fresh start.

Why it doesn't move markets

TikTok isn't publicly traded, so there's no ticker to react and no earnings call where analysts will press on the number. For ByteDance — last valued in the hundreds of billions of dollars — $400 million is a rounding error, not a balance-sheet event. And unlike enforcement actions that hint at industry-wide crackdowns, this one is narrowly about TikTok's specific, well-documented history with underage users rather than a new legal theory that other platforms need to worry about.

What's worth watching instead is the 21 September hearing on the Musical.ly decree, and whether regulators use the newly restructured, Oracle-supervised TikTok as a test case for how seriously the new ownership takes compliance — the kind of scrutiny that, if it goes badly, would matter far more than a nine-figure fine ever could.
