Vistry's £350m state housing win lifts FTSE as Burnham's wider pledge draws fire

A modern housing estate in Salford, Greater Manchester — the kind of development the government's £39bn affordable homes programme aims to multiply. — Kevin Waterhouse / Geograph Britain and Ireland (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Vistry surged 16% on a £350m state housing grant while the FTSE 100 added just 0.2%, showing a single contract moving one stock, not the market.

Vistry shares had their best trading day on record on Tuesday, jumping as much as 16% to lead the FTSE 250, after the housebuilder landed the largest single grant available under the government's new Social and Affordable Homes Programme. The FTSE 100, by contrast, barely stirred — up 0.2% to 10,872 — a gap that tells its own story about how narrowly this news actually lands.

A record grant, a record day

Homes England confirmed Vistry as one of 33 "strategic partners" — a mix of councils, housing associations and private builders — sharing the first tranche of a £39bn, ten-year programme to rebuild England's social housing stock. Vistry's award, £350m to support 3,028 homes, was the maximum available to any single partner and, the company said, significantly larger than what it received under the previous funding round.

"We are delighted that Homes England has made this significant announcement that will create over 73,000 new homes," said Vistry chief executive Adam Daniels. The relief in that statement is worth noting: Vistry's partnerships arm — which builds affordable and social housing on behalf of registered providers — has spent the best part of two years hamstrung by housing associations that held off placing orders while they waited to see how much government money was actually coming. Tuesday's award removes that uncertainty, at least for Vistry's slice of the pipeline.

Elsewhere on the index, Melrose Industries took the FTSE 100's own top spot, up as much as 10% after Orange County prosecutors closed a criminal investigation into a chemical incident at its GKN Aerospace site in Garden Grove, California, without filing charges. GKN separately announced a $100m compensation fund for residents affected by the May evacuation, and is targeting a September 28 restart of full production. Two very different stocks, two very different stories, both landing on the same trading day — a reminder that index-level calm can mask sharp moves underneath.

The wider pledge, and the row it's reopened

The Vistry grant was only the visible edge of a much bigger announcement. The government set out its first wave of allocations from the £39bn Social and Affordable Homes Programme: nearly £10bn going to 33 strategic partners to deliver more than 70,000 homes over the next decade, with roughly 60% earmarked for social rent and the rest split across shared ownership and other affordable tenures.

That 60% figure is where the politics sharpened. Andy Burnham, the Greater Manchester mayor, had argued the entire £39bn programme should go toward council homes let at social rent — telling supporters at one point that all of it "should be dedicated to council homes." The allocation unveiled this week falls short of that, and critics were quick to frame it as a climbdown from Burnham's own position. Housing minister Matthew Pennycook pushed back on the suggestion directly, telling BBC Breakfast: "No, I don't [think Burnham has rowed back] ... that's exactly what we're doing through this initial wave of funding," adding that the government had "taken a pragmatic approach."

What's harder to dispute is the scale of the ambition against the scale of England's housing shortfall. Successive governments have missed housebuilding targets for years, and 70,000 homes spread across a decade and a mix of tenures is a modest annual flow against a shortage estimated in the hundreds of thousands. The government has billed it as the biggest council housebuilding drive since the postwar period; whether the funding, the sites and the delivery partners can actually keep pace with that framing is a separate question from whether the money has been announced.

What the market is actually pricing

Put the two threads together and the picture is fairly plain. Investors reacted to a concrete, company-specific number — £350m, 3,028 homes, cash Vistry can put to work now — not to the abstract shape of a ten-year national programme whose tenure mix is still being argued over in Westminster and Manchester. That's a rational distinction: a grant is a grant, while a decade-long funding pledge is only as good as the allocations, planning permissions and labour that eventually turn it into finished homes.

The next test for Vistry, and for the wider builders, is whether Tuesday's award marks the start of a steadier flow of Homes England contracts or a one-off catch-up payment after two years of funding limbo. With over £16bn of the £39bn programme still unallocated outside London and roughly £5bn earmarked for the capital, further awards — and further single-stock moves — are likely before the programme's shape becomes clear.
