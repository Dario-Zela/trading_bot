Fed holds rates for a fifth meeting as dissent grows and Collins keeps a hike alive

Susan Collins, President of the Federal Reserve Bank of Boston, has signaled openness to a September rate increase absent continued disinflation. — Federal Reserve Bank of Boston / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Boston Fed's Susan Collins says a September rate hike would be appropriate absent sustained disinflation, extending July's rare three-way dissent for tighter policy.

The Federal Reserve left interest rates unchanged for a fifth straight meeting on July 29, holding the federal funds rate at 3.50–3.75%. But the 9-3 vote was the real story: three regional bank presidents dissented in favor of an immediate quarter-point hike, the first time in a decade that a unified bloc of policymakers has broken publicly with the chair over which direction rates should move.

Now one of the Fed's most consistent hawks has all but told markets what it will take to make that hike happen at the September meeting.

"Should evidence of sustained inflation progress not materialize, I believe it will be appropriate to tighten policy soon."

That's Boston Fed President Susan Collins, in comments this week that the Financial Times reported extend to explicit openness to a September increase. Collins isn't a swing vote whose mind changed overnight — she's long occupied the hawkish end of the committee. What her remarks confirm is that the conditional case for tightening, built on the July dissents, has survived three weeks of subsequent data without softening.

A family fight, on the record

The July meeting was new Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's first running the committee, and he got the debate he'd said he wanted. Cleveland's Beth Hammack, Minneapolis's Neel Kashkari and Dallas's Lorie Logan all voted for a 25-basis-point increase rather than a hold — the most dissents in a single direction since September 2016. Warsh, notably unbothered, told reporters afterward: "I asked for a good family fight and I got one... it was a real family fight."

That's an unusual thing for a Fed chair to welcome publicly. Dissents are typically framed as noise to be managed down, not evidence the committee is working as intended. Warsh's comment reads as an attempt to normalize open disagreement rather than paper over it — which makes the hawkish minority's position harder for markets to dismiss as a fringe view heading into September.

The inflation test

Collins' framing puts the decision on a data trigger rather than a fixed calendar date. She has described the current stance as capable of producing "gradual disinflation," helped along by higher long-term bond yields doing some of the Fed's tightening work for it — but she's also called price complaints "pervasive" in conversations with businesses and households across New England. Economists surveyed ahead of this week's core PCE reading expect the July print to hold at a 3.3% annual rate, unchanged from June and still well above the Fed's 2% target.

That's the number Collins' conditionality hinges on. A flat or firmer core PCE print, followed by a CPI report that confirms price pressure isn't fading, would validate the hawks' July dissent and hand Warsh a committee more willing to move. A softer print — even a modest one — gives the hold camp its argument for a sixth straight pause.

What ties it together

The throughline from July's vote to Collins' comments this week is that the Fed's hawkish flank isn't retreating even as broader economic data has softened elsewhere. Three presidents dissented on the record; one of the Fed's more influential voices is now saying publicly what would justify siding with them. For trading desks, that shifts the September meeting from a coin-flip framed around growth data to a bet resolved almost entirely by the inflation prints landing in the next two weeks. Watch the core PCE release this week and the CPI report due before the FOMC meets again in September — either could swing the vote count that was already unusually close in July.
