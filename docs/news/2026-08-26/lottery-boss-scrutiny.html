National Lottery's incoming boss faces scrutiny over gambling-industry background

National Lottery ticket dispensers in a UK supermarket — the monopoly now run on an interim basis by a former bookmaking executive. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
MPs have challenged the Gambling Commission over Allwyn's choice of a sanctioned ex-William Hill executive to run the National Lottery on an interim basis.

Britain's National Lottery has spent three decades selling itself as a harmless flutter — the £2 ticket that funds village halls and Olympic swimmers, kept carefully apart in the public mind from the slot machines and betting apps that make up the rest of the gambling industry. That distinction is now under strain, after the operator handed interim control of the £6.5bn, ten-year licence to a former bookmaking executive with a disciplinary record from the regulator that oversees him.

Allwyn UK confirmed on 19 August that Phil Walker, who spent six years at William Hill including stints as UK & Ireland managing director and chief commercial officer, will take over as interim chief executive from 7 September, replacing Andria Vidler while a permanent successor is sought. Vidler, who steered the National Lottery's licence transition from Camelot and a subsequent digital overhaul, stays on in an advisory capacity until the end of October.

A regulator's own sanction, now under his old employer's roof

The appointment has drawn a formal challenge from Parliament. Labour's Dawn Butler and Conservative Iain Duncan Smith have written to the Gambling Commission saying they are "deeply concerned" by the choice, pointing to a personal sanction the Commission imposed on Walker in 2024 over what it called "widespread and alarming" failures to enforce anti-money-laundering and terrorist-financing controls during his time at William Hill. The firm was fined a record £19.2m in March 2023 across three group entities, after failures that let at least one customer gamble £23,000 in minutes without any checks. The MPs have asked the regulator to explain what weight, if any, it gave that history when Allwyn assessed Walker's fitness for a role overseeing the country's only legal lottery monopoly.

A second thread compounds the concern: Walker sits on the board of Game Nation, Britain's third-largest chain of adult gaming centres, for a role reportedly worth over £50,000 a year. He has agreed to step back from it from 1 September for the duration of his interim contract — a concession MPs are treating as confirmation the conflict was real rather than incidental.

Allwyn's defence is narrow but pointed: the Gambling Commission did not strip Walker of his personal operating licence over the William Hill failings, which it takes as evidence the regulator already judged him fit to work in the sector. The Commission itself has declined to comment publicly, citing a policy of not discussing operators' personnel decisions — leaving the question of oversight resting, for now, on Allwyn's own judgment rather than the regulator's.

What comes next

The episode lands as the National Lottery already faces broader scrutiny of its public-interest obligations under Allwyn's licence, and as MPs continue pressing the Gambling Commission for a substantive reply. The next marker will be whether that letter produces one, and who Allwyn names as Walker's permanent successor once the search concludes.
