England to warn citizens to stockpile food amid climate and hostile-state risks

The Cabinet Office at 70 Whitehall, which is drafting the national resilience campaign. — Paul the Archivist / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
The UK is drafting public guidance urging households to stockpile food and water against both climate shocks and hostile-state disruption to power, water and phone networks.

The Cabinet Office is preparing to tell British households, for the first time in generations, to keep a store of tinned food, bottled water and essential medicines at home — not against a single named danger, but two at once: extreme weather and the possibility that a hostile state disrupts the country's power, water or phone networks.

The guidance is still being drafted and has not been published, but officials have described its shape in enough detail to make the intent clear. A national public-awareness campaign, expected later this year, will urge "simple" and "small but important" steps — enough food and water to get a household through a few days of disruption, according to accounts of the plan given to the Guardian and other outlets. It follows an update to the government's classified contingency plans, the so-called War Book, revised for the first time since 2004, and an expansion of the UK's National Risk Register to include cyber-attacks on data, water and policing systems alongside floods, heatwaves and storms.

Two threats, one campaign

What makes this notable is the pairing. Governments have quietly advised emergency stockpiling before, usually framed around flooding or storms. This campaign explicitly sits climate disruption alongside "hostile state" activity — a category that, in official usage, spans cyber intrusions, sabotage of infrastructure and the kind of hybrid pressure Whitehall associates with Russia. Darren Jones, the Chief Secretary to the Prime Minister, has said the government has "accelerated our Home Defence Planning," citing risks exposed by the war in Ukraine and Middle East conflict, and warning that artificial intelligence brings threats as well as opportunities if "weaponised by criminals against us." The trigger list is concrete rather than hypothetical: an Iran-linked cyber intrusion at a British power plant this summer, a wildfire in the West Midlands on 17 August, and a Cobra meeting convened earlier in August over heatwave response all sit in the immediate backdrop.

Catching up with the neighbours

Britain is, by its own admiral's account, behind. Admiral Sir Tony Radakin, the chief of the defence staff, has said the UK lacks the "culture of total defence" that Nordic and Baltic states already practise. Sweden posted an updated "In Case of Crisis or War" pamphlet to 5.2 million households in 2024, recommending at least three litres of water per person per day; Finland followed with digital guidance of its own. The UK's approach is expected to be softer in tone — officials stress no household will be compelled to buy anything — and pitched partly as a way of freeing emergency services to focus on the most vulnerable if everyone else can cope for a few days unaided.

The unresolved question is reach. Campaigners have already noted that with one in seven households with children facing food insecurity, a message to "stock up" lands differently depending on income — one reason food-policy voices are urging the guidance to include, alongside the tins, a plea to share with neighbours. The campaign's publication date, and how far it goes beyond advice into anything resembling a target, is the next thing to watch.
