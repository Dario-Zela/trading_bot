Labour MPs push Burnham to back voting reform within first 100 days

Prime Minister Andy Burnham, whose own history of backing proportional representation has emboldened backbenchers pushing for early reform. — Alecsandra Dragoi / No 10 Downing Street (Open Government Licence v3.0)
In one sentence
Nearly 100 Labour MPs are pushing PM Andy Burnham to commit to a national electoral reform commission within his first 100 days, testing his authority over his own backbenches.

The test of any new prime minister is not what he promises in the glow of victory, but what he does when his own backbenches start pushing. Andy Burnham, a little over five weeks into Downing Street, is about to find out.

When parliament returns from recess, an amendment tabled by the Leeds North West MP Alex Sobel will come back to the Commons floor on 2 September. It calls for a national commission to review Britain's first-past-the-post voting system — and it already carries 185 signatures from MPs across eight parties, 98 of them Labour. That is more support than any other amendment currently before parliament, according to figures reported by the Guardian, and a YouGov poll of MPs found 53% back some form of review of the electoral system.

"Now we know a majority of MPs back this proposal... I know the prime minister is committed to reforming our electoral system." — Alex Sobel

An awkward inheritance

Burnham arrives at this fight with an unusually clean paper trail. He is the first prime minister in modern British history to have openly backed proportional representation before taking office, a position he traces to his years running Greater Manchester under the Supplementary Vote system — an experience he has described as the moment that converted him from adversarial Westminster politics to something more consensual. At Labour's 2022 conference, as a backbench voice rather than a leader, he endorsed a manifesto commitment to PR for the next parliament.

That history is precisely why his MPs think they have leverage now. Rachel Blake, a former Treasury minister who backs the amendment, put it plainly: "The PLP agrees with Andy Burnham that first past the post is broken, and that a national commission is the way to get a grip on it." Lizzi Collinge, another signatory, framed the case in terms of fairness rather than party advantage, arguing that first-past-the-post "particularly devalues the votes of renters, young people and BAME people."

The MPs want more than warm words. They are asking Burnham to announce the commission before the autumn budget in October, with a mandate to report back ahead of the next general election — squarely inside the informal 100-day window by which a new government's seriousness is often judged.

Number 10's caution

Downing Street has not said no. But it has not said yes either. Burnham's office describes him as "strongly committed to electoral reform" while stopping short of endorsing Sobel's specific commission, treating the question instead as one for Labour's next manifesto — a decision, in other words, that requires a fresh mandate rather than an executive order five weeks into a term nobody voted for on this basis.

The hesitation is not hard to explain. Reforming the system that delivered a government its majority always invites the charge of a stitch-up, particularly with Reform UK's rise reshaping the electoral map that first-past-the-post is meant to referee. Some of the amendment's most notable signatures — Miatta Fahnbulleh, Florence Eshalomi and Jo Platt, all now serving as ministers — may yet be withdrawn under the convention that frontbenchers do not sign backbench amendments, a quiet reminder of how differently the calculation looks once an MP has a government job to protect.

Why this matters beyond the amendment

The vote itself is unlikely to bind the government to anything; amendments of this kind are more often a pressure gauge than a legislative event. What it measures is whether Burnham can hold the space between the reformist instincts that got him elected leader and the institutional caution that comes with actually running the country — and whether his backbenchers, flush with the size of their coalition, are willing to test that gap publicly this early.

A prime minister who spent years outside Westminster arguing that its voting system was broken now has to decide whether fixing it is worth spending political capital on before he has banked any wins. The 2 September vote will not settle the argument. But it will show, within his first weeks in office, whether Burnham leads his party on this question or is led by it.
