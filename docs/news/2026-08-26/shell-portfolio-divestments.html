Shell sheds US chemicals and Cyprus gas stake as it narrows to oil and gas

The Shell Pennsylvania Petrochemicals Complex in Monaca, under construction along the Ohio River — now among the assets Shell is looking to sell. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Shell drew up to $8bn in bids for its US chemical plants and sold its Cyprus gas stake to MOL for $720m, sharpening its focus on oil, gas and LNG.

Shell is in the middle of the biggest portfolio pruning of Wael Sawan's tenure as chief executive, and the shape of what's being cut says as much as the numbers involved. Over the past month the company has drawn non-binding bids of up to $8 billion for four U.S. chemical plants, while quietly closing out a $720 million sale of its stake in the Eastern Mediterranean's largest deepwater gas discovery. Different continents, different buyers, same underlying logic: Shell wants to look more like an oil-and-gas company and less like the diversified major it spent two decades building.

The chemicals auction

Shell's four U.S. chemical sites — spread across Louisiana, Texas and Pennsylvania — have attracted interest from ExxonMobil, LyondellBasell, private equity firm Apollo Global Management and the chemicals arm of Kuwait Petroleum Corporation, according to reporting first carried by the Financial Times. Bidders submitted non-binding offers last month, some for the whole portfolio and others for pieces of it, with the total package valued at up to $8 billion.

The headline asset is the Monaca complex outside Pittsburgh, an ethylene cracker and three polyethylene units that began operating in 2022 after roughly $14 billion of Shell investment — the first cracker built outside the U.S. Gulf Coast in twenty years. That an entire national portfolio anchored by a $14 billion plant might fetch $8 billion says something about how the market now prices chemicals: a cyclical downturn in polymer margins, driven by oversupply from new Chinese and Middle Eastern capacity, has left even relatively modern assets trading at a steep discount to what it cost to build them. Shell is separately marketing its European chemicals assets, which people close to the process expect to fetch a lower valuation still.

The rationale from Shell has been unusually blunt. Sawan has told investors the company does not consider itself the "natural owner" of its chemicals business, and has identified roughly $45 billion of capital tied up in chemicals and renewables that he judges underperforming relative to what it could earn elsewhere in the portfolio. Under his "performance, discipline, simplification" framework, businesses that cannot compete internally for capital get sold, shrunk, partnered away or shut. Chemicals has landed squarely in that bucket.

Exiting Cyprus

The smaller but no less telling piece of the story played out in the Eastern Mediterranean. In late July, Shell agreed to sell its wholly owned subsidiary BG Cyprus — holder of a 35% non-operated stake in the Aphrodite gas field — to Hungary's MOL Group for up to $720 million, including contingent payments tied to development milestones. The deal is expected to close in early 2027, subject to regulatory approval.

Aphrodite, discovered in 2011 roughly 160 kilometres south of Limassol, is estimated to hold about 104 billion cubic metres of gas and 8 million barrels of condensate — the largest deepwater discovery yet made in Cypriot waters. Shell inherited the stake through its 2016 acquisition of BG Group and is exiting a project that, on paper, still has years of development ahead of it: first gas isn't expected before 2031, once a final investment decision is taken and a roughly 250-kilometre subsea pipeline is built to carry the gas to Egypt for sale to state buyer EGAS.

"Our decision to exit is driven by disciplined capital allocation and portfolio choices, as we focus on opportunities that strengthen our integrated LNG value chain," Shell's Integrated Gas president said in announcing the deal. Chevron remains operator with a 35% stake, alongside Israel's NewMed Energy at 30%. For MOL, the acquisition is its largest upstream growth move since it bought into Azerbaijan's Azeri-Chirag-Gunashli field in 2019 — chief executive Zsolt Hernádi called it exactly that.

Two sales, one direction

Set side by side, the deals describe a company narrowing its aperture in two directions at once: out of chemicals, where it lacks scale and competitive edge against integrated producers like Exxon and dedicated players like LyondellBasell, and out of long-dated, capital-intensive gas developments that don't feed directly into Shell's core LNG trading and shipping business. What's left is meant to be a tighter oil-and-gas and LNG major, generating cash rather than tying it up in decade-long project timelines or cyclical petrochemical margins.

The buyers' side of the ledger tells its own story. Exxon and LyondellBasell circling Shell's chemical plants, and MOL doubling down on Eastern Mediterranean gas even as Shell heads for the exit, suggest the assets themselves aren't unwanted — just no longer strategically core to the seller. That distinction matters for how the sales get read: not a fire sale reflecting weak demand, but a major recalibrating what it wants to own versus what a rival is willing to pay a premium-adjusted price for.

The next marker is binding bids for the U.S. chemicals portfolio, which people close to the process expect within the coming months; a signed agreement would crystallise just how much of that $14 billion Monaca investment Shell ultimately recovers. On Cyprus, the Aphrodite sale still needs regulatory sign-off before its early-2027 close, with the underlying field's own final investment decision — the point at which the $4 billion development actually gets built — expected the same year.
