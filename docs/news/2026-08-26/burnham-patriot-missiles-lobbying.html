Burnham to press Trump for Patriot missiles before Russia's winter strikes

UK Prime Minister Andy Burnham, who made Kyiv his first foreign trip in office and now plans to press Trump on Patriot missile stocks. — Alecsandra Dragoi / No 10 Downing Street (Open Government Licence v3.0)
In one sentence
UK PM Burnham will lobby Trump in New York for Patriot interceptors after Ukraine's stockpile ran dry, with winter energy strikes looming and no US commitment yet.

The train that carried Andy Burnham into Kyiv on Monday morning was, in a narrow sense, just the latest stop on a well-worn diplomatic circuit — Western leaders marking Ukraine's independence day, reaffirming solidarity, posing for photographs with President Volodymyr Zelenskyy against a backdrop of sandbagged monuments. But for Britain's still-new prime minister, it was something more specific: his first foreign trip since taking office, and the start of a lobbying campaign aimed squarely at Washington.

Burnham's message in Kyiv was blunt enough. Ukraine is running out of Patriot interceptors — the one weapon in its arsenal that can reliably bring down Russian ballistic missiles — just as Moscow prepares to repeat, and likely intensify, last winter's campaign against the country's power grid. Having heard that appeal in person from Zelenskyy, Burnham now says he intends to carry it to Donald Trump.

"I've not fully confirmed the details yet, but it's likely I will go, particularly after today's discussion," Burnham told Bloomberg in Kyiv on Monday evening. "Leaving today, I'm quite clear what I need to do in the next few weeks." He is expected to travel to New York next month for the UN General Assembly, where he and other European leaders are weighing a fresh gathering of the so-called Coalition of the Willing timed to catch Trump in the same city — and, they hope, in a receptive mood.

A shortage with a number attached

The scale of the problem is not abstract. Ukraine has received roughly 600 Patriot interceptors over the course of the war, according to figures cited by a Zelenskyy adviser, and has expended nearly all of them. Zelenskyy is now asking for an emergency package of 300 more before the cold sets in — enough, Ukrainian officials argue, to plug the gap left as Russia's missile production has outpaced what Kyiv can shoot down. On 6 July, by one account, Ukraine failed to intercept a single ballistic missile fired at it, the first day of the war that happened.

The difficulty is supply. Current estimates put total US Patriot interceptor stocks at somewhere between 759 and 827 missiles — roughly a third of what the Pentagon held before American and Israeli strikes on Iran earlier this year diverted stock toward US bases and regional allies in the Middle East. Handing Ukraine the 300 interceptors Zelenskyy wants would, on those figures, cut what remains in American warehouses by close to half. That is the trade-off Trump will be asked to weigh, and it explains why the administration has so far offered warm words rather than commitments: at a recent meeting with Zelenskyy, Trump said the missiles were "very hard to get" but that "we are going to see if we can make some of them available."

Ukraine's own build-out of energy defences is a hedge, not a substitute. Kyiv has spent the year distributing power generation into smaller, harder-to-target sources and shoring up substations after last winter's bombardment knocked out roughly 80% of the country's generation capacity at points and left a six-gigawatt shortfall, according to First Deputy Prime Minister Denys Shmyhal. Repair bills from that campaign alone have been estimated at $64–70 billion. None of that changes the fact that only Patriot batteries can reliably stop the ballistic missiles Russia aims at the grid — and that Moscow, by Western intelligence assessments, is already massing for a renewed strike campaign against Kyiv's energy infrastructure in the coming weeks, backed by a sharply expanded drone programme.

Kyiv, then the wider coalition

Burnham's visit was not a solo affair. He met Zelenskyy alongside a cluster of European figures marking Ukraine's 35th independence anniversary — European Council President António Costa, Moldovan President Maia Sandu, Luxembourg's Prime Minister Luc Frieden, Estonian President Alar Karis and Nordic and Baltic leaders among them — with German Chancellor Friedrich Merz and French President Emmanuel Macron joining by video link to co-chair a session of the Coalition of the Willing. The UK used the trip to announce that defence contractor MBDA would be permitted to share classified technical information on the Storm Shadow/SCALP cruise missile with Ukraine, a step toward local assembly lines rather than continued reliance on shipments from abroad.

That detail matters for reading the Patriot request correctly. European governments are simultaneously trying to help Ukraine build more of its own capability and trying to unlock the one category of weapon — long-range interceptors — that only the United States can supply at scale. The Kremlin, for its part, has cast the diplomacy as evidence that the West wants the war prolonged rather than ended. Spokesman Dmitry Peskov said this week that "such escalatory statements have prevailed... they want the war to continue," a framing Moscow has used before to characterise Western air-defence support as an obstacle to negotiation rather than a defensive necessity.

Why this is still theatre, for now

What Burnham secured in Kyiv was attention, not interceptors. No release of additional Patriot stock has been confirmed by the Trump administration, and the arithmetic of America's own depleted stockpile means any decision will be contested inside the Pentagon regardless of what Trump tells European visitors in New York. Burnham's trip is best read as one node in a wider European effort — alongside Merz, Macron and the Coalition of the Willing — to keep Washington's attention on Ukraine's air defences before Trump faces the domestic pressure that has, at various points, pushed his administration toward scaling back aid.

The test of whether this amounts to more than diplomatic choreography will come at the UN General Assembly session in New York next month, where Burnham hopes for a face-to-face opening with Trump. Russia, meanwhile, is not waiting on the outcome: the strike campaign Ukrainian and Western officials expect against the power grid could begin before that meeting even takes place.
