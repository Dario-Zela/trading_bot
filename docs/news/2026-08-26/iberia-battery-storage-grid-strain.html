Spain and Portugal battery-storage firms scramble as grid strain persists

An electrical substation near Miranda de Ebro, Spain — the kind of grid infrastructure now bottlenecking the country's battery-storage build-out. — Zarateman / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
Spain and Portugal's battery storage industry is racing for scarce grid connections sixteen months after a blackout, with a capacity market approved but not yet live.

The blackout lasted less than a day. The reckoning has taken sixteen months and counting.

On 28 April 2025, the Iberian Peninsula lost roughly 15 gigawatts of generation — about 60% of Spanish demand at that moment — in the space of five seconds. Some 55 million people across Spain, Portugal and a sliver of southern France went dark for up to 23 hours. Trains stopped, hospitals switched to generators, card payments failed. Spain's National Commission on Markets and Competition later put the economic cost near $1.8 billion; more than 30 sanction proceedings against grid operator Red Eléctrica and other utilities are still open, with no final ruling on responsibility as of this month.

What the blackout exposed, more than any single cause, was how thin Spain's buffer had become. At the time, the country had installed roughly 25 megawatts of grid-scale battery storage — a rounding error next to the 6-7 gigawatts of pumped hydro it could call on, and dwarfed by the pace at which solar and wind capacity had been added. Sixteen months on, that mismatch hasn't closed. It has mutated into a scramble: battery developers racing for grid connections that don't exist yet, curtailed solar farms losing money in daylight, and two governments trying to build a market mechanism fast enough to matter.

A market that grew 589% and is still not enough

Spain's battery fleet did expand fast after the blackout — installed BESS capacity rose from 28 megawatts in April 2025 to 193 megawatts a year later, according to Red Eléctrica figures, a 589% increase. Project applications in the connection pipeline grew even faster, up 464% year-on-year.

But 193 megawatts is still a fraction of the 22.5 gigawatts of storage Spain says it needs by 2030 under its national energy and climate plan, and it trails the roughly 500-megawatt target the country had originally set for 2025 alone. The UK, by comparison, already had 5.6 gigawatts of battery storage online before the Iberian blackout happened at all.

The constraint isn't appetite or capital. Red Eléctrica's registry shows accumulated connection requests now total 22.1 gigawatts — developers are asking. The problem is physical: substations and access points in the sunniest, most oversubscribed corners of the grid are saturated. An AFRY analysis cited by trade publication Energy-Storage.news found Spanish storage projects worth roughly €7 billion sitting in connection queues, representing about 40% of the national storage target — part of a wider European logjam in which some 455 gigawatts of battery projects across eight countries, including Spain, Britain, Germany and Italy, are stuck waiting on distribution-grid upgrades.

The practical result is curtailment: solar plants ordered to switch off because the wires can't carry what they generate. National curtailment is forecast at 3.05 terawatt-hours in 2026, roughly 2.5% of generation — but the average masks the real story. In Badajoz province, home to some of Extremadura's largest solar parks, curtailment has reached an average of 37% this year, according to an analysis by the Circe research centre, with the worst-affected connection nodes discharged as much as 22.5% of the time. Developers are advised to avoid any grid node running above 130% of rated saturation; large stretches of Extremadura and Aragón now exceed it.

Utilities co-locate, optimisers chase megawatts

Faced with a queue rather than an open grid, the biggest players are building storage next to generation they already own rather than seeking fresh connections. Iberdrola, which has more than 56 gigawatts of renewables installed globally, has been pairing batteries with existing solar and wind sites to capture frequency response, balancing-market payments and day-ahead arbitrage from a single connection point — the company installed Spain's first battery at a renewables project at its Arañuelo photovoltaic plant in Cáceres and has since added storage at sites in Burgos and Huelva. In Portugal, EDP is building the 180-megawatt, 360-megawatt-hour BigBATT project at Carregado, part-funded by the EU Innovation Fund, and has paired battery storage with its Alqueva floating solar array in the Alentejo.

A newer layer of the market — battery "optimisers" that trade flexibility on behalf of asset owners rather than build generation themselves — is growing fastest of all. Entrix, a German-founded flexibility trader, says it has passed 3 gigawatts and 8.5 gigawatt-hours of contracted battery capacity across Europe and expects 2 gigawatts operational by the end of this year, citing Iberian demand explicitly as a post-blackout tailwind; the firm raised €43 million in March to fund the expansion. Developers Prosolia and Grenergy have both advanced standalone storage projects in Spain and Portugal this year, betting that flexibility revenue — not subsidy — will carry the economics.

Portugal, meanwhile, is chasing its own version of the same race. Lisbon has committed €400 million to grid upgrades since the blackout and lifted its battery storage target toward 750 megawatts by early this year, rising to roughly 2 gigawatts by 2030, backed by a system-services auction. It had already awarded €113 million in incentives to 43 battery projects totalling 500 megawatts in the months before the blackout. Grid operator REN's queue and Red Eléctrica's are, functionally, two ends of the same bottleneck.

Whether pricing alone can fix it

The deeper question — the one that will decide who makes money and who doesn't — is whether Spain's answer to the bottleneck is a market mechanism or a regulatory one. So far it has tried to make it a market mechanism. Since the blackout, Madrid has exempted battery flexibility from grid-access fees that still apply to consumption, opened voltage-control services to storage and renewables for the first time, and published new grid-access rules meant to route capacity toward projects that can actually connect, backed by a saturation map REE was due to publish in February. In late May, the European Commission approved a Spanish capacity market worth an estimated €9 billion over ten years, modelled loosely on Italy's MACSE scheme, which offers 15-year inflation-linked payments for firm capacity. Italy's first MACSE auction cleared in September 2025 four times oversubscribed, at around €52,000 per megawatt per year for four-hour storage — a data point Spanish investors are watching closely.

But Spain's own capacity auctions aren't expected to run until 2027, and developers still don't know the contracted volumes, price caps, or how much weight will go to new projects versus plants already operating. That gap — pricing signals approved in principle, not yet live in practice — is where the current scramble is happening: firms competing today for scarce connection slots under rules that won't be finalised for another year. If the queue clears faster than the market design catches up, competition and grid investment alone may be enough. If it doesn't, the pressure for Madrid or Brussels to intervene more directly — allocating connections by fiat rather than by price — will grow, and that would reshape who captures the returns from Iberia's storage build-out.

The next concrete marker is REE's saturation capacity map, meant to tell developers which nodes are actually worth bidding for, followed by the first details of Spain's capacity-market auction rules expected before the 2027 tender. Portugal's system-services auction outcome, still pending, will show whether Lisbon's more modest target is achievable on the current grid. Until one of those lands, the country with Europe's fastest-growing solar fleet will keep switching part of it off in the middle of the day.
