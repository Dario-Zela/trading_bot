Twitch and Amazon sued by streamers over undisclosed AI training on livestreams

Twitch's logo. The platform, owned by Amazon, is accused of enrolling streamers in AI training by default. — Diegosegura.me / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
A Twitch streamer's class action claims he and millions of others had their broadcasts fed into Amazon's AI training by default, without real consent or pay.

Warren Pandiscia streams from Connecticut, the kind of small-to-mid Twitch creator whose channel keeps the platform's lights on without ever making him famous. Now he is the named plaintiff in a class action that asks a blunt question: when a platform quietly starts feeding your broadcasts into an AI training pipeline, who owns what comes out the other side?

The suit, filed August 20 in the US District Court for the Northern District of California, targets Twitch and its owner, Amazon. It alleges the companies used millions of streamers' livestreams, clips, video-on-demand uploads and chat logs to train generative AI models without licensing the content or paying for it — and, Pandiscia claims, without meaningfully asking first.

A default nobody chose

The mechanics are the heart of the complaint. On August 12, Twitch rolled out a setting called "Training for Generative AI," tucked into the Security and Privacy tab of account settings. It was switched on for every creator by default. There was no email, no pop-up, no advance notice — streamers found out largely because word spread on social media and gaming press picked it up.

Asked on a company livestream why the setting wasn't opt-in, Twitch's chief product officer Mike Minton gave an answer that has since become the case's most-quoted line: "If it was opt-in, nobody would opt in." Under public pressure, Twitch added an opt-out option days later. But the complaint argues the fix is cosmetic. The toggle only covers a streamer's own channel — anyone who chats on someone else's stream is still captured under that channel owner's settings, regardless of their personal preference. Pandiscia's filing puts it plainly: the systems are "incapable of obtaining" consent from everyone whose voice, image or words end up in a captured broadcast.

The complaint also alleges the scraping predates the disclosure by some margin, claiming Twitch and Amazon have been drawing on creator content for AI training since at least 2024.

The claims and what's being asked

Pandiscia's lawyers have pleaded breach of implied contract, breach of express contract, unjust enrichment and unfair business practices. The suit seeks damages, restitution, disgorgement of the profits Amazon has drawn from AI products built partly on this data, and an injunction to stop the practice going forward. Neither Twitch nor Amazon has issued a public response to the scraping allegations themselves.

Why a default setting matters so much

The legal fight is really about consent architecture, not just AI. Opt-out defaults are a well-worn tool in tech — most users never touch a settings menu, so whatever is pre-selected becomes the practical policy for the overwhelming majority of accounts. Minton's comment more or less conceded that point: an opt-in version of this feature would have starved Amazon's models of data, because almost nobody would have signed up voluntarily.

That is precisely the dynamic the plaintiffs want a court to treat as legally significant rather than just a design choice. If a platform can prove people didn't actively object, is that consent — or is it a workaround for consent that would never be freely given? Twitch is not the only company facing this question: YouTubers have brought a similar suit against Apple over alleged unauthorized scraping of video content for AI training, part of a widening docket of creator-versus-platform cases testing whether user-generated content can be repurposed for AI without separate payment.

What's next

Amazon and Twitch have yet to respond formally in court, where the first real test will be whether Pandiscia's contract and unjust-enrichment theories survive an early motion to dismiss. A ruling in the streamers' favor would push platforms toward genuine opt-in consent and revenue-sharing for AI training data — a precedent that would ripple well beyond gaming, into any platform sitting on a trove of user-generated video, audio or text.
