Shell completes ARC Resources takeover, exits Indian renewables

Shell chief executive Wael Sawan has framed the ARC Resources deal as a liquids-and-LNG play, not a simple gas land grab. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Shell shareholders approved its $16.4bn ARC Resources buy into Canadian Montney gas the same week Shell agreed to sell its Indian renewables arm for $1.8bn.

Shell spent the past week doing two things that look contradictory until you notice they're funded by the same logic: buying deeper into Canadian gas while walking away from Indian solar and wind.

On July 14, shareholders of ARC Resources voted 99.5% in favour of being acquired by Shell in a deal that values the Montney-focused Canadian producer at roughly $13.6bn in equity, or $16.4bn once debt and lease obligations are folded in. The same week, Shell agreed to sell Sprng Energy, its Indian renewables arm, to Aditya Birla Renewables for $1.8bn. Neither deal is small. Together they read as a company telling its shareholders, in the plainest terms available to a board, where it thinks the returns are.

The Montney bet

ARC Resources is one of the largest pure-play operators in the Montney Formation, a shale basin spanning British Columbia and Alberta that has become North America's most important source of liquids-rich natural gas. Under the terms agreed in April, ARC shareholders receive CAD 8.20 in cash plus 0.40247 of a Shell share for each ARC share held — a package Shell says adds an average of 390,000 barrels of oil equivalent per day of production through 2030 and roughly $1.5bn in incremental annual free cash flow.

The transaction has cleared most of its hurdles. Beyond the shareholder vote, it has Canadian Competition Act and Transportation Act clearance, US Hart-Scott-Rodino antitrust approval, and exemptive relief from the Alberta Securities Commission covering Shell's UK and Dutch share buyback programmes. The Court of King's Bench of Alberta reviewed the plan of arrangement on July 15. Assuming that clears, Shell expects to close in the second half of 2026.

Chief executive Wael Sawan has been explicit about why the geography matters. ARC's acreage in British Columbia sits adjacent to Shell's existing position around Groundbirch, which he has called "the primary supply source for LNG Canada Phase 1." The framing he has pushed in public remarks is worth noting for what it isn't: "Don't look at this as the acquisition of an E&P with a predominantly gas portfolio," Sawan said. "Look at it as a liquid-rich addition to Shell with an upside of LNG that we are uniquely positioned to be able to unlock." Shell puts its production compound annual growth rate through 2030 at roughly 4% post-deal, up from about 1% on the pre-ARC portfolio.

The Sprng exit

On the other side of the ledger, Shell Overseas Investment is selling its full stake in Solenergi Power, the parent of Sprng Energy, to Aditya Birla Renewables. The India-focused portfolio comprises 5.0 gigawatts-peak of solar and wind capacity — 3.3GWp operating, 1.7GWp under contract — built to supply state electricity distribution companies. The buyer is funding the purchase with debt and equity from Grasim Industries and Global Infrastructure Partners, the BlackRock-owned infrastructure fund, and expects to close before year-end.

Machteld de Haan, Shell's president of downstream, renewables and energy solutions, described the sale in unadorned terms: "This agreement reflects Shell's continued focus on adjusting the portfolio in our power business." On the buy side, Aditya Birla Group chairman Kumar Mangalam Birla called the deal a step toward a combined platform of "20 GW plus in the coming years," merging Sprng's utility-scale assets with ABRen's existing 4.4GW, largely commercial-and-industrial, book.

Reading the pivot

The Sprng sale is not an isolated retreat. Shell has already exited European onshore renewables and closed its retail power businesses in the UK, the Netherlands and Germany. It has also formally diluted its climate targets: the 2035 carbon-intensity reduction goal was scrapped last year, and the 2030 target was loosened to a 15-20% range. The stated aim across the power division now is a 10% return on average capital employed by 2030 — a bar renewables assets in emerging markets have generally struggled to clear against the balance-sheet cost of capital, while liquids-rich Montney gas, backed by contracted LNG offtake, clears it more easily.

What connects the two deals is capital discipline applied asymmetrically: short-cycle, high-return fossil assets in stable jurisdictions get bought; capital-intensive renewables exposure in emerging markets gets sold, with the proceeds recycled rather than reinvested in kind. For trading desks watching LNG supply curves, the relevant signal is less about ESG positioning than about where a major with genuine optionality is choosing to point its balance sheet — deeper into Groundbirch and LNG Canada Phase 1 feedstock, not into new grid-scale power in South Asia.

The near-term catalysts are dated. The Alberta court's ruling on the ARC arrangement, expected imminently after the July 15 hearing, is the last major gate before a second-half close. The Sprng sale to Aditya Birla is due to complete before the end of 2026, formally closing out Shell's presence in Indian utility-scale renewables. Both dates will confirm whether the strategy Sawan has been describing in public remarks is fully executed, or still has room to be renegotiated.
