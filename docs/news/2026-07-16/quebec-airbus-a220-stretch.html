Quebec offers to co-invest if Airbus stretches the A220

An Air Canada Airbus A220-300, the jet built at Airbus's Mirabel plant north of Montreal, on approach to Toronto Pearson. — Stevenhe1997 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Quebec says it would help fund a longer Airbus A220 if the planemaker greenlights the stretch, a decision Airbus has yet to make.

Quebec is telling Airbus it would come back to the table with more public money if the planemaker decides to stretch the A220, the smallest jet in its lineup and the one Quebec taxpayers have bankrolled for over a decade.

A spokesperson for provincial Economy Minister Bernard Drainville said by email on Wednesday that "discussions are indeed underway, but it is still too early to draw conclusions," adding that "the government of Quebec would like to be partners in this project, notably with the federal government." Ottawa's response was thinner — aerospace is strategically important, a federal spokesperson said, declining to comment on the specifics.

Quebec has standing to ask. The province holds a 25% stake in the A220 program and hosts its primary assembly line at Mirabel, north of Montreal — Airbus's only other A220 line runs in Mobile, Alabama. That stake dates to 2020, when Airbus took full commercial control of the jet after Bombardier, the original developer, exited the program entirely.

What a stretch would look like

The variant under discussion, expected to be badged the A220-500, would lengthen the fuselage by roughly five frames and add about 20 seats, pushing capacity toward 180 in a single-class layout. The wing and engines would stay largely as they are, which means range would fall — reportedly by around 13% versus the existing A220-300. Airbus wants the bigger jet to lift production volumes and finally turn a program that has lost money since its Bombardier days into a profitable one.

The timing is not settled. Industry reporting this month suggests Airbus will not launch the stretch at next week's Farnborough Airshow, with a fourth-quarter announcement now seen as more likely. Leasing companies, whose appetite determines how fast a new variant gets into airline fleets, have been lukewarm — several are said to worry a larger A220 would blur into A320neo territory and dent the residual values of jets they already own.

The case against writing another cheque

Not everyone in Quebec wants the province back in. The Montreal Economic Institute puts total provincial support for the program at C$2.1 billion since 2015 — including C$1.3 billion when the jet was still Bombardier's CSeries and a further C$380 million in 2022 — against a program the think tank says now carries zero book value. "At some point, Quebec will have to realize that it is throwing good money after bad in an attempt to save a bet it has already lost," the MEI's Renaud Brossard said.

That critique is unlikely to change Drainville's calculus on its own. Aerospace employs tens of thousands of workers in the Montreal region, and losing A220 production — or watching Airbus favor its Alabama line for future output — would cost the province more than a stretch program's price tag. The next marker is Airbus's own: whether it launches the A220-500 at all, and if a launch decision does land in the fourth quarter, whether Quebec's cheque is on the table when it does.
