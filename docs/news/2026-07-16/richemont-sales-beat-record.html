Richemont shares hit record as 20% sales growth crushes forecasts

A Cartier boutique — jewelry sales at the Richemont-owned maison rose 24% in the quarter, powering the group's earnings beat. — Jakub Szypulka / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Richemont's quarterly sales grew 20%, nearly double forecasts, as Cartier and Van Cleef jewelry demand sent shares to a record high in Zurich.

Compagnie Financière Richemont, the Swiss group behind Cartier and Van Cleef & Arpels, posted quarterly sales growth of 20% at constant exchange rates on Tuesday — nearly double the 11% consensus forecast compiled by Bloomberg and Visible Alpha. Shares in the Geneva-based conglomerate jumped as much as 7.4% in Zurich trading to a record high, dragging the broader Stoxx Europe Luxury 10 index up 2.5% in sympathy.

The headline number, for the three months to June 30, translates to €6.33 billion in sales against analyst expectations of roughly €5.9 billion. Jefferies called the print a "blow-out"; Bernstein said Richemont had "smashed expectations"; RBC Capital Markets went with "very strong." Bank Vontobel used the word "flabbergasting."

Jewelry does the heavy lifting

The outperformance was not broad-based across Richemont's portfolio so much as concentrated in one division doing exceptional work. The Jewellery Maisons — Cartier, Van Cleef & Arpels, Buccellati and Vhernier — generated €4.73 billion in sales, up 24% at constant rates and well ahead of the 13.5% analysts had modeled. It was the seventh consecutive quarter of double-digit jewelry growth, and management described the gains as broad-based across brands, regions and sales channels rather than concentrated in one-off high-jewelry pieces that can retail north of $1 million.

Regionally, the Americas led with 27% growth, up from 18% in the prior quarter, which the company attributed to "continued strength in local demand" — a signal that wealthy US shoppers are still absorbing Cartier's price increases without flinching. Japan posted the biggest surprise, at 36%, a figure Bernstein flagged as the standout line in the release. Asia Pacific, which includes the long-struggling Greater China market, grew 21%, up from 14% previously, evidence that the region's two-year luxury slump may be turning a corner. Europe brought up the rear at 11% but still beat expectations.

Gold's run to record highs this year — prices are up roughly 30%, breaching $3,500 an ounce — has pushed Richemont to raise jewelry prices repeatedly over the past year. That the volume growth held up anyway is the clearest evidence yet that its core clientele is more sensitive to design and heritage than to sticker price.

The watch business is still catching its breath

Not every part of the group shared in the celebration. Specialist Watchmakers — the division housing IWC, Jaeger-LeCoultre, Panerai and A. Lange & Söhne — saw sales fall 7%, weighed down by soft demand in China, Hong Kong and Japan. That is nonetheless an improvement on the 11% decline logged the previous quarter, and haute-horology names with loyal collector bases and constrained production, such as A. Lange & Söhne and Vacheron Constantin, held up better than the division average. Richemont does not break out margin detail quarterly, but the group's full-year figures reported in May showed watchmaking operating on a thin 3.4% margin against jewelry's 30.5% — a reminder of just how much of Richemont's profit engine now runs on rings and bracelets rather than movements and calibers.

What it says about the luxury consumer

Richemont's result lands at a moment when the wider luxury sector has been groping for a bottom. LVMH and Kering have both spent much of the past two years managing declines, and Richemont itself has increasingly been treated by analysts at UBS, JPMorgan, HSBC and Deutsche Bank as the sector's best-positioned name — its shares are Europe's best-performing major luxury stock this year, ahead of turnaround stories like Kering and Burberry.

The read-through is narrower than "consumers are fine." It is that the top of the wealth pyramid — the buyers underwriting Cartier's Love bracelets and Van Cleef's Alhambra pieces — remains willing to spend through tariff noise, a stronger dollar's effects on cross-border shopping, and rising input costs passed on at the till. Berenberg analyst Nick Anderson's framing is a fair one: the Chinese consumer, not the American one, is probably still the swing factor for the sector as a whole heading into the second half, and Richemont's China-region growth, while improving, remains more modest than its American and Japanese numbers. Jewelry's relative insulation from discretionary belt-tightening — driven by emotional and collectible value rather than everyday utility — also means Richemont's beat says more about resilience among affluent buyers of durable goods than it does about the broader consumer economy tracked by retail-sales data.

The next test comes with half-year results in November, when Richemont will report whether the Americas momentum held through the northern-hemisphere autumn and whether Chinese demand has genuinely turned or merely stabilized off a low base. LVMH's own quarterly update, expected in the coming weeks, will show whether Richemont's strength is company-specific — a function of Cartier and Van Cleef's particular pull — or the first sign that the luxury sector's two-year contraction is ending.
