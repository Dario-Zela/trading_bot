HSBC reviews Turkish retail banking as it leans into cross-border business

HSBC's retail banking arm in Türkiye is under review, while its cross-border and wholesale operations continue. — C. Ford / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA)
In one sentence
HSBC put its Turkish retail and domestic SME banking under strategic review while ring-fencing cross-border business, its latest footprint cut under CEO Georges Elhedery.

HSBC has opened a strategic review of its retail banking and domestically focused small-business operations in Türkiye, a move that could end with the sale or wind-down of a business the bank has run since taking over Demirbank in a 2001 rescue deal. What survives untouched is everything cross-border: the wholesale banking arm serving multinationals and Turkish exporters will keep operating as before.

The bank confirmed the review on 7 July, saying it covers the retail banking business of HSBC Bank A.Ş. and its portfolio of smaller and mid-sized companies with primarily domestic banking needs. "The review will consider all options for the retail banking business," HSBC said. "No decisions have yet been made." HSBC Türkiye currently runs roughly 77 branches and employed around 1,900 people as of last count.

A line drawn around the border

The split is deliberate. HSBC says it will "continue to have a strong presence in the country as it supports international companies investing in Türkiye and Turkish companies expanding overseas" — the corporate and institutional banking unit that serves those clients is explicitly excluded from the review. In other words, HSBC isn't leaving Türkiye. It's deciding whether it wants to be a Turkish bank at all, as opposed to a bank that happens to operate in Türkiye for clients who need to move money in and out of it.

That distinction fits a pattern set by chief executive Georges Elhedery, who since October 2024 has been paring back HSBC's geographic footprint in pursuit of $1.5 billion in cost savings by the end of 2026. Retail banking sales in France and Sri Lanka have already closed, Bangladesh's retail unit is being shut, and the Canada and Argentina businesses were sold off in 2024. Egypt is reportedly under similar scrutiny. The common thread is scale: HSBC is retreating from consumer banking in markets where it lacks the deposit base or branch density to compete with local lenders, while keeping the network business that plays to its actual advantage — moving capital across borders for multinational clients.

The Turkish backdrop

The timing says something too. Turkish annual inflation was running at 32.1% in June, and the central bank has held its policy rate at 37% to keep it there, with energy costs from the war in the Middle East adding fresh pressure. The lira has continued to slide against the dollar and euro through the year. None of that makes domestic retail banking — mortgages, small-business loans, consumer deposits — an easy place to grow margins. Cross-border settlement and trade finance, by contrast, generate fee income that is largely indifferent to what the lira is doing.

HSBC has given no timeline for a decision, and a sale, a partial wind-down and a straightforward retreat to a smaller domestic footprint all remain on the table. The next marker to watch is Türkiye's central bank meeting on 23 July, which will show whether policymakers see room to ease — a signal on economic direction, that, more than anything, is likely to shape how HSBC's review turns out.
