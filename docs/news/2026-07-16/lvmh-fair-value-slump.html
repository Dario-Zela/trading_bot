LVMH looks fairly valued after 38% slump, not cheap

Louis Vuitton's headquarters and flagship store on the Champs-Élysées, Paris. — Ank Kumar / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
After a 38% three-year slide, LVMH's DCF value comes out near €480 against a €496 share price — fair, not cheap.

LVMH shares trade at roughly €496. A discounted cash flow model built on the company's own free cash flow — €13.1 billion over the trailing twelve months — puts fair value near €480. That is not a rounding error kind of gap; it is close enough that, on this measure at least, the stock is not obviously cheap. It may in fact be a shade rich.

That matters because the headline number everyone has been repeating — a share price down about 38% from its 2023 peak — has started to sound like a bargain-hunter's rallying cry. It isn't one, or not straightforwardly. A stock can fall 38% and still be fully priced if the earnings underneath it have fallen too, or if the market has correctly decided the growth that used to justify a premium multiple is not coming back on the old schedule.

What the model says

Simply Wall St's screen, drawing on LVMH's cash flow trajectory, lands the group's intrinsic value a few percent below where the stock currently sits — call it fairly valued, tilting slightly expensive. On earnings multiples the picture is similar: LVMH trades on a price-to-earnings ratio of about 22.6 times, above the broader luxury-goods industry average of roughly 17.3 times, though below the average of roughly 29 times among its large-cap luxury peers such as Hermès and Richemont. The company scores just one of six standard valuation checks as attractive, according to the screen — a long way from the kind of multi-metric bargain signal that usually accompanies a genuine value opportunity.

That is a useful corrective to the instinct that any stock down 38% must be oversold. The decline has been driven substantially by real deterioration in the numbers, not merely by sentiment. Fashion & Leather Goods — the division that houses Louis Vuitton and Dior and produces the bulk of group profit — saw revenue fall 9% on a reported basis in the first quarter of 2026, with organic sales down 2%. Full-year 2025 revenue for the division fell to €37.8 billion with operating profit down 13%, even as the operating margin held at a still-formidable 35%. Profit has fallen alongside the stock; that is why fair value has followed price downward rather than staying put while the market panicked around it.

Isolating LVMH from the sector

The harder question — the one that separates a genuine read from a screen output — is whether LVMH's slump is a company problem or an industry one. The evidence points toward company-specific.

Across the group, Asia excluding Japan delivered 7% organic growth in the first quarter of 2026, its strongest showing in that region since late 2023, as mainland China's recovery gathered pace. Yet within that same quarter, Fashion & Leather Goods still declined. Hermès and Brunello Cucinelli both grew in China in the period; LVMH's fashion houses and Gucci (owned by rival Kering) did not. Richemont, meanwhile, posted 20% sales growth and a record share price in its most recent results — proof that Chinese luxury demand has not collapsed so much as become selective about which brands it rewards.

That selectivity is the real story sitting underneath the valuation math. Bernard Arnault, LVMH's chairman, described the outlook for the rest of 2026 as a choice between recovery and something considerably worse, and said he could not yet say which prevails — an unusually blunt admission from an executive whose public language is normally calibrated for reassurance. The group's non-fashion divisions, and its broader geographic base outside China, have cushioned the numbers. But the flagship business — the one investors are actually paying the luxury premium for — has not yet turned the corner that its industry peers are already rounding.

What it means for the trade

A stock priced within a few percent of its discounted cash flow value, sitting on a P/E above the sector average, is not the coiled spring that a bare 38%-decline headline implies. The market has already done a fair amount of the repricing that a genuine turnaround would otherwise reward. Anyone buying LVMH now at €496 is not picking up cheap luxury exposure; they are paying close to fair value for a bet that the brand-specific softness in Fashion & Leather Goods resolves before the broader industry recovery, evident at Richemont and Hermès, leaves it behind.

The next real data point lands July 28, 2026, when LVMH reports second-quarter results — the first full read on whether Fashion & Leather Goods has stabilized following the China-driven pickup seen elsewhere in the group during the first quarter.
