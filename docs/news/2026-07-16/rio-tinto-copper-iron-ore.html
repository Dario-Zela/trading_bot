Rio Tinto holds copper guidance despite Kennecott outage as iron ore rebounds

The Bingham Canyon Mine in Utah, part of Rio Tinto's Kennecott operation, where a smelter furnace outage cut copper output. — Doc Searls / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Rio Tinto held its 2026 copper guidance and slashed cost estimates despite a Kennecott furnace outage, as Pilbara iron ore shipments jumped 17% on the quarter.

Rio Tinto's second-quarter production update contained a number that should, on paper, have rattled the market: copper output down 7% year-on-year, driven by a mechanical failure at its flagship US mine that the company says will keep hurting output for another six months. Instead, the miner walked away from the update having left its full-year copper guidance untouched — more than 800,000 tonnes for 2026 — and having cut its cost guidance for the metal nearly in half. Iron ore, meanwhile, did the opposite of copper: shipments jumped 17% quarter-on-quarter as the Pilbara worked through the backlog left by cyclone season.

Taken together, the two halves of the update tell a coherent story about how Rio Tinto is managing a supply shock in its highest-growth commodity while leaning on its oldest one to keep the numbers respectable.

The Kennecott problem

The copper miss traces to a single piece of equipment: the flash smelting furnace at Kennecott, Rio Tinto's century-old operation in Bingham Canyon, Utah. The furnace, which has run on the same hearth lining since 2016, needed a shutdown for relining — part of a rebuild the company had budgeted at roughly $33 million. The disruption cut deeper than planned. Kennecott's refined metal production fell 49% year-on-year in the quarter to just 20,000 tonnes, and Rio Tinto now says the site will remain affected for roughly six months as concentrate availability stays constrained.

That matters beyond Rio Tinto's own ledger. Kennecott supplies an estimated 15% to 20% of US domestic copper demand, and its hiccup lands at a moment when copper is central to just about every growth narrative Wall Street is currently pricing — grid buildout, EV production, and the power-hungry data centres behind the AI investment cycle. A soft patch at one of the country's largest integrated copper operations is the kind of detail traders watching US copper premiums will want to track through the second half.

Adding to the drag, consolidated copper production also slipped at Escondida, the Chilean mine Rio Tinto holds a 30% stake in alongside operator BHP. Combined, Kennecott and Escondida were enough to pull group copper output down 7% for the quarter, even as first-half production still edged up 1% overall.

Why guidance held anyway

The offset came from Oyu Tolgoi, the underground copper-gold mine in Mongolia that Rio Tinto has spent years ramping up. Output there grew 31% year-on-year in the first half, and it's the main reason chief executive Simon Trott could tell investors that "copper equivalent production rose 3% in the first half," alongside "continued progress" at the Mongolian project. Oyu Tolgoi's ramp-up was scheduled well before the Kennecott furnace failure, but its timing is doing real work now — it's the buffer that let Rio Tinto hold guidance rather than trim it.

The cost side of the copper story is arguably the bigger surprise. Rio Tinto cut its 2026 copper C1 net unit cost guidance to 30–50 US cents per pound, down from a prior range of 65–75 cents — a reduction the company attributed to higher gold by-product credits (gold and other by-products are stripped out of the C1 cost measure, so a stronger gold price mechanically lowers net copper costs) and productivity gains across the portfolio. It's a large enough revision that it invites scrutiny rather than automatic acceptance, but it lines up with a broader pattern this year of gold prices doing a lot of the heavy lifting for miners with by-product credits, and Rio Tinto has not walked it back since the initial disclosure.

Iron ore does the heavy lifting

While copper absorbed the operational shock, iron ore delivered the quarter's cleanest good news. Pilbara shipments hit 85.3 million tonnes, up 7% year-on-year and the highest quarterly level since 2020; group-wide iron ore sales reached 88.8 million tonnes, a 17% jump from the cyclone-hit first quarter. First-half Pilbara production was the strongest since 2018, beating consensus estimates on both output and shipments.

The rebound wasn't costless. Rio Tinto flagged that the fuel-price spillover from the US-Iran conflict pushed diesel costs from roughly $85 to $140 a barrel, adding about 80 cents a tonne to Pilbara production costs over six months — a reminder that geopolitical shocks reach commodity miners through the input side just as readily as the output side. Even so, the volume recovery was large enough to more than absorb it, and the Pilbara result is what gave Rio Tinto the headroom to hold guidance across the group rather than issue a caveated update.

The read for traders

The two halves of this report reinforce each other. A miner confident enough to hold copper guidance through a smelter outage is making a bet that Oyu Tolgoi's ramp-up curve is durable and that underlying demand — for electrification, grid infrastructure, and AI-linked power buildout — will still be there when Kennecott comes back online in six months. The iron ore rebound is a separate but related signal: construction and infrastructure demand snapped back quickly once cyclone disruption cleared, which is the kind of demand resilience that also underpins the copper thesis.

The next real test of that bet comes when Kennecott's furnace rebuild finishes and the market can see whether Rio Tinto's cathode output actually recovers on the timeline management has set out. Between now and then, the group's half-year results — due in late July — should show whether the C1 cost cut and the Oyu Tolgoi growth rate are holding up under closer audit than a quarterly production note allows.
