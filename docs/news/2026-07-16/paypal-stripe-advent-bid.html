PayPal shares jump 17% on reported $53bn Stripe-Advent takeover approach

PayPal's headquarters in San Jose, California. — Sagar Savla / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Stripe and Advent's reported $53bn, $60.50-a-share bid for PayPal sent shares up 17%, though analysts call the offer an opening bid, not a final one.

PayPal Holdings shares jumped 17% on Wednesday, their sharpest one-day move on record, after Reuters reported that payments processor Stripe and private equity firm Advent International had jointly offered more than $53 billion to buy the company. The stock, which had been trading near $47, spiked toward the low $50s on the report and record volume as investors digested the prospect of one of fintech's original disruptors swallowing the industry it helped disrupt.

According to the report, Stripe and Advent proposed $60.50 a share — a roughly 28% premium to PayPal's last unaffected close — with the two firms set to hold equal stakes were a deal to close. The offer is reportedly backed by about $50 billion in committed bank financing, and follows an initial approach in April that was quietly refined into a formal proposal earlier this month. PayPal has not issued a public response, and people close to the talks say both sides expect to spend the coming weeks working out whether there is a deal to be had at all.

The price is the fight

Size alone makes this notable. At $53 billion-plus, a completed deal would rank among the largest acquisitions in tech history, ahead of Elon Musk's $44 billion purchase of Twitter and Microsoft's $26.2 billion acquisition of LinkedIn. But the market's reaction — a 17% pop that still leaves shares below the $60.50 offer price — signals that traders think the number on the table is an opening bid, not a final one.

William Blair analyst Andrew Jeffrey said PayPal's new leadership is unlikely to embrace an offer investors view as light, and that Stripe and Advent could plausibly move closer to $70 a share if talks progress. Michael Burry, the investor best known for shorting the 2008 mortgage market, was blunter, calling the bid "simply too low" and "only an opening bid," and pegging fair value in a $75-to-$115 range with a best estimate near $100. The gap between those numbers and Wednesday's roughly $55 close is the market pricing in genuine uncertainty about whether Stripe and Advent will pay up — or walk.

A wounded giant, mid-turnaround

The timing says as much as the price. PayPal enters these talks roughly 45% below its 52-week high, having ousted chief executive Alex Chriss in February after the board concluded his three-year turnaround plan wasn't delivering. Enrique Lores, the former HP chief executive who had sat on PayPal's board since 2021, took over as president and CEO on March 1. His diagnosis, delivered over his first 90 days: PayPal had spent years over-indexing on its merchant-side business at the expense of the consumer network — Venmo, checkout, PYUSD — that once made it distinctive.

Lores has since announced $1.5 billion in cost cuts and a $6 billion buyback, and PayPal beat first-quarter estimates in May with $8.35 billion in revenue. Shares still fell that day, on investor skepticism about whether a cost-cutting plan is a growth plan. A takeover approach four months into a new CEO's tenure is an unusual vote of confidence — or lack of one, depending on how the board chooses to read it.

Why Stripe wants what PayPal has

Stripe's own position makes the logic of the deal legible even if its outcome isn't. The company was valued at $159 billion in a February employee tender offer, up from $106.7 billion the year before, and processed $1.9 trillion in payment volume in 2025, up 34% year over year. Co-founder John Collison has said repeatedly that Stripe feels no pressure to go public — "an IPO would be a solution in search of a problem," he said this year — which leaves the private, cash-generative company free to spend on acquisitions instead of listing requirements.

What Stripe lacks, and what PayPal has in abundance, is a consumer-facing brand people actively choose. Stripe dominates the merchant checkout button; it has no equivalent of Venmo, which analysts at Mizuho have called the "ultimate" peer-to-peer franchise and which has posted six straight quarters of double-digit growth in payment volume even as the rest of PayPal struggled. PayPal also brings 439 million active accounts and distribution for its PYUSD stablecoin — an asset that matters more than it might have two years ago, as payments companies race to build stablecoin rails ahead of expected regulatory clarity. A combined Stripe-PayPal would pair merchant-side dominance with consumer-side reach in a way neither company can currently claim alone.

What it signals, and what comes next

Whether or not this specific bid closes, it marks a shift in how fintech's largest players are treating each other: not as permanent rivals competing indefinitely as public companies, but as assets that can be bought outright once growth slows enough and the price is right. A financial sponsor the size of Advent, willing to commit tens of billions alongside a strategic buyer, is itself a signal that private capital sees value in a business the public market has spent two years discounting.

The immediate catalysts are PayPal's board response — expected in the coming weeks rather than days — and the company's second-quarter earnings call on July 28, which will now double as a referendum on whether Lores's standalone turnaround can outrun a $60.50-a-share floor. If the board rejects the offer as too low, as several analysts already expect, the next number to watch is whatever Stripe and Advent come back with.
