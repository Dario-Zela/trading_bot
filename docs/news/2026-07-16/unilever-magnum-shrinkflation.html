Unilever shrinks Magnum sizes as own-brand ice cream squeezes margins

A Magnum ice cream bar — the brand has cut stick and pack sizes in stages since 2022 while holding shelf prices steady. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Magnum, now trading independently as MICC after its Unilever spin-off, keeps shrinking ice cream packs as cheaper supermarket own-label tubs erode its pricing power.

Magnum's mini ice creams once came six to a box. Then five. The multipack sticks that were 110ml in 2021 are 100ml now, and the box that used to hold four dropped to three years ago, price held steady throughout. The pattern has repeated often enough that when Unilever's then-CEO Hein Schumacher was asked in 2023 whether more cuts were coming, he didn't deny it — he called smaller "entry packs" a service to "cash-strapped" consumers. That candour has aged into policy. The brand once built on gold-wrapped indulgence and near-total price indifference is now managing grams and millilitres like any other packaged-goods line under margin pressure.

What's changed since is who's doing the managing. Magnum is no longer a Unilever brand in the operational sense. Unilever demerged its entire ice cream arm on 6 December 2025, spinning it out as The Magnum Ice Cream Company (MICC), now listed in Amsterdam, London and New York with a market value of roughly $10.7bn. Unilever kept a 19.9% stake, to be sold down over five years to help cover separation costs — enough to keep the Unilever name attached to headlines about Magnum, but not enough to make Unilever the one deciding stick sizes anymore. That decision now sits with MICC chief executive Peter ter Kulve and a balance sheet no longer cushioned by Unilever's diversified earnings.

A standalone company meets a private-label problem

The timing is not kind. MICC's first full quarter as an independent company showed organic sales growth of 4.5% but revenue down 1.2% to €1.77bn once currency effects were stripped out, with pricing in Europe and Australasia actually negative, down 0.3%. Management has reaffirmed guidance for underlying EBITDA margin to improve by 40 to 60 basis points this year, but analysts remain unconvinced the business arrives at that target from a position of strength. Svetlana Menshchikova, who covers the stock, has argued the ice cream unit was "underinvested under Unilever" and still carries transitional service charges from its former parent — leaving margins behind those of Froneri, the Nestlé–PAI Partners joint venture that dominates the category from the other direction.

That other direction is the real source of the squeeze. Froneri manufactures more than 70% of the two-litre own-label ice cream sold in UK supermarkets, including ranges built to mimic Häagen-Dazs or Ben & Jerry's-style positioning at roughly 40% less shelf price. Private label, once the budget fallback, has become the driver of most unit growth in UK grocery this year. Ice cream was long considered relatively immune to trading down — an "impulse" purchase, bought on heat and mood rather than unit economics — but a shopper who has already traded down on butter and coffee is more willing to do the same at the freezer aisle.

What it signals

Shrinking the pack while holding the price is the quieter alternative to raising the price outright, and it works only as long as shoppers don't notice or don't mind. For Magnum, now standing on its own financial legs with no Unilever cushion behind it, the practice has gone from occasional cost offset to structural habit. Whether it holds is a question for MICC's full-year results and for whether private-equity interest already circling the stock — Blackstone and CD&R have both been reported as potential suitors — turns into an actual bid that changes who is making the size calls next.
