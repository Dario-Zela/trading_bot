Buffett finds no value in a market he says favours 'gambling'

Warren Buffett, chairman of Berkshire Hathaway, in 2010. — The White House / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
Warren Buffett says he can't find value stocks as Berkshire's cash pile hits a record $397bn and it keeps selling more than it buys.

Warren Buffett spent six decades teaching the market how to find value. This week he told CNBC he can't find any.

"It's tough to find values when everybody is preferring gambling," Buffett, 95, said in a July 15 interview with CNBC's Becky Quick on Squawk Box. "There's more money in cultivating gamblers than there are cultivating investors."

His complaint is not sentiment — it's arithmetic. Berkshire Hathaway, the conglomerate he now chairs after handing the chief executive role to Greg Abel on January 1, closed the first quarter of 2026 sitting on a record $397.4 billion in cash and short-term Treasury bills, up from $373 billion at the end of 2025 and past the previous high of $381.7 billion set in the third quarter of last year. Between 2022 and 2024 Berkshire was a net seller of equities to the tune of $172.9 billion, including $134.1 billion in 2024 alone. The buying, when it happens, has been narrow: additions to Delta Air Lines, Alphabet and Macy's in the first quarter, against a reduction in the total number of holdings from 42 to 29.

A poor year to sit still

The caution has a cost attached. Berkshire's stock is down 1.4% so far in 2026 against a 10.0% gain for the S&P 500, an 11.4 percentage-point gap that marks one of Buffett's weaker relative stretches. He is not chasing the shortfall. "There are times when opportunities are just thrown at you so fast you can't [keep up] ... it's unbelievable," he told Quick. "There's other times when you're very, very lucky if you find one thing in a couple of years." He's filing this stretch in the second category.

From church to casino

July's comment builds on one Buffett made at Berkshire's annual meeting in May, when he described the market as "a church with a casino attached" — people still show up to worship long-term compounding, he said, but the casino next door has gotten a lot more crowded. His target is specific: same-day options, leveraged ETFs and the retail trading apps that make both frictionless. Buying or selling a one-day option, he has said, "is not investing, it's not speculating, it's gambling." Skeptics have pointed to the same mechanics — plus the options and leverage stacked onto AI-linked stocks — as evidence that a meaningful slice of current volume is momentum-chasing rather than price discovery.

Buffett's refusal to deploy $397 billion into a market at all-time highs is itself a data point, not a prediction. He has been early before, and Berkshire under Abel has shown no sign of forcing a change of approach. The next test of his thesis arrives with Berkshire's second-quarter results, expected in early August, and with the 13F filing shortly after that will show whether the cash pile grew again — or whether Abel finally found something Buffett couldn't.
