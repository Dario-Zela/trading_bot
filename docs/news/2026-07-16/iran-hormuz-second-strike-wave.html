IRAN · HORMUZ · By C. Tanaka · 2026-07-16 · 6 min read

US launches second wave of strikes on Iran as Hormuz standoff escalates

NASA satellite image of the Strait of Hormuz, the chokepoint at the center of the escalating U.S.-Iran confrontation. — NASA / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
U.S. forces hit Iranian military sites near Hormuz in a second strike wave as a tanker blockade and Iran's own hardline rhetoric push oil and shipping risk higher.

U.S. forces began a second wave of strikes against Iran at 3 p.m. ET on Wednesday, the Pentagon said, extending a campaign that has now run five straight days and pushing the confrontation over the Strait of Hormuz toward a point where the world's most important oil chokepoint is effectively a war zone.

CENTCOM said the strikes targeted Iranian command centers, air-defense sites, missile and drone launch positions, and coastal surveillance facilities — the infrastructure Tehran has used to threaten commercial vessels transiting the strait, an 21-mile-wide waterway that carries roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil. Precision munitions hit targets including sites near Bandar Abbas and missile storage and launch positions on Greater Tunb Island, in a operation that ran roughly 90 minutes. Iran responded by claiming strikes on U.S. military infrastructure in Kuwait, Bahrain and Jordan.

A blockade turns kinetic

The second wave did not arrive in isolation. It followed the collapse, on July 8, of a memorandum of understanding that Donald Trump and Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian had signed on June 17 to wind down hostilities that began in February, when U.S. and Israeli strikes killed Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei and triggered months of tanker attacks, a shipping blockade, and the largest oil-price shock since the 1973 crisis. That truce lasted barely three weeks before Iran struck multiple commercial vessels in the strait, prompting Washington to resume both its bombing campaign and a naval blockade of Iranian ports.

That blockade turned physical this week. CENTCOM disabled the M/T Belma, a Curacao-flagged tanker, after it ignored repeated warnings while sailing empty toward Iran's Kharg Island export terminal — the first vessel forcibly stopped since the U.S. Navy resumed blockade enforcement at 4 p.m. ET Monday. A U.S. aircraft fired Hellfire missiles into the tanker's smokestack rather than sinking it; CENTCOM reported no injuries. In the enforcement window's first 24 hours, the Navy also redirected two compliant commercial vessels away from Iranian ports. Maritime traffic through the strait has fallen accordingly — just six vessels were tracked crossing in a recent 12-hour window, against 18 to 22 daily crossings earlier in the month.

The U.S. has built the force to sustain this posture. Three aircraft carrier strike groups — built around the USS Gerald R. Ford, USS George H.W. Bush and USS Abraham Lincoln — along with roughly ten destroyers, are now in or near the region, the largest concentration of American naval power in the Middle East since the 2003 Iraq invasion.

Tehran's line in the water

Iran's response has come through Mohammad Bagher Qalibaf, the parliament speaker serving as the country's top negotiator, who has been unambiguous about what Tehran considers non-negotiable. "Our armed forces have complete freedom of action, as always, to confront the enemy's aggression," Qalibaf said, adding that Iran's national security rests on preserving what he calls the "Iranian Arrangements" governing the strait — an arrangement he has separately said will let the strait "only open with Iranian Arrangements, not with American threats."

Qalibaf has also laid out the logic behind Iran continuing to talk while it fights: "Negotiation at this stage is not equivalent to compromise but, along with war, is part of the strategy of resistance and protection of national interests," he said, warning that treating negotiation and war as mutually exclusive options "is a strategic error." In a separate statement, he framed Iran's posture as one of enforced self-reliance — "we have no choice but to rely on our own strength and become strong" — and said Tehran sees no reason to honor the June memorandum if Iran itself derives no benefit from it. Taken together, the statements read less as posturing for a domestic audience than as a deliberate signal that Iran will keep contesting control of the strait by force even as diplomatic channels stay open.

Washington's mixed signals

Trump's own rhetoric has oscillated between menace and outreach in the space of days. Asked Wednesday whether he had given Iran a deadline before ordering strikes on its bridges, he said: "I don't like giving deadlines, but they pretty much know — they know the story. They better behave." He has also warned that the U.S. would begin targeting Iranian power plants and bridges and that Iran would not "have anybody left" absent a deal. At the same time, Trump has repeated a claim — first made in early July and echoed since — that Iran has reached out through back channels: "They called a little while ago, they want to make a deal so badly. I just don't know if they're worthy of making it." Three U.S. officials separately told Reuters the strikes are intended to strengthen Washington's hand for further escalation, though the officials offered no specifics on timing or scope — language consistent with a White House keeping every option visibly on the table rather than committing to one.

What the strait means for prices

The market reaction has been immediate and, so far, orderly rather than panicked — but it is moving. Brent crude climbed more than 4% as the strikes and blockade enforcement intensified this week, with September futures touching $85.92 a barrel, the highest since mid-June, before easing slightly to settle near $85. That is well off the $126 peak Brent hit in March, when Iran's initial closure of the strait triggered what analysts called the largest disruption to world energy supply since the 1970s — but it marks a fresh leg higher after prices had spent June drifting back toward pre-war levels on hopes the memorandum would hold.

Shipping markets are pricing the risk more starkly than crude is. War-risk insurance premiums on vessels transiting the strait have risen to between 2% and 6% of a ship's value, up from a fraction of a percent before the conflict, according to marine underwriters — a direct tax on every barrel and container that still moves through the waterway. Container freight rates have jumped in tandem, with spot rates to the U.S. West Coast reaching $7,400 per 40-foot container and nearly $9,000 to the East Coast, as carriers reroute around the Gulf and pass the cost through. Tanker rates, by contrast, have stayed comparatively steady, reflecting how few vessels are still attempting the transit at all — traffic has thinned to roughly a quarter of its pre-crisis pace, which is itself a form of price signal: the safest cargo, for now, is the cargo that doesn't sail.

The next marker to watch is less a date than a threshold: whether Iran responds to the second strike wave with another attack on shipping, which would test whether the blockade-and-strike strategy can hold traffic near zero without hardening into a permanent closure, or whether Qalibaf's talk of "Iranian Arrangements" survives contact with the destroyers now massed outside the strait.
