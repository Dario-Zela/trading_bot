Senior US defense officials said to be weighing Cuba military options

Leeward Airfield at Naval Station Guantanamo Bay, the US base at the center of rising tension with Havana — U.S. Navy photo by Chief Mass Communication Specialist Bill Mesta / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Pentagon officials reviewed Cuba military options, including a 101st Airborne air assault plan, but stress no decision has been made and Iran is the priority.

Senior US defense officials have spent recent weeks examining military options against Cuba, including a plan for an Army-led air assault involving thousands of soldiers from the 101st Airborne Division, according to a CBS News report citing officials granted anonymity to discuss national security planning. The options were laid out in a concept-of-operations briefing held in late June — the kind of early-stage contingency planning the Pentagon routinely runs for a range of scenarios, weighing troop numbers, logistics and risk long before any decision to act.

Officials were emphatic on one point: none of this means Donald Trump or the Pentagon has decided to do anything. It is planning, not policy. And practically, it isn't going anywhere soon — officials told CBS that aircraft, intelligence assets and other resources have already been redirected toward the Middle East to sustain the reopened air campaign against Iran, making a Cuba operation unlikely "at the moment."

Why Cuba, why now

The backdrop makes the leak legible even if the leak itself is thin on specifics. Washington has spent 2026 tightening the vise on Havana: an executive order in January designated Cuba an "unusual and extraordinary threat" and slapped tariffs on any country supplying it oil, part of a blockade that US officials say has cut Cuba's energy imports by 80 to 90 percent and helped trigger a full collapse of the island's power grid in March. The Justice Department indicted 95-year-old former leader Raúl Castro in May over a 1996 aircraft shootdown. CIA Director John Ratcliffe flew to Havana that same month with an ultimatum — political reform in exchange for sanctions relief, on a clock the administration says won't stay open indefinitely.

The immediate security concern is Guantanamo. US officials say Cuba has acquired more than 300 attack drones since 2023 with Russian and Iranian backing, and that the island hosts Russia's largest overseas signals-intelligence facility. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, visiting the naval base on June 10, warned that any Cuban weapon capable of reaching Guantanamo "would be inviting the kind of confrontation not only do they not want, but they could not stand." Secretary of State Marco Rubio followed on July 11 with a statement accusing Havana's "corrupt elites" of clinging to a "morally bankrupt Marxist ideology."

A story about posture, not policy

None of that amounts to a timeline, a troop deployment, or a market signal. Contingency planning for a base as sensitive as Guantanamo is standard practice, not a leading indicator, and the same officials who described the briefing were careful to note Cuba isn't the priority while Iran operations are running. For now this reads as pressure — another lever alongside the oil blockade and sanctions — rather than a step toward action. The next marker to watch is whether resources start moving toward the Caribbean once, or if, the Iran campaign winds down.
