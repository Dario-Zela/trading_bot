Fed's Beige Book shows job growth cooling, price gains slowing across districts

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C., headquarters of the central bank that compiles the Beige Book from its twelve regional districts. — AgnosticPreachersKid / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
The Fed's Beige Book shows hiring cooling unevenly and price growth slowing in all 12 districts, but fuel-cost uncertainty tied to the Middle East keeps the inflation fight unresolved.

The Federal Reserve's latest Beige Book, released Wednesday, describes an economy that is still growing but no longer generating much new work — and one where the price of fuel keeps threatening to undo whatever progress has been made on inflation. Taken together, the twelve regional reports paint a picture familiar to anyone who has watched this cycle grind on: activity that refuses to stall, hiring that refuses to accelerate, and a geopolitical wildcard sitting under the price data like a lit fuse.

"Economic activity increased at a slight to moderate pace in eleven of twelve Federal Reserve Districts," the report states, with San Francisco the lone district reporting no change at all. That is, on its face, a reasonably healthy reading. But the employment section tells a more uneven story, and it's the one likely to draw the most attention from traders parsing the report for clues about the Fed's next move.

A patchy labor market

Only five of the twelve districts — Boston, New York, Richmond, Dallas and Minneapolis among the stronger performers in recent cycles — reported employment gains that qualified as modest, moderate or solid. The other seven described conditions as flat. That's an improvement on the prior Beige Book, in which just one district reported meaningful hiring growth, but it still leaves the labor market underperforming the broader economy: activity is expanding in eleven of twelve regions, hiring is doing so in fewer than half.

Contacts across districts pointed to a familiar culprit: skilled labor, particularly technicians and tradespeople, remains scarce even as broader hiring cools, a mismatch that has kept wage pressure alive in pockets of the country even as headline job creation slows. The Fed's business contacts, per the report, "generally expected the economy to continue to expand in the coming months" — not a recession call, but not a hiring boom either. It reads as an economy coasting rather than climbing.

Price growth breaks stride

The more encouraging thread, and the one likely to matter most for rate-cut positioning, is on prices. "Prices increased moderately overall, with nine Districts reporting moderate growth, two robust growth, and one slight growth," the Beige Book found — and, notably, price growth was the same or slower in every single district compared with the prior reporting period. No region reported acceleration. That's a meaningful break from earlier in this cycle, when Middle East-linked cost pressures were showing up as "robust" or even outright acceleration in multiple districts at once.

St. Louis was the outlier on the high side, still reporting a robust and widespread pace of increases, while Chicago and San Francisco described more moderate pass-through as firms wrestled with how much of their higher input costs they could actually charge customers. The broader signal — deceleration everywhere, acceleration nowhere — is the kind of uniform pattern the Fed likes to see before it feels comfortable easing further.

The fuel-cost wildcard

What keeps this from reading as an unambiguous win for the disinflation case is fuel. "Several Districts noted elevated uncertainty in the outlook for fuel costs," the report says, and contacts across regions flagged high fuel and transportation charges as a drag on consumer spending even where other categories held up. Some tied rising input costs to tariffs; many more pointed directly at the renewed Middle East conflict, which has kept oil markets on edge for weeks.

That's not an abstract worry. A brief U.S.-Iran ceasefire in June had pushed fuel prices down and helped cool the price data; the second wave of U.S. strikes and the resulting standoff over the Strait of Hormuz have since reversed some of that relief, pushing energy costs — and, contacts say, prices for petrochemical inputs like resins and adhesives — back up. It is the one variable in this report that isn't purely domestic, and the one the Fed has the least ability to manage with interest rates alone.

What it means for the Fed

The combination — activity holding up, hiring cooling unevenly, and price growth decelerating almost everywhere except where energy costs bite — is close to the textbook definition of a soft landing that hasn't quite been secured. Futures markets currently assign roughly 79.5% odds that the Federal Open Market Committee, under new Chair Kevin Warsh, holds the federal funds rate at 3.50%-3.75% when it meets July 28-29, having already found reason for caution in the June Beige Book's fuel-driven price pressure.

That leaves this report as more confirmation than surprise: the disinflation trend is real and broad-based, but it remains hostage to a conflict thousands of miles from any of the twelve districts filing these reports. The next Beige Book, due in early September, will show whether the Hormuz standoff further denting the fuel outlook, or the ceasefire logic reasserting itself, is the story the Fed ends up having to answer to.
