New Zealand coalition one seat short; BBC links German boat dealer to Channel gangs

In one sentence
A BBC headline says New Zealand's coalition is one seat short of a majority, so talks may decide the result, while a second BBC report links a German dealer to Channel gangs.

New Zealand's governing coalition has been reported as one seat short of a parliamentary majority, leaving the next few weeks to be settled by negotiation rather than by the ballot box. The detail comes from a BBC World headline. The seat totals, the parties' names and the date of the count were not available to this desk, and the figures should be read with that in mind.

A count that kept moving

The context is a general election that was expected to be among the tightest in years. Early counts on election night, 6 November, were reported as too close to call. One summary put Christopher Luxon's National Party, together with ACT and NZ First, at 67 seats at one point. Another put the Labour-led opposition bloc at 56. Both figures shifted as counting went on. One report described a 129-seat Parliament with 65 seats needed for a majority.

Those early numbers do not square neatly with a one-seat shortfall, and the gap is the reason for caution. A one-seat shortfall may reflect later counting, a change in how the House is sized, or simply a loose headline. The Electoral Commission's official results are the figure to rely on.

Special votes are the main source of movement. These are ballots cast outside a voter's home electorate, or by people enrolled late, and they are counted after election night. Coverage of this election noted that they have historically added seats for the left at the expense of the right-leaning bloc. A margin of one seat can therefore change hands after the preliminary count.

What a one-seat gap means

Under New Zealand's proportional system, a government needs the confidence of a majority of the House. A coalition one seat short can still govern if a smaller party or an independent agrees to support it on confidence and supply. That gives whoever holds the swing seat unusual leverage over policy and portfolios.

New Zealand has done this before. The 2017 election also ended in stalemate, and it took weeks of talks before a government was formed. The 2023 coalition talks that followed Luxon's win also ran for several weeks. A shortfall this small points to a similar slow, close negotiation, though the available material does not show which parties hold the balance.

Separately: a German boat supplier

The BBC also has a report on the supply of small boats to the Channel smuggling gangs. The claim is that a German boat dealer is suspected of supplying them. "Suspected" means no charge or finding has been established, and the BBC's own article was not available to this desk. The dealer, the evidence and any official response therefore cannot be set out here.

The claim fits earlier reporting. A BBC investigation in 2024 found Germany to be a significant hub for the trade. Undercover reporters in Essen were offered a boat package for about €15,000. The UK's National Crime Agency told the BBC that Germany is a central place for storing boats and engines. Berlin's interior ministry replied that no direct smuggling takes place from Germany, since it does not border the UK. Later joint operations between French, British and German police seized dozens of boats and engines. In March 2026, four people were arrested in North Rhine-Westphalia in raids on a smuggling network. A dpa report put the share of smugglers' boats and motors sourced in Germany at about 90 per cent, which is a press-agency estimate and not an official figure.

What to watch

For New Zealand, the next marker is the Electoral Commission's final count including special votes, which will show whether the shortfall is real and which party holds the deciding seat. For the boat story, the test is whether German prosecutors act on the BBC's findings against a named dealer.
