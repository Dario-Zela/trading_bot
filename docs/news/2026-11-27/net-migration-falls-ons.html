Net migration falls to its lowest level since 1997, excluding the pandemic

In one sentence
ONS figures show net migration fell to about 77,000 in the year to June 2026, the lowest outside the pandemic since 1997, but a large outflow of Britons clouds the claim of control.

Net migration to the UK fell to about 77,000 in the twelve months to June 2026, the lowest level outside the pandemic years since 1997, according to figures from the Office for National Statistics published on 26 November. The previous year's figure was revised to 204,000, so the latest estimate is down by more than half in a single year. It gives the government a number it can point to as evidence that it has taken back control of the borders. Whether that claim holds depends on what is driving the fall.

The number and where it comes from

The ONS attributes the decline mainly to fewer people arriving from outside the EU to work or study. That fits a run of policy changes in recent years: tighter rules on dependants of students and care workers, higher salary thresholds for skilled-worker visas, and a general chilling of demand from overseas applicants. Net migration is the difference between people arriving and people leaving, so it can fall because fewer people come, because more go, or both.

The second half of that sum is where the figures get awkward. The ONS estimates that 245,000 British nationals left the UK in the year to June 2026, against 109,000 who moved in. That is a net outflow of British citizens of roughly 136,000 — a large swing in the data, and one the statisticians themselves treat with caution. A Department for Work and Pensions dataset used to estimate it was delayed, and the ONS says it expects to revise the figures in May 2027.

That caveat matters because the headline total is a balance. A bigger-than-expected outflow of Britons pulls the net figure down just as surely as a fall in visa arrivals does, though the two carry very different political meanings. Critics on the right have already seized on it. The National Pulse put it bluntly, arguing that the figure is low "only because Britons are fleeing en masse". The ONS data do not say why the departures rose, and the estimate is among the least certain parts of the release.

Claiming credit

The Home Secretary, Shabana Mahmood, said: "We have brought net migration to 77,000, down 92% from its peak under the Conservatives." The Conservatives, unsurprisingly, dispute the credit. Chris Philp, the shadow home secretary, said the fall is mainly the result of changes made by the previous Conservative government, whose visa restrictions began to bite in 2024.

Both arguments have some support. The peak the government cites was the post-pandemic surge, which followed the post-Brexit points-based system that the Conservatives introduced. The restrictions that followed came from both administrations. Full Fact, which has tracked the claims, has noted that provisional figures are subject to revision and that timing makes attribution difficult. A policy announced under one government can take twelve to eighteen months to show in the data, by which time another is in office.

Andy Burnham has taken a more careful line. In an ITV interview he cited falling boat crossings and lower net migration as evidence of "a much better control" of migration, while declining to say whether immigration is too high. Reports suggest he would keep Mahmood's hard-line approach if he became prime minister, with an ally arguing that the public must trust that its elected representatives control the borders before migration can be discussed positively. I could not find the exact wording the triage notes attribute to him on migration being under "sufficient" control, so that phrase is not quoted here.

What the fall changes

The significance extends beyond the political contest. Net migration feeds directly into official assumptions about population, labour supply, housing demand and tax receipts. The Office for Budget Responsibility had already projected that the figure would fall to its lowest level since 1997 excluding the pandemic, so the outturn is not a surprise to the forecasters. But a lower baseline means a smaller workforce than earlier forecasts implied, with consequences for the growth and tax assumptions that underpin fiscal plans. Employers in social care, hospitality and higher education, which relied heavily on overseas recruitment, have been among those most exposed. The squeeze on household budgets elsewhere in the edition, including the effect of higher mortgage costs, shows how closely these pressures interact.

The argument over "control" is also partly a dispute about what counts as success. A figure near 77,000 would be a long way below the levels that dominated debate in 2022 and 2023. But if the fall is driven partly by emigration among British citizens, or by a shrinking pool of students and skilled workers that employers say they need, ministers will be asked whether the lower number is what they wanted.

The next test is the revision. The ONS plans to update its estimates of British-national migration in May 2027, and a materially different outflow would change the headline. Until then, the figure stands as the government's strongest piece of evidence, and the opposition's strongest argument that it did not earn it.
