Mortgage holders face about £100 more a month as gilts stay near 5% after the BoE hike

In one sentence
The BoE raised Bank Rate to 4% on an 8–1 vote; gilt moves and the £100 a month mortgage rise rest on thinner, partly unverified evidence.

The Bank of England raised Bank Rate by a quarter of a point to 4% on 5 November. The gilt market's reaction, on the evidence available, was muted rather than defiant. For households with a mortgage the arithmetic points the same way. The Bank's own estimates, and a headline carried by BBC Business, put the average rise for borrowers coming off cheap fixed deals at roughly £100 a month. Two of the figures behind the story could not be confirmed, and the piece flags them below.

The decision

The Monetary Policy Committee voted 8–1 to lift Bank Rate to 4%, according to the Bank's November summary and minutes. The lone dissenter wanted a larger move, 0.5 points to 4.25%. Some secondary sources give different splits, including 9–0, and one describes a five-four hold. The 5–4 description matches the November 2025 meeting, when rates were held at 4%. The official minutes are the authoritative record, and this piece uses 8–1.

A committee that votes 8–1, with its only dissent on the hawkish side, is not signalling comfort. The direction of the dissent matters more than the size of the majority, because it shows where the debate sits: on whether 25 basis points was enough, not on whether to move.

The gilt market

The triage premise was that yields have stayed near 5% despite the hike. That is not established. A Dow Jones report on the day said sterling rose and gilt yields trimmed earlier losses after the decision. That is consistent with a market that had largely priced the move. I found no sourced yield figures by maturity or date, and the "near 5%" claim comes from unverified search snippets with no tenor attached. Ten-year and 30-year gilts can differ by half a point or more, so "near 5%" could describe either, or neither.

What can be said is narrower. The Bank moved short rates, and the reported market response was a partial retracement, not a rally or a rout. Whether long yields have stayed elevated since is a question for the closing prices of the last three weeks, and those were not verified here.

The mortgage bill

The household end of the story is better documented in outline than in detail. The Bank of England has repeatedly estimated that borrowers whose fixed deals expire will see average monthly payments rise by about £100. Its earlier estimate was £107 a month, and other reports cite different averages and different groups of households. Which? has put the typical remortgage rise at £146, with around 420,000 households facing more than £500 a month. A later Bank figure was lower, at £45 for those rolling off within two years. The BBC's "£100 a month" headline fits the range, but its exact basis and date could not be checked.

The mechanism is simple. A borrower who fixed at 2 to 3% in 2021 or 2022 and refinances at a rate several points higher faces a payment rise regardless of what Bank Rate does this week. A hike adds to that gap only to the extent that it pushes swap rates, which price fixed deals, higher. Swap rates follow the expected path of Bank Rate and gilt yields more than the base rate itself. That is why the gilt question and the mortgage question are really one question.

The pressure also reaches beyond household budgets. Higher payments squeeze discretionary spending, which is the usual route by which mortgage stress shows up in UK consumer-facing companies. No firm-level data was available for this piece.

What to watch

Two things would settle the open points. The first is the next MPC decision on 17 December, with its vote split. A second dissent for a larger move, or the first for a pause, would show which way the committee is leaning. The second is the path of 10-year gilt yields and two- and five-year swap rates, which feed directly into fixed-rate pricing. If those stay high after the hike, the £100 average will be an underestimate for those remortgaging next year. If they ease, the hike will have cost borrowers less than the headline implies.
