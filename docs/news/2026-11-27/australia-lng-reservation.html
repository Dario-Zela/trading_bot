Australia orders LNG gas reserved for home use, putting Shell and European peers in focus

In one sentence
Australia's gas reservation bill, which would make east-coast LNG exporters supply up to 20% of volumes at home from 2028, awaits the Senate, with Shell among the operators affected.

Australia is not so much telling LNG exporters to hold gas back as writing a legal obligation to sell part of it at home. A bill that would require east-coast liquefied natural gas exporters to supply up to 20% of their export volumes to the domestic market has passed the House of Representatives. A Senate committee backed it on 11 November. The aim is to head off a forecast shortage in the eastern states. For Shell, the most prominent European name among the affected operators, the question is how much gas it would have to redirect and at what price.

What the bill does

The scheme has changed shape several times this year. The May 2026 framework set a flat 20% reservation from 1 July 2027. The September exposure draft, as summarised by the law firm Allens, softened it. The Minister would set the obligation at "up to 20%", and the scheme would target aggregate domestic supply of 110% of forecast demand. Domestic obligations would start on 1 January 2028, six months later than first proposed. The earlier "release valve", which let exporters ship surplus reserved volumes abroad, was removed.

The enforcement mechanism is the export licence. Exporters must actually supply gas, generally under a binding contract or through a net contribution to facilitated markets, rather than merely offer it. An exporter meeting 90% of its obligation can defer the shortfall across later regulatory periods. The Minister can suspend or cancel a licence for non-compliance, without compensation.

Relief is available for existing long-term LNG contracts, infrastructure limits and existing reservation regimes. None is automatic. Western Australia, which already requires 15% over a project's life, has been promised an exemption, according to its premier on 18 November. The Northern Territory is largely exempt. The practical target is the three Queensland export projects, run by Origin Energy, Shell and Santos.

Shell's position

Shell operates QCLNG on Curtis Island, with 8.5 million tonnes a year of capacity across two trains. Train 1 is split 50-50 with CNOOC, and Shell holds 97.5% of Train 2. It also invests in Arrow Energy's Surat Gas Project, a 50-50 venture with PetroChina, to keep QCLNG supplied.

QCLNG already sells into the domestic market, which makes it less exposed than Santos' Gladstone LNG. That project has no spare gas and does not supply the east coast, according to coverage of the Senate report. Its partners include TotalEnergies, Petronas and Korea Gas. TotalEnergies is therefore the other European major with a stake in the outcome.

Shell's public stance is critical of the design rather than the idea. Shell Australia's chair, Cecile Wake, has said she does not oppose a domestic reserve but sees significant flaws in the current policy. She has warned that mandatory sales obligations would "absolutely destroy" investment signals. Reports on Shell's stance are not wholly consistent. One May account listed Shell among supporters of a reservation policy, and the later remarks suggest conditional rather than blanket support.

No source reviewed gives the volume QCLNG would have to redirect, or a figure for Shell's earnings exposure. Those numbers would have to come from company filings and the regulator's yet-to-be-published baselines. This article does not estimate them.

The global supply question

The reservation does not remove Australian LNG from the market. It narrows the margin. Australia is a major exporter, and buyers in Japan, South Korea and China, and the European buyers competing with them, count on its cargoes. Gas pushed into the domestic market is gas that does not sail, though the scheme's own logic is that forecast demand, not a fixed share, sets the obligation. If domestic demand is met, the call on exporters shrinks.

Exporters make the opposite case. Ryan Stokes, chair of Beach Energy, told the committee process that "forcing exporters to oversupply the domestic market undercuts the companies that have always supplied it." Producers argue a domestic glut would depress prices enough to make new exploration uneconomic, which would reduce future supply.

The competition regulator has warned that the eastern states could face shortfalls from 2027, as Bass Strait output declines. That timetable is the government's case for acting. Because the start date has slipped to 2028, any tightening of seaborne supply is a story for later years rather than this winter.

What to watch

The bill now goes to the Senate, where the Greens and the Coalition both object to parts of its design, so the final text is not settled. After that come the Australian Energy Regulator's baselines, additionality tests and financial-assurance rules, none yet released. Those instruments will decide how much gas each exporter must place, and so how far the headline 20% reaches Shell's and TotalEnergies' accounts. The first licence decisions are due during 2027, ahead of the 1 January 2028 obligations.
