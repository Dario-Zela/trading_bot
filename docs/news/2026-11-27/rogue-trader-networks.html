From a £250 job to a £9,000 bill: inside the rogue trader networks

In one sentence
A BBC investigation says London rogue plumbers and electricians run recruited, profit-sharing networks, citing a £9,153 bill for a job an expert said should cost £250.

The headline figures are easy to misread. In the BBC's account, £250 is not what a householder was quoted. It is what an independent electrician said an outdoor socket should have cost. The bill that followed was £9,153. The investigation, published on 27 November, argues that this is no freak overcharge. It says the pattern repeats across a network of plumbers and electricians operating in London.

The account below rests on the BBC's findings as relayed by secondary outlets. The original report was not accessed directly.

The £9,153 socket

Clive, who is undergoing cancer treatment, called out an engineer to fit an outdoor socket for garden lights he already owned. The BBC reports the job took about two hours. The bill was £9,153. A freelance electrical specialist told the BBC the work had been done incorrectly and left unsafe, and should have cost no more than £250. Clive said he felt pressured into paying and no longer feels safe at home.

A second case follows the same arc, with a smaller final figure. Tom was quoted £49 on the phone for plumbing work, then billed £6,237.60 by Ibby & Co. Ltd, a firm he had never heard of. He says the plumber left taps running, which created a sense of panic. An independent plumber put a fair price at no more than £1,000. After Tom complained, he received threats from an unknown man. The BBC says it recorded and heard these.

The bait is a cheap phone price or a vague promise. The switch happens once a stranger is in the house.

A network, or a trade?

The organised-network claim rests on one name and a recruitment pitch. The BBC links Nessar Khawaja, 23, who posts on TikTok as ThatGuyNess, to the operation. His videos boast of earning thousands from "desperate" customers and of recruiting others. In one he says: "Me and my team, we're top of the food chain."

An associate of Khawaja told an undercover BBC journalist that engineers must hand over 60% of each job's takings. Insiders say recruits are told to inflate prices, especially where children or pets are present, and to mime lock-picking before drilling. A former plumber, Lee, spent a year working for Khawaja before leaving over his conscience. He called the operation "giants" next to established plumbing firms.

Khawaja denies wrongdoing and told the BBC the posts were light-hearted comedy. He is listed as director of two dissolved companies at the same address as Chriss Electrician Ltd, which did not respond to requests for comment. He denies any connection to it. The BBC said it had not seen evidence that he controls or runs the company. TikTok banned his account for breaching its fraud and scams rules after the BBC approached it.

That leaves a gap. A 60% cut, a shared sales script and a recruitment drive describe a franchise-like model. But the evidence in the coverage ties the pieces together mainly through one man's social media and one insider's recollection. Whether the Clive and Tom jobs sit inside the same structure is something the reporting suggests rather than proves. The coverage also mentions no police statement, investigation or action.

Search results and the bank refund

The operators are said to depend on search traffic. They either pay for prominent listings or use names that closely resemble reputable firms. The BBC asked Google why rogue traders could advertise on its platform. The company said it has strict ads policies but did not answer the specific questions, according to one summary of the report.

Trading Standards told the BBC the average call-out scam now costs the victim about £10,000. The Clive and Tom bills sit at or below that figure.

The one clear remedy so far came from a bank. After the BBC made contact, Clive's bank refunded the full amount as a "gesture of goodwill". Goodwill is not a right, and most victims will not have a BBC producer ringing on their behalf.

What to watch

Three things would settle the "network" question: any response from police or Trading Standards to the evidence the BBC gathered, any action by Google on the advertisers involved, and whether Companies House or the courts take up the firms named. Until then, the story is a documented pattern of extreme overcharging, with an organised structure that is plausible but only partly evidenced.
