GSK's Exdensur beats Nucala in EGPA study; Merck and Lilly face pipeline questions

In one sentence
A single Zacks item says GSK's twice-yearly Exdensur beat monthly Nucala in an EGPA study, but it gives no trial data and the claim is unverified.

GSK's newest asthma biologic is reported to have beaten the company's own older drug in a study of a rare inflammatory disease. The result, if it holds, is mainly about how often patients are injected. A single market-commentary item from Zacks says that Exdensur, dosed twice a year, did better than Nucala, dosed monthly, in eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA). That item gives no trial figures, and no GSK statement or trial publication was checked for this article. Everything below about the study should be read with that caveat.

What is claimed, and what is not

EGPA is a rare condition in which small blood vessels become inflamed, typically in people who also have asthma and raised eosinophil counts. Eosinophils are a type of white blood cell. Nucala (mepolizumab) targets interleukin-5, a signalling protein that drives those cells, and is already used in EGPA on a monthly schedule. Exdensur (depemokimab) works on the same pathway but is built to act for longer, which is the basis for the twice-yearly schedule.[^1]

The Zacks item says the newer drug outperformed the older one. It does not say what "outperformed" means. It gives no primary endpoint, such as remission rates or reduction in steroid use. It gives no sample size, effect size, confidence interval or safety comparison. It does not say whether the study was designed to show superiority or only that the newer drug was no worse (non-inferiority). Those distinctions decide whether the result is a clinical finding or a convenience finding.

The framing suggests the latter. A biologic given twice a year instead of twelve times is easier on patients and clinics, and adherence tends to improve when injections are less frequent. This is an inference from the dosing schedules. It is not something the source states, and efficacy and safety data would have to support it.

An incumbent undercut from within

GSK owns both products, so a switch from Nucala to Exdensur would move revenue within the company rather than hand it to a rival. That is a familiar lifecycle tactic in biologics: replace an ageing product with a longer-acting successor before competition arrives. Whether it works depends on regulatory filings, label wording and payer coverage. None of those is described in the source.

Merck and Lilly: a comparison without news

The same Zacks material compares Merck and Lilly. The second angle is a stock comparison, not a development, and the points it makes are not new. The following are from memory and could not be checked against live sources:

  • Merck's Gardasil vaccine has been hampered by weakness in China. Shipments there were reportedly paused in 2025 after a distributor-ordering problem.
  • Merck's larger exposure is Keytruda, whose US exclusivity is generally understood to run to about 2028. That is a different horizon from the 2027 date implied for Gardasil.
  • Lilly's oncology business, which includes the BTK inhibitor Jaypirca (pirtobrutinib), is small next to its obesity and diabetes franchise built on tirzepatide (Mounjaro and Zepbound).

These are background framing, not findings. Neither company has an event attached to this item, and no figures are offered.

What would settle it

The claim can be tested against a GSK press release or a peer-reviewed publication or conference presentation of the EGPA data. The things to check are the stated endpoints, whether the comparison was head-to-head or indirect, and the safety record over a full dosing cycle. Until then, the result is a single-source report with no numbers attached.

[^1]: Drug mechanisms and dosing schedules are described from general knowledge of the products and were not re-verified against GSK prescribing information for this article.
