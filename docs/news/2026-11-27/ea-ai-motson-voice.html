EA adds AI-generated John Motson commentary to FC 27

In one sentence
EA's AI-recreated John Motson commentary in FC 27 has his family's blessing and payment, but campaigners say relatives cannot consent for a dead performer.

Football video games have always borrowed the voices of the men who called the real thing. EA is now borrowing one from a man who can no longer be asked. From a title update on Thursday 26 November, EA Sports FC 27 offers John Motson, the BBC commentator who died in February 2023 aged 77, as an optional commentary choice. His voice is partly rebuilt with artificial intelligence.

The family says it is delighted. A campaign group for voice performers says that is not the same as consent. The row is a small, early test of a question that will recur: when a performer dies, who owns the sound of their voice?

The voice in the box

Motson was the sound of English football for a generation. His career ran from the early 1970s to the late 2010s and took in 29 FA Cup finals, 10 World Cups, 10 European Championships and more than 2,500 matches, according to Extra.ie. He was the lead voice of the FIFA series from the mid-1990s to the mid-2000s, and made a brief return in FIFA 19.

EA says it built the new version from almost 10 years of in-game recordings plus "new AI tools". The old audio could not do the job alone. IBTimes UK reports that EA found it lacked commentary on modern players such as Bradley Barcola and Chloe Kelly. The AI fills the gaps, and the result mixes his original sessions with generated material.

It is, by IBTimes UK's account, the first time the franchise has featured a deceased commentator.

This is not EA's first use of synthetic commentary. Earlier this year, Guy Mowbray confirmed he had let EA use an AI version of his voice to read some of the 20,000-odd player names in FC 26. EA described the arrangement as a collaboration. Mowbray, who is alive and in the studio when he chooses to be, signed off. Motson cannot.

Consent by proxy

EA says it worked with Motson's widow and his son, Fred, and paid the family for permission to use his likeness and recreate his commentary. The sum was not disclosed. Mat Prior, EA's senior game design director for the series, is reported to have said the company would not have gone ahead without the family's approval.

Fred Motson has been warm in public. "My father adored being the voice of the game," he said, "and we are so grateful to the team at EA Sports FC for this touching tribute." He has also recalled growing up with FIFA and pestering his parents for the latest console.

The objection is structural rather than personal. Peter Caulfield of Save Our Voices Now, a campaign on voice cloning, is paraphrased by IBTimes UK as arguing that Motson could not consent himself and that permission should come from the individual, not relatives. PC Gamer reports that some fans have called the feature "gross" and "bleak".

The two positions are not as far apart as they sound. Nobody doubts that the family acted in good faith. The question is what the law and industry practice say they are entitled to sell. In the UK there is no general personality right that passes to heirs. Estates rely on a patchwork of contract, copyright in recordings and passing off. A family deal is therefore the practical way to clear a recreated voice, and also a precedent that other publishers, with other estates, may not handle as carefully.

There is a second distinction the family's blessing does not settle. Archive recordings Motson made for EA are one thing. New sentences about players he never saw, in his cadence, are another: he never said them, and he never agreed to a machine saying them.

What current commentators think

The working commentators are not, on the available evidence, up in arms. Mowbray is described as seeing a relatively positive use of AI that lets Motson's legacy continue. Derek Rae has reportedly said AI can handle routine surnames and simple lines but that expressive commentary still needs human performances. EA says AI will not replace its human commentators.

That reassurance is worth testing. Surnames and stock phrases are the cheapest part of a commentator's job to automate, and they are also a large share of the studio hours. A technology introduced as a way to cover the long tail of player names has now been used for a full recreation of a dead voice. The step between those two is shorter than the marketing suggests.

What to watch

The immediate test is reception once the update is live: whether players treat Motson as an optional curiosity or a draw, and whether EA repeats the exercise with other retired voices. The longer test is legal and contractual. Performers' unions and campaigners are pressing for explicit, individual consent for digital replicas, ideally given in life. Until that is standard, each deal like this one will be judged on the goodwill of the family involved, and on the terms, which remain undisclosed.

This article draws on published reports; EA's own announcement and the BBC Technology report that first covered the story were not independently checked here.
