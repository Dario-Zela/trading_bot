Huawei found guilty of stealing US trade secrets but acquitted of racketeering

In one sentence
A Brooklyn jury convicted Huawei on 10 of 12 counts, including trade secret conspiracy, but acquitted it of racketeering, narrowing the US government's original case.

A federal jury in Brooklyn has convicted Huawei Technologies of conspiring to steal trade secrets from American companies. It also convicted the company of fraud and sanctions-related charges, but it cleared Huawei of the racketeering conspiracy charge that sat at the centre of the government's case. The verdict came on 24 November in the largest US criminal prosecution of a Chinese technology company. Prosecutors won a conviction on most counts and lost the one that framed the whole company as a criminal enterprise.

What the jury decided

Huawei and three affiliates, Huawei Device, Futurewei Technologies and Huawei Device USA, went to trial in the Eastern District of New York. Reports of the verdict say Huawei was convicted on 10 of the 12 counts it faced. They include conspiracy to steal trade secrets, wire fraud, bank fraud, money-laundering conspiracy and violations of US sanctions on Iran. Several of those counts relate to the allegation that Huawei misled banks about its business in Iran.

The jury acquitted on racketeering conspiracy, the charge that alleged Huawei operated as a criminal enterprise. Reports also say it cleared the company of conspiracy to obstruct justice, though outlets differ on which defendants each acquittal covered. Reports describe the three affiliates as convicted on the trade secret and wire fraud conspiracy counts, and some say Futurewei and Huawei Device USA were also cleared of racketeering. Court records would settle those details.

The US case was first brought in 2019, when prosecutors indicted Huawei and its American subsidiaries on charges that included racketeering conspiracy, bank fraud and trade secret theft. Racketeering is the heavier tool. It lets prosecutors present a pattern of conduct over many years as one enterprise, and it is a statute normally associated with organised crime. The jury declined to accept that framing, even as it accepted much of the underlying conduct.

Two readings of the result

Both sides claimed something from the outcome. Huawei welcomed the racketeering acquittal and said it vindicated a position the company has held for eight years. It called the remaining charges "equally without legal or factual merit". It also argued that the five trade secret disputes cited at trial were civil matters resolved long ago, and that "no court ever found Huawei liable for willful intellectual property misappropriation." The company said it would review the verdicts and take legal action to protect its rights. Its lawyer, Brian Heberlig, reportedly called the acquittal a "profound rejection" of the prosecution's story and said an appeal is expected.

The Justice Department emphasised the convictions. Tysen Duva, assistant attorney general for the criminal division, said: "For over two decades, Huawei built its global empire on a foundation of fraud, theft, and deception." Attorney General Todd Blanche said no person or company is above the law. US Attorney Joseph Nocella Jr. called the verdicts a step toward accountability.

The split matters in practice. A conviction on 10 counts is a court finding against a major Chinese tech company, which the US can cite in its arguments about telecom security and export controls. But the acquittal narrows the theory the government pursued for years. The jury appears to have accepted discrete acts of fraud and theft while rejecting the idea that they added up to a single criminal enterprise run from the top. That is an inference from the pattern of verdicts. The jury gave no reasons.

What comes next

Sentencing is reportedly set for 14 September 2027. Corporate defendants cannot be jailed, so the exposure is financial: possible criminal fines, forfeiture and restitution. The size of any penalty will be a closely watched number, as will how much the racketeering acquittal shapes the judge's view of the scale of the conduct.

An appeal is the other event to watch. Huawei's argument that the trade secret disputes were settled civil matters is likely to feature, along with any challenge to how the sanctions and bank fraud counts were put to the jury. Neither side has yet shown how it will handle those points on appeal. Until then, the case is a conviction for the US government, but not the one it first set out to win.
