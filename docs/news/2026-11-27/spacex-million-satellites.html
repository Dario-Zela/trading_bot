Regulator approves Musk's plan for a million SpaceX data satellites

In one sentence
The FCC has conditionally approved up to one million SpaceX orbital AI data-centre satellites, but has not yet signed off on the debris plan that critics say matters most.

A US regulator has said yes to what would be the largest satellite constellation ever proposed, and the thing it would put in orbit is not broadband but computing. On Wednesday the Federal Communications Commission gave SpaceX conditional approval to deploy and operate up to one million satellites, called Starmind, to host AI data centres in low Earth orbit. The approval is real, but it is a licence to start, not a finished plan. The FCC has not yet signed off on how SpaceX will deal with the debris.

What was approved

The authorisation covers a constellation of up to 1 million satellites at altitudes of roughly 500 to 1,002 kilometres, according to TechCrunch's reading of the filing. The satellites would talk mainly to one another, and to Starlink, by laser. The FCC also authorised radio links as a backup, so operators can keep contact and control if the primary system fails.

The clock is long. SpaceX has seven years to put its first operational satellite into orbit and 14 years to deploy the full constellation. Approval is conditional, and the number is a ceiling rather than a build order. A figure of one million is more than a hundred times the number of active satellites in orbit today, and analysts had treated it as an opening position in a negotiation rather than a manufacturing target.

FCC Chairman Brendan Carr said the action "opens a bold new frontier for the computing infrastructure needed to power America's leadership and growth." He added that Starmind and "competitor constellations will use solar power to extend America's lead" in AI. Elon Musk thanked him on X: "Much appreciate. This will be a great asset to America and the world."

The logic of the pitch is that AI data centres on Earth are running into limits on power, land and local opposition, whereas in orbit solar panels can collect sunlight almost continuously. The approval does not show that the idea works. TechCrunch notes that SpaceX must still cut satellite manufacturing and launch costs, work out how to cool processors in a vacuum, and finance chips destined for orbit.

The debris question, still open

The part of the file most relevant to other orbit users is also the part left unresolved. The FCC has not yet approved SpaceX's debris-mitigation plans for satellites operating above 600 km. SpaceX has asked for a waiver of the rule that requires satellites ending their missions below 2,000 km to re-enter the atmosphere within five years. It also wants to move some retired satellites into other orbits around Earth or the Sun rather than bring them down.

That matters because of altitude. Objects at the lower end of Starmind's range decay quickly; those near 1,000 km can linger for many years if they fail. Adam Becker, an astrophysicist, warned of "a cascading chain reaction of space junk collisions." That is a worst case, not a forecast, but it explains why the unapproved debris plan carries so much weight.

Who objected, and what the FCC set aside

Amazon's Kuiper unit (Amazon Leo), Viasat and the American Astronomical Society all petitioned the FCC to deny the application. They cited debris, congestion, radio interference and environmental effects. Amazon called Starmind a "lofty ambition rather than a real plan" and, per CNBC, said SpaceX would need "centuries" to build it. Carr answered that Amazon "should focus on the fact" that it is behind on its own deployment milestone. Amazon declined to comment on the approval.

The FCC dismissed some objections as outside its authority, including environmental concerns and effects on optical astronomy. Jan Hasselman of Earthjustice criticised the timing, in a US holiday week, and argued the approval skipped an environmental review he believes the National Environmental Policy Act requires. SpaceX did not respond to CNBC's request for comment.

What comes next

SpaceX is not the only company aiming at orbital compute. Google's Project Suncatcher launched its first chips into space in October, on a SpaceX rocket and a Planet Labs satellite. Blue Origin has petitioned the FCC for up to 51,600 satellites under Project Sunrise.

The next concrete test is the FCC's ruling on the debris plan and the five-year disposal waiver. That decision will say more about the actual risk than the headline number does. After that, the data point to watch is the first operational Starmind satellite, which SpaceX has until 2033 to launch, and whether it can show that computing in orbit is cheap enough to compete with a warehouse on the ground.
