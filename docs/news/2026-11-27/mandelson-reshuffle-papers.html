Mandelson tried to influence Starmer's reshuffle, new documents reveal

In one sentence
Reported new Mandelson papers allegedly show him seeking to shape a reshuffle and keeping shares as ambassador, but neither claim is yet tied to a verified document.

A new instalment of the Mandelson papers is said to show the former ambassador trying to influence a Starmer cabinet reshuffle. It is also said to show him keeping shares while in post in Washington. Neither claim can yet be tied to a primary document the Tribune has seen. What the published record does support is narrower, and it still matters for how the government handled his appointment.

What is established

Peter Mandelson was named UK ambassador to the United States in December 2024. He was removed in September 2025 after details of his friendship with Jeffrey Epstein emerged. He is now the subject of a police investigation into whether he leaked government documents to Epstein. That inquiry is why large parts of the released material are redacted, and why some documents, including vetting papers, have been held back.

The government has released the files in tranches after Conservative pressure forced the disclosures. The first, in March, showed that Sir Keir Starmer had been advised in December 2024 that Mandelson's Epstein ties posed a "general reputational risk" before his appointment was confirmed. Reporting on that batch also said the vetting agency had concluded he should be denied clearance, and that the Foreign Office overruled it. The Foreign Office's most senior official at the time, Olly Robbins, was subsequently sacked.

The reshuffle claim

The closest match in the public record is the second tranche, published on 1 June. In messages reported by Irish, SCMP and PoliticsHome outlets, Mandelson said Starmer's No 10 operation needed a "complete revamp". He told Pat McFadden, then at Work and Pensions, that the prime minister lacked "verve". Starmer chaired a cabinet meeting as the messages exposed divisions in Labour.

That is commentary on Downing Street's machinery, from someone who had left government and was writing to a cabinet minister. It is not, on the reporting available, evidence that he lobbied for or against named individuals in a reshuffle. The new claim goes further than that, and the source, date and wording of the document behind it are not yet confirmed. Until they are, it is a claim about the papers rather than a finding from them.

The shares

The shareholding allegation is similarly unverified. No regulator or official has been named as its source, and the document has not been identified.

The public record does show that conflicts of interest were a live concern in the process. Questions were raised about commercial conflicts arising from clients of Global Counsel, the consultancy he co-founded, which is now defunct. But the completed conflict-of-interest declaration, the form that would show what he disclosed and what he was told to do about it, has been withheld by police. The template has been published; his answers have not.

The papers have also shown a request for a severance payment of more than £500,000 after his dismissal, according to reporting on the documents.

What it would mean

If a primary document does show a serving ambassador pressing on cabinet appointments, or holding shares against official advice, the questions would move from the Epstein link to the machinery around it: who knew, what vetting was done and why clearance was apparently overridden. That is the thread the earlier tranches already began to pull.

For now it is better read as a further drip than a fresh disclosure. The test is whether the completed declaration of interests is released once the police inquiry allows. A further tranche would also settle whether the reshuffle language holds up as written.
