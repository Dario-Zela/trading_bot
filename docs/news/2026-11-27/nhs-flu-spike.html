NHS boss warns of 'horrible spike' in flu as admissions surge

In one sentence
Flu admissions in England hit 9.0 per 100,000 against 4.9 a year ago, and NHS England's chief says a 'horrible spike' is coming.

Flu is putting more people in English hospital beds than at this point in any recent year, and the head of the NHS in England has said the worst of the rise is still to come. Sir Jim Mackey, NHS England's chief executive, told the NHS Alliance autumn conference that the service was about to hit a "horrible spike". His one concrete claim was that hospitals were holding more flu patients than a year ago: "Today, I think we have more beds occupied with flu than we did this time last year." He gave no bed count, so that comparison rests on his say-so for now. The surveillance data from the UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA) points the same way.

What the figures show

UKHSA reports that flu hospital admissions in England reached 9.0 per 100,000 people in the latest reporting week, up from 5.0 the week before. That is a rise of about 80% in seven days. In the equivalent week of 2025 the rate was 4.9 per 100,000, which puts this year roughly 84% higher. UKHSA's data show the rate has not been this high at this point in November in any year this decade. One secondary report goes further, saying it is the highest for the time of year in 15 years. That claim could not be checked against the primary series, so it is treated here as unconfirmed.

Other indicators are rising too. The share of flu tests coming back positive rose from 11.5% to 17.8% in a week. Emergency department attendances for flu-like illness were "above seasonally expected levels", according to UKHSA. UKHSA's reporting also describes hospital and intensive care admission rates rising by almost 80% over the past week.

Two cautions apply. A single week's jump from a low base can overstate momentum, and weekly surveillance figures are often revised. UKHSA's own wording is that flu is circulating at a moderate level, which suggests the rise is steep but is not yet at the peak seen in severe seasons. Whether the season is peaking early, or simply starting early, cannot be read from one week of data.

Vaccination gaps

The pressure is landing while vaccine uptake lags. UKHSA data to 22 November show:

  • 67.6% of adults aged 65 and over vaccinated, which UKHSA says is behind where it should be.
  • 34.2% of under-65s in clinical risk groups, of whom about 9.5 million are eligible.
  • 34.9% of pregnant women.
  • 32.1% of school-aged children.
  • Almost four in ten toddlers.
  • About one third of healthcare workers.

Vaccination in these groups is meant to blunt exactly this kind of surge. Uptake is not the only factor in how severe a season becomes, since the circulating strain and background immunity also matter. But the low figures for risk groups and for staff leave more people exposed in the weeks when admissions are climbing.

Strain on hospitals

Mackey said some hospitals have introduced mask wearing to slow the spread of respiratory illness. He also said corridor care, where patients are treated in hallways because no bed is free, has become "normalised" in parts of the NHS. He is described as outgoing, so the handover of winter planning is a further variable.

The arithmetic matters for planning. Winter bed capacity is usually set around a peak in late December or January. If flu admissions are already well above last year's in late November, there is less slack before the usual peak, and staff sickness, which tends to follow community flu, adds to the strain.

What to watch

The next UKHSA weekly flu report and the NHS England winter situation report will show whether the 9.0 per 100,000 rate rises again or levels off. The sitrep will also give actual occupied-bed numbers, which would test Mackey's claim directly. Updated vaccine uptake figures will show whether the warning moves the unvaccinated.
