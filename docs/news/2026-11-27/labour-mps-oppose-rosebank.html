Nearly 100 Labour MPs, including ministerial aides, oppose Rosebank oil field

In one sentence
Reports say nearly 100 Labour MPs, including ministerial aides, oppose Rosebank, pressing the government before its North Sea consent decision; the count is single-sourced.

Nearly 100 Labour MPs are reported to oppose development of the Rosebank oil field west of Shetland. If the figure holds, it would be one of the largest backbench revolts of this parliament on climate policy. The reported opposition is not confined to the back benches: it is said to include ministerial aides, who sit inside the government's payroll vote. The government has yet to announce its consent decision on the field.

The headline figure rests on a single source, BBC Politics, and has not been matched by a second outlet. Other coverage points to a smaller number, so the reporting here is cautious about the exact count.

What is reported, and what is not

Earlier coverage in mid-November, citing the Sunday Times, described a letter to the Prime Minister, Andy Burnham, signed by about 75 Labour MPs. Supporters hoped the total would reach 100. The BBC's "nearly 100" is consistent with that trajectory, but it is a different figure from the one in the earlier reports, and the letter has not been published in full in the reporting reviewed.

The aides are the part that needs the most care. Reports have described at least one parliamentary private secretary to a cabinet minister as considering resigning if Rosebank is approved. A Scottish Labour MP who works as an aide to Ed Miliband has also said publicly that he opposes the project. Neither amounts to a verified, named list of aides among the signatories. Until the letter or a count by name is published, "including ministerial aides" is best read as a reported characterisation, not an audited one.

An aide who resigns over policy is a different matter from a backbencher who signs a letter. Parliamentary private secretaries are bound by collective responsibility, and a resignation is a formal, public break.

The pressure on both sides

The revolt arrives in a decision that is already contested. A majority of the cabinet is reported to favour approval. More than 1,000 North Sea workers have signed an open letter urging the Prime Minister to approve the field before Christmas. Scottish Conservatives have told Mr Burnham to "ignore the growing noise" from his own benches, and business leaders have urged him not to bow to MP pressure.

On the other side, ten unions called in October for Rosebank to be rejected, and campaign groups have pressed the same case since the summer. Backbench anger was sharpened when the Jackdaw gas field was approved first; one outlet reported that eighteen MPs publicly condemned that decision, with many more objecting in private.

The objections fall into two groups. The climate case is that a new field is hard to reconcile with the manifesto's pledge of no new licences and with the UK's carbon budgets. The industrial case, advanced by supporters, is about jobs and the supply of domestic oil and gas as the existing basin declines. Opponents say Rosebank's output would be sold on world markets and would not lower household bills. Supporters say that rejecting it would only increase imports.

What the decision would set

Rosebank is a consent decision for one field, but its consequences are wider. Approval over the objections of this many MPs would show how far the party's climate commitments bind it when they meet energy-security arguments. Rejection would signal to operators that further North Sea development faces a high bar. Either outcome would serve as the precedent for other fields in the queue, Jackdaw included.

The immediate test is the timing and manner of the announcement. Three things bear watching: whether the signatories' letter is published with names, so the number of aides can be counted; whether any aide resigns before or after a decision; and whether Mr Burnham decides before Christmas, as industry has asked. A government that approves Rosebank would need to manage a rebellion of this size, and one that rejects it would face the cabinet majority reported to be in favour.
