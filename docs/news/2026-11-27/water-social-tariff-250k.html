Water firms to identify 250,000 vulnerable households for discounts

In one sentence
Water firms are reportedly to find about 250,000 more low-income households for social tariffs, but the funding and the source figure are unconfirmed.

England's water companies are being asked to find about 250,000 more low-income households and put them on social tariffs, the discounted bills reserved for customers who struggle to pay. The figure comes from a Guardian report. It has not been matched to a regulator, government or company document, and none of the available material says how the discounts would be paid for.

What is known

Social tariffs are not new. Each water company runs its own scheme, with its own eligibility rules and discount levels. The Consumer Council for Water (CCW) reports that about 2.3 million households in England and Wales were receiving help through them in 2025-26. That was almost 380,000 more than a year earlier. The CCW also found that average annual reductions on the main water and sewerage companies' schemes ranged from £159 to £474.

Against that base, 250,000 further households would be a sizeable but not extraordinary expansion. It would be roughly 11% on the 2025-26 total, and less than the 380,000 added in the latest year. Earlier figures show the same direction of travel. A Social Market Foundation briefing put the number of customers on company social tariffs at 1.6 million, which was 4% of all customers in England and Wales and more than 250,000 above 2022-23.

It is also possible that the Guardian's 250,000 is a different number, such as a past increase in take-up, rather than a new target. That cannot be settled without the underlying document.

Who pays

The funding question is the one that matters for the sector, and it is unanswered here. Water social tariffs have mostly been funded by other customers. Companies add a small amount to everyone else's bills to cover the discounts, within the price limits set by the regulator, Ofwat. If the 250,000 new households are covered the same way, the cost moves between customers and company earnings are largely untouched. If instead companies have to absorb part of it, the hit falls on revenue and margins, and would show up in the next set of results.

A rough bound is possible, though it is arithmetic and not reporting. At the CCW's range of £159 to £474 a year, 250,000 households would cost somewhere between about £40 million and £120 million a year. That is small against sector-wide revenues running into the tens of billions of pounds. A cost of that size would be unlikely to change a listed company's earnings on its own. It matters more as a precedent, because it signals how far ministers and the regulator will push companies on affordability while bills rise.

Identification is the hard part

The wording of the push, that firms should identify households, points to a take-up problem more than a funding one. Many eligible customers do not apply, because they don't know the scheme exists or find the forms burdensome. Some companies are already using data-sharing to find eligible customers and enrol them without an application. Ofwat has described a project in Kent in which customers are moved on to the social tariff automatically. Wider use of that approach would add households faster than awareness campaigns.

What to watch

Three things would turn this from a headline into a measurable change. The first is the primary document, from Ofwat or government, that sets the 250,000 figure and its deadline. The second is whether the discounts are paid for through other customers' bills or out of company revenue. The third is the CCW's next annual affordability data, which would show whether enrolment is rising at the stated pace.
