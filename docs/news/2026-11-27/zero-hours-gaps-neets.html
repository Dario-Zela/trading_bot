Up to 1.4m workers could miss zero-hours protections; Neets fall below a million

In one sentence
Unions say up to 1.4m workers could fall outside the new zero-hours protections, an unverified estimate that turns on how the reforms define who is covered.

A law can pass and still leave people out. That is the argument unions are now making about the zero-hours provisions in the government's employment rights reforms. They say up to 1.4 million UK workers could fall outside the new protections. The figure is a union estimate, reported by the Guardian, and has not been independently verified. The question it raises is who the law actually covers, not whether it passes.

The claim

The reforms are meant to curb the least predictable forms of insecure work. As widely described, they would give workers on zero-hours and low-hours contracts a right to be offered guaranteed hours, reasonable notice of shifts, and payment when shifts are cancelled or cut short at the last minute.

The unions' point is about reach. Protections of this kind depend on definitions: who counts as a worker, which contracts count as zero-hours or low-hours, and over what period hours are measured. Anyone who falls on the wrong side of a definition does not benefit, however well the rights are drafted. The unions put that group at up to 1.4 million people.

The summary of the story available to this newsroom does not say which provisions produce the gap, and this article does not guess. The mechanism needs checking against the bill text and the Guardian's full report. It is also unclear how the 1.4 million figure was built, or whether it overlaps with groups ministers have already said sit outside the scheme.

Why the definition matters

A headline estimate of this kind is only as good as its assumptions. "Up to" signals an upper bound. The real number depends on how many of those workers would be excluded on the most restrictive reading of the rules, and how many would be covered once the secondary legislation and guidance are written.

Both sides have reasons to fight over the wording. Unions want the widest coverage. Employers, particularly in hospitality, retail and social care, where flexible contracts are common, want clarity and room to keep some flexibility. Ministers have to show that a flagship reform reaches the people it was sold to. If a sizeable group is left out, the policy's stated aim is weakened for those workers, and the political cost lands on the government.

No government response or independent analysis accompanied the figure in the material available. Both are needed before the 1.4 million can be treated as more than an opening position.

A second number, a different test

A separate Guardian report this week looked at young people. The count of those not in education, employment or training, the Neets, has reportedly fallen below one million. Andy Burnham is reported to be pushing new measures to tackle what the report describes as a crisis.

The available detail is thin: no survey source, date or breakdown. The same caution applies as with the union estimate. A fall below a round-number threshold is welcome, but it does not say whether more young people have found work or training, or whether the measure has moved for other reasons, such as changes in population or survey response. The two stories share a theme. Headline figures in labour policy, whether of people helped or people missed, need testing against how they are defined and counted.

What to watch

The practical test is the detail still to come: the final definitions in the employment rights legislation, any government estimate of how many zero-hours workers are covered, and the guidance employers will work from. If ministers publish their own figure, the distance between it and the unions' 1.4 million will show how contested the coverage really is. For the Neets figure, the next official release will show whether the fall holds.
