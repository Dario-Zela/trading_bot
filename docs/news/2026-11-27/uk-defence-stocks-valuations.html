Rolls-Royce and UK defence stocks: valuations reassessed as budgets shift

In one sentence
Valuation commentary says investors are reassessing Rolls-Royce and UK defence names as budgets shift, but no price move or specific spending decision could be verified.

Investors are re-examining what British defence and aerospace shares are worth, and the argument runs through government budgets. Rolls-Royce is the name most often cited. The case is a valuation one, though, and it rests on a spending decision rather than a market event. This article does not cite a specific rally, because none could be verified.

The sole source behind the story is Simply Wall St, a retail-investor platform that publishes automated and analyst-written valuation commentary. That is a useful prompt for a question. It is not reporting of a price move, and no share-price figures, dates or budget amounts are given here for that reason.

The budget argument

The thesis is simple. If governments redirect money towards defence, companies that sell into defence budgets should see more predictable revenue, and their valuations should adjust. The policy backdrop is established. The UK government has committed to raising defence spending to 2.5% of GDP by 2027. NATO allies agreed at The Hague summit in June 2025 to a target of 5% of GDP by 2035, of which 3.5% is core military spending.

The backdrop is already public, so it is not new information. A valuation reassessment that rests on it only works if investors are updating their forecasts for specific contracts and programmes.

Why Rolls-Royce is a mixed case

Rolls-Royce is not a pure defence stock. Its largest business by revenue is civil aerospace, which depends on airline traffic and engine flying hours. Its defence division supplies engines and power systems for military aircraft, and its submarines unit supplies nuclear reactors for the Royal Navy's submarine fleet. Any rise in defence budgets would feed through to only part of the group, and the market would be weighing it against the much larger civil engine business.

Pure-play British defence contractors would reflect budget decisions more directly. The distinction matters for interpretation: a move in Rolls-Royce shares on a budget announcement says less about defence demand than a move in a defence specialist would.

What is missing

Three things would be needed to turn this from a valuation talking point into a story:

  • the actual share-price moves, measured over a stated period against the FTSE 100;
  • the specific spending decision, whether a UK budget line, a procurement award or a NATO commitment, that investors are said to be pricing in;
  • forward earnings or order-book figures showing that analysts have changed their estimates, rather than only the multiples.

Without these, a valuation call amounts to a restatement of a trend that has been visible since the spending pledges of 2025. Valuation tools also tend to describe prices after the move has happened, so a reassessment can lag the event it claims to explain.

The practical strain on delivery is visible elsewhere in this edition. The Ministry of Defence cannot email half its reservists, and East Midlands road schemes have been shelved to make room for defence. Larger budgets do not turn into contracts automatically.

What to watch

The next data points are the ones that connect budgets to company revenue: the Treasury's next fiscal statement for any change to the defence line, announcements of equipment contracts, and company trading updates showing whether defence orders are growing. Until one of those is in hand, the reassessment remains an argument rather than a result.
