Nvidia beats estimates in first report since Huang resigned, as Asia's exposure to the chipmaker comes under scrutiny

In one sentence
Nvidia beat on revenue, earnings and guidance in its first report since Jensen Huang quit as CEO, but inventory, receivables and supply commitments are rising fast.

Nvidia's first quarterly report since Jensen Huang left the chief executive's chair beat Wall Street's estimates, and the shares rose about 5% in after-hours trading. The numbers were hard to fault. The balance sheet behind them is growing fast, and that is where the questions now sit.

The company reported third-quarter fiscal 2027 results on 17 November, for the quarter ended 25 October. Revenue was a record $115.8bn, up 103% from a year earlier, with $107.6bn from data centres. Non-GAAP earnings were $2.68 a share, against a consensus of about $2.48 cited by one data provider. Guidance for the fourth quarter is about $130bn, plus or minus 3%, above what analysts had expected. That guidance assumes no data-centre compute revenue from China.

A founder's exit, and a board that won't reverse it

The leadership change is now confirmed. Nvidia's board announced on 12 November that Huang had stepped down as president, chief executive and director, effective immediately. Chief financial officer Colette Kress is interim chief executive. Harvey C. Jones became the company's first board chair. The company's release gave no reason for the departure, and that is why this piece does not offer one.

The earnings call added a twist. Huang said earlier that day that he would be "honored" to return if the board judged it in shareholders' interest. The new chair said at the start of the call that the board did not intend to reinstate him. No timetable has been given for naming a permanent successor, and the board will consider internal and external candidates.

That leaves investors with a company run by its finance chief, who is also the executive who signed off on the numbers they are now picking over. Some will read that as continuity. Others will want to know who would challenge the figures.

What the balance sheet shows

A strong quarter does not settle the question that analysts are asking about the accounts. Three lines stand out.

Inventory rose to $39.7bn from $31.6bn in the prior quarter, a jump of about 26% in three months. Revenue grew more slowly than that. The company ties the build to the ramp of its Rubin chips. A new product generation needs stock in the warehouse before it ships, so a build is expected. It becomes a concern only if demand softens before the stock clears.

Receivables, the money customers owe, reached $81.0bn. Days sales outstanding, a measure of how long Nvidia takes to collect, lengthened to 64 from 60. One source attributes this to Rubin shipment timing and to longer payment terms on large multi-quarter deals with investment-grade customers. That explanation is plausible, but it is also an admission that Nvidia is, in effect, lending more to its buyers' timetables. I could not confirm the prior-quarter receivables figure, so the quarterly growth rate is not stated here.

Supply commitments, the contracts under which Nvidia has promised to buy chips, memory and manufacturing capacity, are cited by secondary sources at roughly $390bn. I found no detailed breakdown of that figure. These obligations are what a chipmaker signs when it expects demand to keep climbing. They are also what hurts if it does not.

None of this proves trouble. A company doubling its sales would be expected to carry more stock and more unpaid invoices. The test is the next two quarters: whether inventory turns, whether collection days stop lengthening, and whether commitments are worked down or keep growing faster than revenue. The $130bn guide is the company's own bet that they will.

Asia's stake in the outcome

The leadership and balance-sheet questions matter well beyond Santa Clara, and nowhere more than in Asia. The region's exposure is physical as much as financial. Taiwan makes the chips, South Korea supplies the memory, and Southeast Asia hosts a growing share of the servers. When Nvidia's stock moves, the shares of those suppliers have tended to move with it. In an April 2025 selloff, Taiwan Semiconductor fell 2.5% and SK Hynix 3.9%.

A BBC Business report has examined how heavily Asian markets are exposed to the chipmaker. I was unable to retrieve it, so index weights and the named critics it cites are not reproduced here. Commentary has also noted how Huang cultivated governments across the region, and Asian governments have responded to his exit in different ways. Those relationships were personal in a way that a corporate successor cannot simply inherit.

The point about supply commitments is sharpest here. Much of that $390bn or so is, in effect, orders placed with Asian manufacturers. If Nvidia's demand holds, those suppliers keep filling their order books. If it stalls, the stress travels back along the same chain.

What to watch

Three things will decide whether this quarter calms the market. The first is the naming of a permanent chief executive, and whether the board picks an outsider or the interim incumbent. The second is the fourth-quarter report, when Nvidia must show inventory and receivables moving in step with the $130bn it has promised. The third is any change in the supply-commitment disclosure in the next annual filing. A beat bought some time. The next set of accounts will show whether it was used well.
