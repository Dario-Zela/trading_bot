Man City owners should be forced to sell, say rival club chiefs

In one sentence
Senior executives at three rival clubs say City's owners must be forced to sell, a remedy beyond the Premier League's sanction range that only the new regulator could deliver.

Manchester City's owners should be forced to sell the club as punishment for breaking financial rules, according to senior executives at three top-flight rivals. They told BBC Sport that anything short of that would leave the sanction hollow. Whatever the commission decides, the remedy they want is one the Premier League itself has never imposed.

The executives spoke anonymously. One said City's punishment would mean little unless the owners were also forced out. Another said the breaches had damaged the league's credibility and that their club could not move on if the owners stayed. Others declined to comment or said they would leave the matter to the commission and the regulator. The Premier League has asked clubs not to state their views on sanctions in public, which helps explain the anonymity.

Not a stalled case, but an unfinished one

The case is further along than its reputation suggests. The league charged City with 115 alleged breaches in February 2023, covering 2009-10 to 2017-18. In September, according to BBC Sport's reporting, an independent commission found the club had breached the financial rules between 2009 and 2018 and had failed to cooperate with the investigation. The reported finding is that revenues were inflated and losses of £900m disguised through "sham" commercial arrangements.

City deny wrongdoing. They say the ruling contains "clear material errors, of law, principle and fact and is unsafe", and they have appealed. No sanction has been set. The range reported runs from a fine and points deduction to the stripping of past titles or expulsion from the league. A forced sale is not on that list.

That gap is the heart of the rivals' demand. A league sanction falls on the club: its points, its money, its trophies. The owner, Abu Dhabi United Group, a private investment company belonging to the UAE vice-president and deputy prime minister Sheikh Mansour, is a separate matter. Fining a club does not touch the person who owns it.

The regulator, not the league

The route to an owner's exit runs through the Independent Football Regulator rather than the Premier League. Under section 34 of the Football Governance Act, the regulator can test whether an owner meets suitability standards on honesty, integrity and financial soundness. If an owner fails, it can direct them to relinquish ownership within a set period. If they do not, it can issue an ownership removal order.

The regulator's chair, David Kogan, told a parliamentary committee the body would act if needed. "We will absolutely have the stomach to do so," he said, adding that the name and size of the club make no difference. He also said the regulator's response depends on the outcome of City's appeal. Chief executive Richard Monks told MPs that a £2m litigation reserve will build up over two years through the clubs' levy, with government lending more if a large case required it. The regulator is set to cost clubs in the top five English men's divisions £20.3m a year.

That levy explains the rivals' leverage. One executive said anything less than a sale would make clubs question why they pay for the regulator at all.

Politics and pressure

The politics are less tidy. Prime Minister Andy Burnham said in September he would be "really concerned" if the scandal ended in the owners selling. Critics questioned that stance, given the UAE's trade and investment ties with Britain.

City's commercial partners are also pushing back. Etihad and Etisalat, both UAE-based sponsors, are considering legal action against the Premier League. Last week clubs voted to cover legal costs for the league's five board directors if the sponsors sue. Rivals are demanding a harder line while also underwriting the people who would have to deliver it.

What to watch

The next decision point is the appeal. If the finding stands, the commission must set a sanction, and the regulator must then decide whether it will assess City's owners under its suitability test. That would be the first time English football's rules were used against an owner rather than a club. Kogan has said the regulator will act. City's appeal will show whether the finding survives for him to act on.
