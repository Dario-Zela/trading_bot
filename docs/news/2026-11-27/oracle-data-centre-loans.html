Oracle falls below $100 as banks try to offload data-centre loans

In one sentence
Reports of banks selling down Oracle-linked data-centre loans are partly documented, but the sub-$100 share price and its link to the sales are unverified.

Oracle's shares have been trading below $100, according to the financial-news aggregator 24/7 Wall St. The same report says lenders are trying to sell down debt tied to Oracle-linked data-centre projects. Neither claim could be confirmed against a primary source: no bank, filing or named reporter backs the price move or ties it to the loan sales. The wider pattern of banks struggling to place this debt is better documented, though, and it is the more important story.

What is established

The loans at issue finance data centres built for Oracle and its customer OpenAI under the Stargate programme. In September 2025 Bloomberg reported that banks were preparing about $38bn of debt for Oracle-tied sites in Texas and Wisconsin, developed by Vantage. A separate, roughly $18bn package financed the Project Jupiter campus in New Mexico.

The resale has been slow. According to reports from September this year, relayed by Reuters and the Financial Times, the New Mexico loans were quoted at 89 to 91 cents on the dollar, and banks ended up holding more of the debt than planned. One trade-press account says less than $1bn of the $38bn package remained to be placed about six months in, with some lenders selling to non-bank buyers at a discount. That figure comes from a secondary source, and other coverage describes the syndication as largely successful.

Smaller sales have followed. The FT reported that ING and Société Générale had offered up to $800m of data-centre debt, including about $300m of project-finance exposure to Oracle-linked sites in Wisconsin and Texas. A further auction of roughly $500m of loans, including Vantage-linked debt and a slice of the New Mexico site, was reported with bids due on 19 November. The seller was not named, and the outcome of that auction has not been published.

Why the price of the debt matters

Banks that underwrite a large loan usually intend to sell most of it to institutional investors quickly. They earn fees for arranging it and free up capital for the next deal. When the loan sells at 90 cents, or sits on the balance sheet, that model stops working. The bank holds a bigger exposure than intended, and the next borrower pays more.

Oracle's own credit standing is part of the explanation. Reuters reported that S&P downgraded the company in July to one notch above junk. In July, 24/7 Wall St. also cited S&P's warnings about concentration in OpenAI and a projected $42bn free-cash-flow deficit in fiscal 2027. Separately, TD Cowen has reported that lenders financing Oracle's projects since September have charged roughly double the earlier interest rates. S&P Global's Dhaval Shah has said some banks may be nearing their comfort limits on data-centre exposure.

These are the signals that make a sell-down worth watching. Data-centre debt is long-dated and rests on a small number of tenants. If it can be placed only at a discount, the cost of building AI capacity rises, whatever the equity market is doing that day.

What is not established

The share price is the weaker element. The most recent 24/7 Wall St. coverage that could be found put Oracle at about $132 in mid-July, a 52-week low after a 28% fall in a month. A move below $100 would be a further decline of roughly a quarter. It is plausible given that trend, but no price, date or percentage move was available to check, and no search turned up the article itself. A current quote should be consulted before the figure is relied on.

The causal link is also unproven. Oracle's shares have been under pressure for months from its debt load, its reliance on OpenAI and its negative free cash flow. A report of loan sales is one more input, and it may be a symptom of that pressure as much as a cause. The aggregator's claim is single-sourced, names no lenders, loan sizes or buyers, and should not be read as evidence of a broad retreat. Bank selling is also normal behaviour: lenders routinely sell down exposure, and the question is the price.

What to watch

The clearest test is the result of the 19 November loan auction: whether bids cleared, and at what price relative to the 89–91 cent quotes from September. Beyond that, the next Oracle earnings report will show how much capital spending the company is committing to and how it plans to fund it. A named lender confirming a sale, or a narrowing of the discount, would say more about the appetite for this risk than any single day's share price.
