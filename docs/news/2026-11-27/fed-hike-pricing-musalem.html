Fed hike pricing builds as Musalem urges further tightening

In one sentence
TheStreet Pro reports futures price Fed hikes in December and January after Musalem urged tightening; the claim is unconfirmed, with no pricing levels supplied.

A report that US interest-rate futures have swung from pricing cuts to pricing rises would, if borne out, mark one of the sharper hawkish turns in the Federal Reserve outlook this year. The claim comes from a single trade-press outlet, TheStreet Pro, and has not been independently confirmed. It is set out here with that caveat attached.

What is being reported

TheStreet Pro says futures markets are now pricing quarter-point rate rises at the Fed's December and January meetings. The same report links the move to remarks from Alberto Musalem, president of the St. Louis Fed, who is said to have publicly urged further tightening. The report as relayed supplies no futures levels, no implied probabilities and no yields. The date, venue and wording of Musalem's remarks have also not been verified, so no quotation is given here.

The gap matters. "Pricing a hike" can mean a probability barely above a coin toss or a near-certainty, and the two imply very different market behaviour. Without the CME FedWatch figures or the fed funds futures strip, the headline claim cannot be sized.

Why it would matter beyond Washington

A shift from expected easing to expected tightening would reprice duration globally. US Treasury yields would tend to rise, the dollar would tend to firm against its main pairs, and borrowing costs elsewhere would feel the pull.

The UK is directly exposed. Gilts have been trading close to 5% after the Bank of England's recent hike, a backdrop that has already fed through to mortgage costs (see related coverage). A hawkish Fed would add to upward pressure on gilt yields through the usual transatlantic correlation in long-dated debt. Sterling's response is less predictable: a stronger dollar would weigh on it, while higher UK yields would partly offset that. The report gestures at both channels but offers no data on either.

What would settle it

Three things would confirm or kill the story quickly:

  • Futures data. The December and January fed funds contracts, or the FedWatch probabilities derived from them, would show directly whether a hike is priced and by how much.
  • A second source. Reuters, Bloomberg or the Fed's own published remarks should be able to corroborate Musalem's comments and the market reaction.
  • Treasury moves. A repricing of this size would be visible in two-year Treasury yields, the part of the curve most sensitive to the policy path.

The next scheduled catalysts are the Fed's December policy meeting and the US data releases that precede it. Until the futures figures are in hand, the story stays at the status of a credible-sounding but unconfirmed report.
