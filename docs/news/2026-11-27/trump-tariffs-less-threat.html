Why Trump's new tariffs may be less of a threat, by Faisal Islam

In one sentence
The BBC's Faisal Islam argues the latest US tariffs may hurt markets and the UK less than feared, though Section 301's open-ended rate-setting leaves room for the picture to change.

The latest round of US tariffs may do less damage to markets and to the UK than the headlines suggest, according to the BBC's economics editor, Faisal Islam. That is a contrarian position on a subject that has been covered heavily since the Supreme Court struck down the administration's emergency-powers tariffs in February. The full text of Islam's analysis could not be retrieved for this article. His argument is taken from its headline and summary only, and the figures below come from separate published sources.

How the tariffs got here

The route to the current measures was a legal one. After the Supreme Court ruled against the tariffs imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA), the administration fell back on Section 122 of the Trade Act. That gave it a 10% import surcharge, in force from 24 February. Section 122 has a statutory ceiling of 15% and a limited duration, and the surcharge expired as new duties under Section 301 took effect on 24 July, according to law-firm and congressional research summaries.

The UK's position changed less than the legal churn implies. The Congressional Research Service notes that the additional 10% tariff on UK goods was left unchanged, although its legal basis moved from IEEPA to Section 122 and then to Section 301. ITV reported a new 10% levy on the UK when the Section 301 measures were announced, covering some 60 countries. Law firm Clifford Chance puts the Section 301 range at 10% to 12.5% across the countries covered. The exact UK rate under the newest measures is not confirmed here.

Why the impact may be limited

Several features support a milder reading. Economists cited by Semafor and the Atlantic Council expected only minor global disruption from the July measures. Broad exemptions, including for critical minerals, narrow the base of goods affected. NPR reported that the new tariffs are not significantly higher than those they replace.

The UK has a further cushion. Under its pharmaceuticals arrangement with Washington, the UK committed to spend more on new medicines, and the US committed not to apply additional pharmaceutical tariffs on the UK under Section 232 or Section 301. Drugs are a large UK export to the US, so the commitment covers a significant sector.

If Islam's thesis holds, the practical consequence is that UK exporters face a tariff that is already in their pricing, not a new shock. Gilt and sterling markets would then have less reason to price in a fresh hit to growth. That matters for a Bank of England already working against persistent inflation, as reflected in gilt yields near 5%.

What the optimistic reading leaves out

The caveats are real. Section 301, unlike Section 122, carries no fixed upper limit on rates, so the present calm depends on how the administration chooses to use it. Challengers argue the new regime is a pretext for the old one, and NPR warned that further court fights could follow. Commentators also say the wider effect on UK competitiveness, and on the UK-US Economic Prosperity Deal, remains unclear.

The things to watch are any change in the Section 301 rates applied to the UK, the outcome of legal challenges to the new duties, and UK trade data showing whether exports to the US hold up. A single analyst's assessment is a useful counterweight to the alarm. It does not substitute for that data.
