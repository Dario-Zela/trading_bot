Nvidia confirms $12.9bn Hugging Face acquisition

Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, who announced the $12.93bn Hugging Face acquisition and pledged the platform would remain open to the wider AI ecosystem. — Government of India / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Nvidia is buying Hugging Face, the platform 18 million developers use to share AI models, for $12.9bn — its second-largest deal ever.

Nvidia is buying Hugging Face, the platform that has become the default meeting point for the world's open-source AI developers, for $12.93 billion. The deal, confirmed this week after weeks of reporting, hands the world's dominant chipmaker not just the hardware layer of the AI boom but a large piece of the software distribution layer too — the place where models, datasets and demo apps get uploaded, downloaded and argued about.

Hugging Face is not a household name the way ChatGPT or Nvidia itself is, but among the people who actually build AI systems it is closer to indispensable. More than 18 million developers, researchers and companies use it to share and download the raw material of modern AI: upwards of three million models, half a million datasets and a million small applications, according to figures released alongside the announcement. Roughly 200,000 companies use the platform to find, test and deploy models rather than train their own from scratch. If Nvidia's chips are the engine of the AI industry, Hugging Face has become something like its supply depot.

Why a chipmaker wants a model library

The logic, on Nvidia's own telling, is straightforward. "Together, we will scale Hugging Face's platform, strengthen its infrastructure and expand access to AI for developers and institutions worldwide," chief executive Jensen Huang said in a post on the company's blog announcing the deal. Nvidia is already, by its own count, the largest single contributor of open models and datasets to the platform — more than 500 models and 250 datasets — and Huang has spent much of the past two years arguing publicly that open-weight models are essential to keeping American AI development ahead of Chinese rivals.

There's a commercial logic sitting underneath the ideological one. Hugging Face had reportedly turned down a $500 million approach from Nvidia last year; by the time talks resumed, the smaller company had grown into a business generating an estimated $150 million a year in revenue and edging toward profitability, on total funding of under $400 million. That a company raised under $400 million and sold for nearly $13 billion says something about how much strategic value now attaches to being the place where AI developers already are, rather than the place trying to lure them somewhere new.

The transaction is Nvidia's second-largest on record, smaller only than the roughly $20 billion licensing-and-hiring deal it struck for Groq's chip assets last December — itself structured to sidestep some of the antitrust scrutiny that a straight acquisition would invite, after senators Elizabeth Warren and Richard Blumenthal publicly questioned the manoeuvre. The Hugging Face purchase, by contrast, is a conventional acquisition: roughly $11.9 billion to Hugging Face shareholders, with up to $1 billion more set aside as retention awards for employees joining Nvidia. It is expected to close in the first half of 2027, pending regulatory clearance.

The promise of neutrality — and why it's being doubted

Aware of how the deal looks, Nvidia has front-loaded a set of commitments. Huang has pledged that Hugging Face will remain open to the entire ecosystem: developers will keep choosing whichever models, frameworks, clouds and computing platforms they want, Nvidia hardware will not be required to build on or deploy through the platform, and Hugging Face will keep supporting open-weight models regardless of who trained them or which chips they were built on. "Open models let startups, businesses, universities and public institutions build on advanced capabilities without training every model from scratch," Huang said.

Analysts are taking the pledge with some caution. Charlie Dai at Forrester noted that the deal gives Nvidia "a stronger position at the developer, model distribution, and community layers" — on top of the GPU hardware, CUDA software and networking gear it already controls — and advised enterprises to "watch for future shifts rather than immediate disruption, while risk assessment around deeper integration with Nvidia tooling will be necessary." Others have raised a subtler concern: that a chipmaker buying its own biggest software distribution channel, while also being that channel's largest content contributor, risks a kind of circular economy in which capital and usage metrics flow between the same small set of players, flattering growth figures without adding much real competition to the market.

What regulators, and rivals, do next

The practical test will be whether rival chipmakers and cloud providers — AMD, Google, Amazon, Microsoft — keep treating Hugging Face as neutral ground once it is a subsidiary of the company whose GPUs they compete against. Model publishers and enterprises that rely on the platform for distribution have little alternative today; a credible fork or competitor would take years to reach comparable scale. That dependency is exactly what makes the antitrust question live, and after the pushback Nvidia already faced over the Groq structure, regulators on both sides of the Atlantic are likely to look hard at whether the openness commitments are enforceable or merely promised. The deal's first concrete checkpoint is the close, targeted for the first half of 2027 — the window in which competition authorities will decide whether "open platform" is a binding condition or just a sentence in a blog post.
