DOJ denies pausing antitrust cooperation with Canada

The Robert F. Kennedy Department of Justice Building in Washington, D.C., headquarters of the U.S. Justice Department. — Coolcaesar / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The DOJ called a WSJ report of a Canada antitrust cooperation freeze 'false,' but its own account leaves open how much actually changed.

There is a particular flavour of Washington story that runs entirely on the gap between what an email allegedly said and what a spokesperson insists it meant. This is one of them.

The Wall Street Journal reported this week that Lynda Marshall, chief of the Justice Department antitrust division's international section, told officials in a Wednesday email to halt all cooperation on cases and policy engagement with Canadian competition authorities — no reason given. A follow-up email on Friday reportedly asked section chiefs to compile lists of every area where the division works with Canada, due by the end of the day.

The Justice Department says none of that happened. "The alleged direction was never given to anyone," a spokesperson told Reuters, calling the WSJ account false. The department's version is narrower: antitrust staff were asked to "temporarily hold off on a scheduled meeting on a specific investigation" so the U.S. team would have more time to prepare — a scheduling request, not a policy freeze.

A pause with little precedent

What makes the denial worth lingering on is what it doesn't explain. Antitrust officials quoted in the original report noted that a blanket halt of this kind would be unusual: the division cooperates routinely with foreign counterparts, including in countries with far chillier relations with Washington than Canada has. The U.S. and Canada have run parallel antitrust probes for decades — auto-parts price-fixing, air-cargo cartels — under cooperation agreements dating back to the 1990s and reinforced in 2004. A sudden, undocumented order to stop all of it would be a break from that pattern significant enough to draw notice inside the department itself, which is presumably how it reached the Journal in the first place.

The two accounts aren't strictly incompatible — a request to delay one meeting and a broader freeze on cooperation could, in theory, both have happened, described differently by different people. But the department's flat "false" leaves no room for that reading; it is a denial of the entire premise, not a clarification of scope.

The backdrop that makes it plausible

None of this is happening in a vacuum. Washington and Ottawa have spent the past year in an escalating tariff fight: the U.S. imposed duties on Canadian steel, aluminum, wine and cement in August, and Canada responded with retaliatory tariffs of 15–50% on more than 700 American goods — including doubling its own steel and aluminum duties to 50% — set to take effect September 8. Prime Minister Mark Carney has publicly sparred with both President Trump and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick over the collapse of trade talks. It is against that backdrop that a report of antitrust cooperation being iced reads as plausible even before anyone checks the sourcing — and why the DOJ's denial, however categorical, is unlikely to fully settle the question of whether Ottawa is being frozen out somewhere short of a formal directive.

What's next

Neither side has published the underlying emails, so the dispute currently rests on the Journal's sourcing against the department's on-record denial. Canada's retaliatory tariffs land September 8, a date that will sharpen the political temperature around any further antitrust friction — watch for whether either government's competition authority confirms or denies a change in working-level contact in the days around that deadline.
