BoE holds at 3.75% as gilt yields hit highest since 2007

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, London. — Diliff / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
BoE held at 3.75% on a 6-3 vote with three MPC members wanting a hike, while 10-year gilt yields hit their highest since 2007 ahead of the 17 September decision.

The Bank of England left Bank Rate at 3.75% on 30 July, but the vote that got it there — 6-3, with three MPC members pushing openly for an immediate rise to 4% — is doing more to unsettle markets than the headline hold. Megan Greene, Catherine Mann and Huw Pill dissented in favour of tightening, one more hawkish vote than the previous meeting produced. Governor Andrew Bailey has downplayed the shift, telling the Jackson Hole gathering in late August that "we're seeing quite subdued second-round effects" and pointing to a softening labour market. The bond market is not entirely convinced.

A committee no longer of one mind

UK inflation ran at 2.9% in July, well clear of the Bank's 2% target and, per the MPC's own projections, headed toward a peak near 3.2% in the fourth quarter. That combination — above-target inflation with a widening bloc of members arguing for higher rates — is why the next decision, due 17 September, is being treated as more contestable than the July vote alone would suggest. Money markets still lean toward inertia: pricing implies only around a 15% chance of a September move, but a quarter-point rise is fully priced in by year-end and a further increase by next March. The August inflation print, due from the ONS on 16 September, lands the day before the MPC votes and will be the last hard data point the committee sees.

Gilts price in the tightening the vote implies

While the rate itself hasn't moved, the bond market has been pricing the risk of it doing so. The 10-year gilt yield climbed above 5.25% this week, its highest level since 2007 — the last stretch before the financial crisis — and 30-year yields touched 5.89%, a level not seen since 1998. The move reflects both the domestic rate story and a broader global bond sell-off tied to oil prices and hawkish signals from other central banks, but the timing is awkward for the Treasury. Chancellor John Healey is due to deliver his first Budget on 28 October, and Deutsche Bank estimates the yield rise alone could cut his fiscal headroom from roughly £26 billion to £13.8 billion versus the spring forecast — pressure that exists regardless of what the MPC actually decides.

What ties the two threads together

A rate rise and a gilt sell-off are not the same event, but they are feeding each other: a more divided MPC makes a September hike look plausible enough to keep long-dated yields elevated, and elevated yields tighten fiscal and borrowing conditions before the Bank has to lift its own rate at all. For households and businesses, financing costs are already moving in the direction that only a minority of the MPC formally wants. The next two data points — the 16 September CPI release and the 17 September rate decision — will determine whether September marks the moment the majority runs out, or another instalment of the same 6-3 split.
