Beekeepers call for national campaign as Asian hornet sightings rise

The Asian hornet (Vespa velutina), an invasive predator of honeybees now established across southern England. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
UK beekeepers are calling for a national campaign as Asian hornet sightings spread north, warning of pollinator losses that carry real costs for honey and crop production.

Britain's beekeepers want a national public-information campaign on the Asian hornet, and they want it now, before this year's crop of young queens scatters to found next spring's colonies.

The British Beekeepers' Association says the case has moved beyond seasonal vigilance. As of 20 August, the National Bee Unit — part of the Animal and Plant Health Agency — had logged 145 credible sightings and 41 confirmed nests across England this year, according to its rolling public update. Kent remains the epicentre, with clusters around Dover, Ashford, Canterbury and Ramsgate, but the map has stretched to Dorset, Norfolk, Derbyshire and, as of 19 August, a secondary nest at Chadderton in Greater Manchester — the furthest north the species has established a breeding colony in the UK to date. A secondary nest, rather than a first-generation one, means an already-established colony has produced new queens of its own.

"Asian hornets leave behind pollinator deserts, with a serious impact on food security and agricultural economy," said Lucie Chaumeton, a BBKA trustee and the association's Asian hornet coordinator.

A predator built for honeybees

Vespa velutina arrived in Europe by accident — most likely in a shipment of pottery from China that landed in south-west France around 2004 — and reached the UK mainland near Tetbury in September 2016. It has since spread through the Channel Islands, where the population is now well established: Jersey recorded 705 nests in 2025, and by late April this year had already logged 72 embryo nests and 1,310 queens, roughly double the equivalent point in 2025.

The hornet's danger to honeybees is structural, not incidental. Colonies station workers to "hawk" outside hive entrances, snatching foraging bees as they return, which stresses colonies into shutting down and starving even when they haven't been directly attacked. Western honeybees, unlike their Asian counterparts, have not evolved defences against this tactic. In parts of France where the hornet is established, beekeepers report colony losses ranging from 5% to 80%, averaging around 30%.

The economic exposure is not trivial. The UK's roughly 250,000 honeybee hives are estimated to contribute around £150 million a year through honey and pollination services, and researchers at the University of Exeter have put potential annual losses from unchecked hornet spread at £30 million to £45 million. Pollinator decline more broadly carries a bigger tail risk: the Non-Native Species Secretariat estimates that losing all UK pollinators — not just honeybees — would cost the farming economy around £440 million a year, roughly 13% of farm income, with fruit crops such as apples and pears among the most exposed.

Reporting fatigue undercuts eradication

The BBKA's push for a coordinated campaign is partly a response to a signal problem. Nearly 30,000 reports came in through the Asian Hornet Watch app last year, but only around 70 were credible sightings, leading to 24 confirmed nests. That volume of noise slows the National Bee Unit's ability to trace and destroy nests quickly — the only method that reliably limits spread, since queens that survive to autumn disperse to overwinter and found new colonies in spring.

More than 1,000 BBKA volunteers have been mobilised to give local beekeeping associations backup when a report needs verifying on the ground, and the association has published an identification sheet to help the public distinguish the Asian hornet from native wasps and hornets — smaller, darker, with distinctive yellow-tipped legs — ahead of Asian Hornet Week, which runs 7–13 September.

What's next

With autumn approaching, the next few weeks are the period when mature colonies release fertile queens — the point at which this year's nest count either stalls or seeds next spring's spread further north. Defra and the Welsh government fund the National Bee Unit's surveillance and destruction programme, but a national public-awareness campaign of the kind the BBKA wants would require a policy decision, not just charitable mobilisation. Whether ministers respond before next season starts will likely determine whether 2027's nest count looks like a plateau or another jump.
