VIX logs longest calm streak since 1992

Trading on the floor of the New York Stock Exchange. — Wikimedia Commons / NIST (public domain)
In one sentence
The VIX has held between 14 and 17 for 25 straight sessions, nearing the longest such calm streak since 1992 despite Fed and geopolitical uncertainty.

The VIX has done something almost nobody trading today has seen it do before: it has stopped moving. For 25 straight sessions through Friday, the CBOE Volatility Index — Wall Street's standard gauge of expected market turbulence — has stayed locked between 14 and 17, an unusually tight corridor for an index whose entire purpose is to spike. One more session in-band and it ties the modern record; a second would make it the longest such stretch since May 1992, when the streak ran 26 days.

That 1992 comparison is doing a lot of work here. It predates the 2008 financial crisis, the 2010 flash crash, the 2018 "Volmageddon" blowup, the 2020 pandemic collapse and the 2022 rate-shock year — all of which reset expectations for how calm markets are supposed to get. To match that calm now, with the S&P 500 at record highs, the Federal Reserve mid-debate on rate cuts, and geopolitical flashpoints from the Strait of Hormuz to Eastern Europe still live, is either a genuine sign of a well-absorbed set of risks or a warning that traders have simply stopped pricing them.

The VIX has stayed between 14 and 17 for 25 straight trading days — one day shy of the May 15, 1992, record of 26 days.

Why the calm looks unusual

Seasonality is the first flag. The VIX's median level since 1990 sits around 16.5 in late August, climbs toward 18 by mid-September and reaches roughly 19 by early October — precisely the stretch strategists call the market's stormiest calendar window. The index spent much of that window this year at the bottom of its historical range rather than climbing into it; it touched 14.13 in late August, its low for 2026.

The futures curve tells a related story. September VIX futures were pricing around 17.92 and December contracts near 20.38 — both comfortably above the spot level, an unusually steep contango that signals traders expect the calm to break, just not yet. Jonathan Krinsky, head of market strategy at BTIG, put the concern in seasonal terms: markets are entering "a window that historically sees downside volatility... with the market at all-time highs and VIX at YTD lows," adding that history counsels against getting too comfortable during the worst stretch of the calendar in mid-term election years.

The mechanics behind the number

Part of the explanation is structural rather than sentimental. Institutional "vol-sellers" — funds and desks that systematically short volatility to harvest option premium as yield — have been doing so aggressively this year, and that selling is mechanically self-reinforcing: it suppresses the VIX, which encourages more selling, which suppresses it further. That flow doesn't reflect a considered view that risk has fallen; it reflects a strategy that only works while the trend holds, and unwinds violently when it doesn't. The 2018 Volmageddon episode remains the textbook case of that unwind.

Meanwhile the news flow the VIX is supposedly ignoring hasn't been quiet. The Fed remains split over the pace of further cuts even as rate-cut odds hold near 70%, gilt yields sit at multi-decade highs across the Atlantic, and sabotage incidents across Europe have kept geopolitical risk premia elsewhere elevated. None of it has moved the option market's pricing of near-term S&P swings.

What comes next

The streak's fate is a binary, near-term data point: another closing print inside 14–17 on Monday ties the 1992 record, and Tuesday would break it. Beyond that, the more consequential test is whether the crowded short-volatility trade holds through the historically volatile mid-September-to-October window — and whether the next genuine shock, whenever it lands, finds a market that has quietly stopped paying for insurance against it.
