Burnham and Macron agree to scale up joint action on Channel boats

Andy Burnham, photographed at 10 Downing Street shortly after taking office, hosted Emmanuel Macron for his first bilateral talks as prime minister. — Alecsandra Dragoi / No 10 Downing Street (OGL v3.0)
In one sentence
Burnham and Macron agreed to expand a French-funded 45-officer 'surge team' against XXL smuggling boats, even as summer Channel crossings hit a seven-year low.

Emmanuel Macron's first call on Britain's new prime minister produced a familiar kind of announcement: more money, more officers, more boats intercepted before they reach open water. Meeting Andy Burnham at Downing Street on Thursday — a day after the two men stood alongside King Charles at a preview of the Bayeux Tapestry exhibition — the French president agreed to expand a UK-funded French policing operation aimed at the Channel's newest problem: XXL smuggling boats capable of carrying more than 200 people at a time.

"It's clearly working," Burnham said of the wider UK-France arrangement. "The progress we have seen this summer shows what can be achieved when the UK and France work together."

The figures behind that claim are real. Small boat crossings between June and August fell to their lowest summer total since 2019 — 102 boats carried 7,371 people, against a 2025 season that ran far higher. August arrivals dropped 46% year-on-year, the lowest for that month since records began in 2018; July fell 64% against the same month last year. The Home Office credits a three-year, roughly £660m deal signed with Paris in April, which it says has prevented more than 48,000 attempted crossings.

The new surge team

What Burnham and Macron agreed to this week is an addition to that deal, not a replacement. France will deploy a fresh contingent of around 45 specialist police officers to beaches west of Calais, equipped with drones and aerial surveillance, riot gear and crowd-control training. Layered on top of the roughly 1,050 officers France already has patrolling the coast, the surge team pushes combined resourcing in the area close to tripling since the initiative began.

The target is specific: smugglers who have responded to tighter policing not by giving up but by packing more people onto fewer, larger vessels. Macron and Burnham both pointed to the emergence of these "mega-dinghies" as the reason enforcement needs to keep scaling even as headline crossing numbers fall. "We cannot be complacent," Burnham said. "Smuggling gangs are constantly adapting." Macron, for his part, called the relationship "very strong" and "extremely important" to him personally — diplomatic language, but notable for a leader making his first visit to a British prime minister who has been in office less than two months.

Continuity, at a rising price

For Burnham, the substance matters less than the signal. Channel enforcement was Labour policy before he took over in July, and nothing announced this week changes its shape — only its scale. That continuity is presumably the point: a new prime minister, untested on immigration, needed to show voters and his own party that switching leaders did not mean switching strategy.

The harder question, largely unaddressed in Thursday's exchanges, is one of diminishing returns. Crossings are already down to a seven-year low under the April deal's terms. Scaling up spending now — after the metric being targeted has already improved sharply — invites scrutiny of whether the marginal officer, drone or boat intercepted is worth what it costs, or whether the surge team is better understood as insurance against smugglers' next adaptation than as a response to a current shortfall. Tripling the officer count in one stretch of coast, funded by London, is not a small commitment for a policy area that competes for the same public spending headlines as gilts, welfare and NHS waiting lists.

What's next

The surge team is expected to deploy within weeks; whether it holds August's 46% year-on-year drop through the autumn, when crossings historically pick up again as smugglers adjust to enforcement gaps, will be the first real test of whether the extra spending buys anything the existing deal wasn't already delivering.
