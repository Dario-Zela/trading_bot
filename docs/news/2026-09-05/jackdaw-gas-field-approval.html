Shell's Jackdaw gas field set for UK approval within weeks

An offshore oil and gas platform in the North Sea, where Jackdaw sits among a cluster of Adura-operated fields awaiting fresh consent. — Erik Christensen / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Ministers are expected to approve Shell and Equinor's Jackdaw gas field within weeks, testing how far Labour-turned-Burnham government climate pledges bend under energy security pressure.

Sometime in the next fortnight, Miatta Fahnbulleh will sign a piece of paper that has been four years, one change of government and one Scottish court ruling in the making. The energy secretary is expected to grant fresh consent for Shell and Equinor's Jackdaw gas field, 150 miles east of Aberdeen, clearing the way for first gas from a project that has already been approved once, blocked once, and become an unlikely proxy war over what Labour's climate promises actually mean once it holds office.

Government sources cited by the BBC and confirmed by trade press including New Civil Engineer say an announcement is likely in mid-September, before Parliament rises for party conference season. A spokesperson previously dismissed reports of an imminent green light as "unfounded speculation," the standard hedge ministers use for decisions they haven't finished making. Markets are pricing it as done: Shell shares are trading close to a 2026 high, up roughly 25% year-to-date, after Morgan Stanley upgraded the stock to Overweight this month and lifted its price target to 3,780p, citing — among other things — clearer production visibility from precisely this kind of approval.

What Jackdaw actually is

Jackdaw is a condensate gas field operated by Adura, the joint venture Shell and Equinor completed this year to combine their UK North Sea assets into what is now the basin's largest independent producer. The field itself represents roughly £2.1bn of investment, three-quarters of it spent in the UK, and Adura says it will supply 6-6.5% of UK Continental Shelf gas production at peak — enough, the company says, to heat 1.4 million homes — while accounting for less than 1% of UKCS emissions. Environmental campaigners dispute the output figure, putting it closer to 2%, and Greenpeace has calculated lifetime emissions from burning the gas at 35.8 million tonnes of CO2 equivalent, close to a full year of Scotland's total emissions.

Both numbers are true in the sense that both sides mean different things by them: Adura is talking about UK production share, campaigners about global demand displacement. Neither figure has moved much since the row began.

The court did this once already

Jackdaw was first waved through by the Conservative government in 2022, under then-business secretary Kwasi Kwarteng, alongside the much larger Rosebank oil field. Both consents were quashed in January 2025, when the Court of Session in Edinburgh ruled — in a case brought by Greenpeace and Uplift — that regulators had failed to account for the downstream, or "Scope 3," emissions produced when the extracted fuel is eventually burned. That followed a UK Supreme Court ruling in the unrelated Finch case, which established the same principle for onshore oil development in Surrey. Adura and the operators of Rosebank have since resubmitted environmental statements addressing lifecycle emissions, which is the legal box ministers now say has been ticked.

Tessa Khan, executive director of Uplift, isn't persuaded. "The science is clear that the world already has far more oil and gas than can ever be safely burned if we are to limit warming," she said, arguing Jackdaw would do "precious little" for UK energy bills or supply. A renewed legal challenge on the adequacy of the new environmental statement is a live possibility if approval goes ahead.

A different government than the one that campaigned

The politics have moved as much as the law. Labour's 2024 manifesto promised no new exploration licences for undeveloped fields — a pledge that, strictly read, doesn't cover Jackdaw or Rosebank, both of which hold licences dating from well before the election and are seeking renewed consent, not new permission to drill. Ministers have leaned on that distinction throughout, though it has done little to quiet accusations of a climbdown.

Ed Miliband, energy secretary until July, once called Rosebank "a colossal waste of taxpayer money and climate vandalism" from opposition, then spent much of his time in office declining to say when — or whether — he'd approve it. He no longer has to decide. Keir Starmer resigned in July after a leadership crisis, and Andy Burnham, until then mayor of Greater Manchester, became prime minister with backing from 379 of 401 Labour MPs. Burnham moved Miliband to the Foreign Office and installed Miatta Fahnbulleh, the MP for Peckham and former New Economics Foundation chief executive, as energy secretary. It is Fahnbulleh, not Miliband, who now signs off on Jackdaw — a reminder that a change of principal can do as much to a policy as a change of law.

What it unlocks, and what it doesn't

Jackdaw's approval is widely read in the industry as a bellwether for Rosebank, the £8.7bn North Sea oil project awaiting its own decision in the coming months, and beyond that for a pipeline of 111 offshore projects that Offshore Energies UK says are economically viable and together represent 3.25 billion barrels of oil and gas reserves. OEUK has argued the combined Jackdaw-Rosebank investment of £10.8bn would generate £1.4bn in tax revenue by the end of this Parliament and £3.8bn by 2034. The backdrop helps the case: UK petrol prices are at their highest since the Iran war began in February, a visible reminder to any government of what happens when energy security arguments collide with climate ones.

For Shell and Equinor, the calculus is more straightforward. Adura already holds stakes across a dozen North Sea fields, including Rosebank itself, and secured a $3bn lending facility on the expectation that these assets start paying dividends rather than absorbing capital. A Jackdaw approval doesn't resolve the argument over whether new North Sea gas serves energy security or entrenches emissions Britain has pledged to cut — it simply tells you which side of that argument currently holds the pen. The next test comes with Rosebank, where the sums, and the emissions, are considerably larger.
