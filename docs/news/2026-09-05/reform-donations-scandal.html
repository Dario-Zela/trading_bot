Reform UK donations scandal widens as aides quit and police probe opens

Nigel Farage, whose party faces a police referral over an alleged foreign-funded polling arrangement. — Laurie Noble / UK Parliament, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
Two senior Reform UK aides quit after a sting showed them discussing foreign-funded polling, triggering a police probe as Farage's conference goes ahead under a cloud.

Two of Nigel Farage's closest lieutenants have quit Reform UK, London's Metropolitan Police have confirmed they are looking into the party's donation records, and Cambridge University has opened its own inquiry into one of the men — all within the space of a week that was supposed to be devoted to Reform's annual conference.

The trigger was an undercover investigation broadcast by Channel 4 News on 3 September, in which secretly filmed footage appeared to show James Orr, Reform's head of policy, and Dan Jukes, a long-serving Farage aide, discussing how a foreign backer might fund political polling for the party without it showing up as a foreign donation — something British electoral law flatly prohibits. Both men resigned within hours of the broadcast. By the following day, Reform was facing referrals to police from both the Liberal Democrats and the governing Labour Party, and a criminal investigation had been opened by the Met into a suspected breach of the Political Parties, Elections and Referendums Act 2000.

What the sting caught

The operation was run by a company called Verbatim, a vehicle set up in July by the Centre for Climate Reporting, whose journalists posed as an American financier and his UK-based son looking to support Reform. In the footage, Orr is heard discussing commissioning polling that would be organised by Reform but paid for by a US company said to belong to the fake donor's father — an arrangement that, if it had gone ahead as described, would sidestep the ban on foreign money in British politics. Jukes was separately filmed suggesting ways to route donations from abroad. Rival parties allege the arrangement covered three polls commissioned earlier this year at a cost of roughly £32,500.

Bob Posner, a former chief executive of the Electoral Commission, said the allegations, if substantiated, could amount to "serious criminal offences" warranting a full police investigation rather than a party-run inquiry. Reform disputes that any money actually changed hands and says it is cooperating with its own internal review, which is why Orr and Jukes stepped aside "pending the outcome." The party has also sought to discredit the sting itself, noting that Verbatim's founder, Lawrence Carter, has previously been criticised in the United States Congress over undercover footage judged to have been edited in ways that left an incomplete record of conversations — an argument Farage leaned on hard when he called the whole affair "a prolonged exercise in entrapment."

Defiance at a conference already going badly

The timing could scarcely have been worse for Reform, which convened its conference this week already nursing a slide in the polls. Support for the party has fallen roughly ten points from its peak a year ago, dropping it into third place behind Labour and the Conservatives for the first time since April 2025, according to recent tracking polls. Only 19% of Britons now see Farage as a prime minister in waiting, down seven points since June, and even a quarter of Reform's own voters doubt he has the job in him.

Farage used his keynote to go on the offensive rather than apologise. "The party has done nothing wrong. The party has taken no illegal money — in fact, no money was taken at all," he told delegates, dismissing the idea that Reform had previously taken disguised foreign donations as "loose pub talk," while conceding that what Orr and Jukes said on camera was "injudicious at best." Delegates in the hall, by most accounts, took the row in stride, treating it as another instance of the media working against the party rather than a genuine scandal.

A widening perimeter

The episode does not sit in isolation. Orr, an associate professor of philosophy of religion at Cambridge's Faculty of Divinity who took unpaid leave to advise Farage, is now also the subject of a university conduct inquiry — "the University is looking into these matters," a spokesperson said — over whether discussing ways to obscure a donation's origin is compatible with his academic post. Separately, the Electoral Commission is still investigating whether Farage should have declared a £5 million personal gift from the cryptocurrency financier Christopher Harborne in 2024, on top of more than £25 million Harborne has given the party. Two scandals about the same underlying question — where Reform's money actually comes from — are now running in parallel, each lending the other more weight than it would carry alone.

What happens next

The near-term test is procedural rather than dramatic: whether the Met opens a formal criminal investigation, and whether the Electoral Commission moves from watching brief to enforcement action on either the polling arrangement or the Harborne gift. Neither outcome is close to certain, and Reform's defence — that this was a stitch-up by campaigners with their own record of selective editing — gives it real room to survive the news cycle largely intact, as the conference crowd's shrug suggests it already has politically. But a party polling third, whose leader's personal credibility is sliding, has less capacity to absorb a slow-burning police inquiry into how it is funded than one riding high. Any move by regulators to tighten scrutiny of foreign money in British politics — beyond this one case — would be the signal worth watching, since it would mark a shift in how seriously the state treats a loophole that, until this week, most voters had never heard of.
