Fed rate-cut bets hold near 70% despite jobs whipsaw and hawkish dissent

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C., headquarters of the central bank weighing its September rate decision. — AgnosticPreachersKid / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
A mixed delayed jobs report (119,000 added, unemployment at 4.4%) left traders pricing a 70% chance the Fed holds rates, despite hawkish Fed dissent and fresh Trump pressure for cuts.

The jobs data that was supposed to settle the argument over the Federal Reserve's next move has instead confirmed both sides of it. A delayed non-farm payrolls report showed employers added 119,000 positions, comfortably ahead of the meagre gains private-sector trackers had flagged days earlier — but the unemployment rate climbed to 4.4%, its highest reading in years. Traders looked at the mix, shrugged, and left their positioning almost exactly where it started: futures markets are pricing roughly a 70% probability that the Federal Open Market Committee holds rates steady when it meets on September 15–16.

That stability is notable given how much the picture wobbled to get there. In the days before the payrolls release, weaker signals — ADP's private-payrolls estimate came in around 38,000, below already-modest forecasts, while weekly jobless claims rose to roughly 206,000 — had briefly revived hopes of an imminent cut. The delayed non-farm print undercut that narrative just enough to restore the status quo: firm enough job creation to keep the Fed patient, soft enough labour-market slack to keep cut bets alive for later in the year.

A dissenter with contacts in Cincinnati

The case for staying put — or even tightening further — has an unusually vocal advocate inside the building. Cleveland Fed president Beth Hammack has spent recent weeks making the hawkish argument as plainly as any sitting policymaker: "Policy is not restrictive. Inflation is too high — and the longer it stays above our objective, the harder it will be to bring it back down."

Hammack has grounded that view in anecdote rather than just modelling. In a LinkedIn post, she described a Cincinnati retailer raising prices pre-emptively and a Northeast Ohio manufacturer facing double-digit inflation in input costs — contacts, she said, who are telling her the Fed should be raising rates, not cutting them. She has also pointed to workers with steady jobs still relying on food banks, evidence, in her telling, that inflation rather than employment is the district's live wound. It is a striking position to stake out publicly ahead of a meeting where market pricing still leans toward a hold rather than a hike, and it underscores that the 70% figure traders have settled on is a probability, not a consensus — Hammack's remarks are a reminder that the committee itself is not of one mind.

Political pressure, indifferent to the data

The numbers did not go unnoticed at the other end of Pennsylvania Avenue. Within hours of the jobs figures crossing wires, President Trump posted on Truth Social that "High interest rates put the U.S.A. at a very unfair disadvantage, and I won't allow that to happen!" — followed by a blunter escalation: "LOWER THE RATE OR I'LL STOP TRADING WITH COUNTRIES WITH WHICH WE HAVE A DEFICIT."

What makes the intervention notable is its indifference to the underlying data. Trump's demand for lower rates has been a fixture regardless of whether the latest print reads hawkish or dovish, which strips it of much informational value for traders trying to price the meeting — it is pressure as a constant, not a signal. Fed officials have long maintained that the committee sets policy independent of the White House, and nothing in the September pricing suggests markets are treating the posts as a genuine input into the rate path. If anything, the more relevant tension is the one inside the building, between Hammack's hawkishness and a labour market that is cooling by enough to keep the doves in the conversation.

What the 70% actually means

Put together, the picture is less a story of decisive data than of a market that has absorbed a whipsaw and landed close to where it stood beforehand. A weak run of private indicators, a mixed official payrolls report, an internal hawkish dissent and an unrelated political broadside have all pulled in different directions — and the net effect has been remarkably little net movement in what the Fed is expected to do in two weeks. That is arguably the most useful signal in the whole episode: with the data ambiguous enough to support either a hold or the beginning of a cutting cycle, the committee has effectively been handed cover to do the cautious thing.

The next hard catalyst is the FOMC decision itself on September 16, when the post-meeting statement and Chair press conference will show whether Hammack's hawkish contacts carry any weight in the room, or whether the committee's median view has already drifted toward easing later this year. Between now and then, another round of labour and inflation data — plus any further posts from the White House — will test whether that 70% holds, or whether it was simply the market's best guess in a genuinely unsettled moment.
