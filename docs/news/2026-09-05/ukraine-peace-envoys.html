Witkoff and Kushner head to Moscow and Kyiv with new Trump peace push

Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff meet Vladimir Putin at the Kremlin during an earlier round of talks in December 2025. — kremlin.ru / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
Witkoff and Kushner meet Putin Saturday and Zelensky Sunday with an undisclosed 'concrete' peace plan, days after Russia struck Kyiv's SBU headquarters.

Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner are about to do something the Trump administration's Ukraine diplomacy has not attempted before: show up in Moscow and Kyiv in the same weekend, carrying, in the president's words, a "concrete proposal" to end the war. Witkoff meets Vladimir Putin in Moscow on Saturday; Kushner joins him for the leg to Kyiv on Sunday to see Volodymyr Zelensky, according to officials cited by CNN, Axios and Al Jazeera. It will be the pair's first joint visit to the Ukrainian capital since Trump returned to office.

Trump told reporters he'd sent his two envoys "to see whether or not we can get something done," adding, with characteristic understatement about the two leaders he is trying to align: "President Zelenskyy and President Putin truly hate each other." He did not describe what the proposal contains. That omission is the story. Everything about this trip — the synchronized itinerary, the vague-but-confident language, the timing — reads as stagecraft for a negotiation whose substance nobody outside the room has yet seen.

A process that already failed once

This is not the first attempt. Through 2025, the Trump administration pushed a settlement built around territorial concessions, security guarantees and a postwar reconstruction package — the same broad architecture Witkoff and Kushner discussed in a five-hour Kremlin session with Putin last December. That framework collapsed by early this year, and the diplomatic track has been largely dormant since, with Putin himself describing talks as "frozen."

The underlying positions have not moved much in the interim, which is why the trip is being read with caution rather than optimism in Kyiv. Russia's core demands remain that Ukraine cede the territory Russian forces currently hold, abandon its NATO ambitions, and substantially reduce the size of its military. Ukrainian officials regard those terms as a capitulation dressed up as a settlement, and several have told reporters they are skeptical a workable deal is achievable before next spring — a timeline that implies a harder winter first, with intensified Russian strikes on the power grid, rather than a near-term ceasefire.

The visit itself is already contested

The run-up has not been quiet. On Friday, a Russian drone struck the Kyiv headquarters of Ukraine's SBU security service in daylight — the first direct hit on the building in four and a half years of war — injuring twelve people. Zelensky said the drone had been aimed at the office of SBU chief Oleksandr Poklad, who was not in the building at the time. Ukraine's foreign minister, Andrii Sybiha, called it out directly: "Moscow launched this strike during a renewed push for peace — laying bare its true rejection of diplomacy."

Zelensky, for his part, has said Ukraine will hold off on strikes for the duration of the envoys' visit and has called on Moscow to reciprocate. "We need to look for real options for how to move this war toward an end," he wrote on X confirming the Sunday meeting. Whether that restraint holds through the weekend is itself a signal worth watching — a quiet 48 hours would suggest both sides see some value in not sabotaging the trip before it starts; continued strikes would suggest one side already views it as theater.

What the tactical shift actually signals

Prior rounds of this diplomacy ran as shuttle missions — envoys ricocheting between capitals over days or weeks, with positions relayed rather than confronted. Compressing Moscow and Kyiv into consecutive days is a different posture: it forces the American side to reconcile two incompatible sets of demands in near-real time rather than carrying vague assurances back and forth. That can mean Washington believes it finally has language both sides might accept. It can equally mean the administration wants a fast, visible answer — yes or no — after nearly a year of a process that produced neither.

For markets and regional risk pricing, the visits themselves are not the datapoint. The war has run for more than four and a half years, with Russian losses estimated near 450,000 and Ukrainian losses in the 125,000–150,000 range — a grinding toll that has repeatedly reset expectations for how this ends. What matters now is whether Putin or Zelensky, after their respective meetings, signal any actual movement on territory, security guarantees or a ceasefire mechanism — or whether both simply restate positions and the envoys go home with photographs but no framework. The SBU strike suggests at least one side is entering the weekend unpersuaded that diplomacy is the operative channel.

What's next

The substantive test lands quickly: statements out of the Kremlin after Saturday's Putin meeting, and out of Kyiv after Sunday's Zelensky meeting, should show within days whether the "concrete proposal" contains anything Moscow and Kyiv haven't already rejected. Watch also for whether Ukraine's strike pause extends beyond the visit itself — an informal de-escalation signal would be the first tangible output of the trip, concrete proposal or not.
