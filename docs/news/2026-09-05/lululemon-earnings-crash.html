Lululemon shares crash 17% as tariff pressure clouds recovery path

A Lululemon storefront in New York. The retailer's stock posted its worst one-day fall since the pandemic after a September 4 earnings report. — Ajay Suresh / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Lululemon shares fell 17% after tariffs and a scrapped duty exemption carved $240m from profit atop a fifth straight quarter of falling North American sales.

In one sentence: Lululemon's stock fell 17% after Q2 earnings showed tariffs and a de minimis exemption repeal carving out roughly $240 million in profit, compounding a five-quarter North American sales slide.

Lululemon Athletica lost 17% of its market value on September 4, its third double-digit sell-off of the year, after a second-quarter earnings report confirmed what investors had begun to suspect: the athleisure retailer's problems are not a one-quarter stumble but a slower-moving squeeze between a domestic sales slump and a tariff bill that keeps growing. The stock closed at $100.49, capping a year in which shares are down roughly 56% — the worst performance in the S&P 500.

The headline numbers looked bad enough on their own. Second-quarter revenue fell 4% year-over-year to $2.4 billion, missing the $2.46 billion analysts expected, while comparable sales dropped 9-10% globally. Look under the hood, though, and the story sharpens: North American comparable sales fell 12%, marking a fifth consecutive quarterly decline in the company's home market, while sales in mainland China — long Lululemon's growth engine — fell for the first time since the company began reporting the region separately in 2023. Adjusted earnings per share of $2.92 beat the $1.82 consensus, but $0.86 of that beat came from a one-time tariff refund; strip it out and the underlying number was closer to $2.06.

A tariff bill that won't go away

The more structural problem sits in the cost base rather than the top line. Lululemon executives said tariffs and the elimination of the de minimis import exemption are set to erode roughly $240 million from this fiscal year's gross profit. The exemption, which let e-commerce shipments under $800 enter the U.S. duty-free, was scrapped by the Trump administration on August 29 — a change that hit Lululemon harder than most retailers because the company fulfills about two-thirds of its U.S. online orders from distribution centers in Canada. Every one of those parcels now clears customs like a commercial import.

That is the crux of the Barron's framing on this quarter: tariffs are not a transitory expense that fades once new supplier contracts kick in, they are a cost structure the company now has to design around — new fulfillment centers, renegotiated sourcing, possibly higher prices in an environment where the brand is already discounting more than it used to. Guidance reflects the strain. Lululemon cut its full-year revenue outlook to $10.35–$10.5 billion, down from $11.0–$11.15 billion just a quarter earlier, and slashed full-year EPS guidance to $9.48–$9.73 from $10.95–$11.15. Third-quarter revenue is expected to shrink more than 10%. JPMorgan cut its price target from $154 to $95 and moved to neutral; Bernstein cut from $145 to $115 and shifted to market perform.

The brand problem underneath the trade problem

Tariffs alone don't explain a fifth straight quarter of North American declines. Interim co-CEO Meghan Frank pointed to "negative commentary in media and social channels" weighing on store traffic, and acknowledged a "softer than planned response to some new product launches,” particularly in the core women's bottoms category that built the brand. Rivals have noticed the opening: Alo Yoga and Vuori, both younger and more nimble, have each added roughly a point of activewear market share over the past year and increasingly plant stores next to Lululemon's own. Across comparable store bases tracked by industry analysts, Alo grew visits 7.5% year-over-year and Vuori 1.9%, while Lululemon's fell 3.1%.

The company is also mid-transition at the top. Calvin McDonald, who ran Lululemon for seven years, stepped down at the end of January under pressure from founder Chip Wilson and activist investor Elliott Investment Management, which built a stake exceeding $1 billion and pushed for change. A proxy fight with Wilson was settled in May with two of his board nominees seated. Heidi O'Neill, a 25-year Nike veteran who most recently ran its consumer and brand division, was named CEO in April and formally starts September 8 — four days after this earnings report landed. She inherits a company simultaneously fighting a trade-policy headwind and a product-relevance problem, with no obvious quick fix for either.

A tale of two tapes

The same trading session that punished Lululemon lifted SanDisk, whose shares jumped roughly 9-10% — not on anything related to apparel or tariffs, but on Nvidia's confirmed $12.9 billion acquisition of Hugging Face, which investors read as a bullish signal for memory-chip demand tied to AI infrastructure. The juxtaposition was coincidental in cause but instructive in effect: capital rotated toward an AI-adjacent momentum story on the same day it fled a consumer-discretionary bellwether facing a cost shock with no clean resolution. It is a small illustration of a broader pattern this year — a market willing to look past thin justification for AI-linked rallies while showing little patience for legacy consumer names whose margin math has genuinely gotten harder.

That asymmetry is the real takeaway for anyone watching premium retail more broadly. Lululemon built its valuation on the idea that brand strength and pricing power would insulate it from exactly this kind of shock. The tariff bill says otherwise: a $240 million hit lands the same whether the brand commands a premium or not, and it arrives at the same time as a domestic sales slide the company has yet to explain convincingly. Investors will get their next real read on whether either problem is easing when Lululemon reports third-quarter results in early December — by which point Heidi O'Neill will have had a full quarter to show whether new leadership can do anything a trade policy shift cannot.
