China's export-led trade surge accelerates to 17.3% as domestic demand lags

Container cranes at the Yangshan Deep-water Harbour Zone, part of the Port of Shanghai — a hub for the electromechanical exports now driving China's trade growth. — Alex Needham / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
China's exports surged 23.9% in July as electromechanical goods filled the gap left by a 0.6%-growth retail sector and a 19.2% property investment slump.

China's factories are doing the work its shoppers won't. Customs data released this month show goods trade for the first seven months of 2026 rose 17.3% year-on-year to 30.13 trillion yuan (about $4.46 trillion), a pace that has kept Beijing's growth target within reach even as the two engines that used to power Chinese demand — household spending and property — keep stalling. Exports alone climbed 14% over the same stretch to 17.44 trillion yuan, and within that, mechanical and electrical goods — industrial machinery, EVs, batteries, wind turbines, chips — did the heavy lifting: 11.12 trillion yuan of shipments, up 21.2%, now accounting for nearly two-thirds of everything China sells abroad.

The numbers behind the surge

The acceleration shows up most clearly in the monthly figures. July exports rose 23.9% year-on-year to $397.85 billion, beating forecasts, while imports climbed 27.7% to $285.35 billion — enough to produce a $112.5 billion trade surplus for the month, down from June's $125.6 billion but still comfortably above the roughly $107 billion economists had penciled in. Seven months into the year, the cumulative surplus stands at $687.37 billion. Some of that is timing: manufacturers have been front-loading shipments ahead of possible new US tariff action, a pattern that has repeated through much of the year. But the underlying trend — semiconductor and high-tech exports up more than 40% by value in July alone, according to customs figures — points to something more durable than a one-off rush to beat a deadline: a genuine gain in competitiveness in higher-value manufacturing, which is exactly the shift Beijing has been trying to engineer.

Demand at home stays parked

The trouble is what that shift has been substituting for. Retail sales rose just 0.6% year-on-year in July, missing a forecast of 1.5% and decelerating from June's already-soft 1%; growth over the first half of the year averaged a bare 0.7%. Fixed-asset investment fell 6.7% in the January–July period, with property investment down a steep 19.2% — the sector that once accounted for a quarter to a third of GDP, and with it the bulk of China's steel, copper and cement demand, is still contracting three years into its correction. Beijing has resisted the kind of large-scale stimulus that might revive it, wary of reinflating a debt bubble it spent years trying to deflate.

Households, meanwhile, are still saving rather than spending — more than 30% of disposable income, versus roughly half that rate across the OECD, largely because pensions, healthcare and unemployment cover remain thin. The OECD expects Chinese growth to slow to 4.5% this year and 4.3% in 2027, and estimates net exports contributed only about 0.8 percentage points to growth in the first half of 2026, down from 1.6 points — nearly a third of total growth — in 2025. Exports are carrying less of the load in relative terms even as their absolute value hits records, because the gap they're filling on the consumption side keeps widening.

The currency and trade-tension math

For traders, the immediate read-through is on the yuan. A trade surplus this size pushes exporters to convert dollar receipts into yuan, a flow that has kept upward pressure on the currency even as weak domestic demand argues for looser policy. Authorities have leaned on the usual toolkit — guiding the daily reference rate, adjusting bank reserve requirements, occasional state-bank selling — to keep the currency from appreciating too quickly and eroding the very export competitiveness driving the surplus. That tension gets harder to manage into September and October, seasonally strong months for Chinese exports.

The second read-through is political. The EU's goods trade deficit with China widened to roughly €360 billion in 2025 and is still rising, prompting Brussels to tighten steel-import and e-commerce rules this year, while Washington's tariff actions have already reshaped the composition — if not yet the total volume — of what China ships where. An export surge this size, arriving while Beijing's own currency management keeps the yuan from doing much of the adjusting, is the kind of pattern trading partners tend to read as competitive devaluation whether or not that's the intent. For commodity markets, the signal is more mixed than a headline 17.3% growth number suggests: the manufacturing side of China's economy is absorbing more copper, aluminium and specialty metals for EVs, batteries and grid equipment, while the property side that once dominated bulk demand for steel and cement keeps shrinking. Base-metals traders are increasingly pricing two different Chinas rather than one.

The next test lands within days: China's August trade figures are due from customs around September 8, the first full month of data since the latest round of US tariff and export-control moves took effect. A material slowdown from July's pace would suggest the front-loading story is fading and put more weight on the structural competitiveness argument; a repeat of July's strength would sharpen the currency and trade-friction questions further.
