Reform UK pledges £80bn in cuts and threatens Lords and courts overhaul

Nigel Farage, leader of Reform UK, in his official parliamentary portrait. — Laurie Noble / UK Parliament, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
Farage paired an unfunded £80bn cuts pledge with threats to pack the Lords or hold a referendum on it, and to curb judges' reliance on human-rights law.

Nigel Farage spent the weekend doing what Farage does best: saying the quiet part loud enough to fill a conference hall. At Reform UK's gathering in Birmingham, he didn't just outline a spending plan — he sketched out what happens to the institutions that might get in its way.

The headline arithmetic came first: £80bn a year in public spending cuts, most of it — £50bn — carved from welfare, with £10bn from scrapping net zero programmes, £8bn from shrinking the civil service and £7.1bn from capping the overseas aid budget. Economic spokesman Robert Jenrick, sounding every bit the fiscal disciplinarian, told delegates Reform would be the "party of fiscal responsibility" and would make "big changes... in a careful way, not a cack-handed way," while conceding tax cuts would have to wait until the party had "earned the trust" of the markets.

The numbers problem

That trust is precisely what's in question. The figures arrive with no departmental breakdown of where the axe actually falls, and independent economists have already warned that Reform's broader platform — mass income-tax exemptions, corporation tax cuts, a pledge to zero out NHS waiting lists — could blow a comparable hole in the public finances and risk a run on the pound. Farage has previously mused about scrapping the Office for Budget Responsibility altogether, an idea he says he's still giving "very serious thought," though Jenrick has since softened the line to protect the party's market credibility. An £80bn pledge without a costed ledger is not new in British politics; what's new is that it now shares a stage with talk of remaking Parliament's upper chamber.

Lords, courts, and a national emergency

Farage's institutional pitch was blunt. He told delegates that Parliament would "sit, if necessary, for 24 hours a day, for six days a week" to pass Reform's legislation, and pledged to declare a "national emergency" to clear away obstacles to his agenda. Top of that list: the House of Lords. If peers didn't "honour" a Reform government's manifesto pledges, Farage said he would appoint hundreds of new ones to secure a majority — and if that proved too slow, put the Lords' entire composition to a referendum, to be "swept away by a British public appalled by them."

The judiciary got similar treatment. Farage identified courts as a second potential obstacle and said he wants to strip judges of the ability to lean on international conventions such as the European Convention on Human Rights when ruling against government policy.

Why the two threads matter together

Taken separately, an unfunded spending pledge and a swipe at the Lords are standard conference-season theatre. Taken together, they describe something more specific: a party rehearsing what it would do when its numbers meet institutional resistance. Cutting £80bn without cross-party buy-in, or without a fully costed plan the OBR will sign off on, is the kind of policy that invites exactly the sort of scrutiny — from peers revising legislation, from courts testing it against human-rights law — that Farage now says he's prepared to override. The spending pledge supplies the motive; the Lords and courts threats supply the mechanism for pushing it through anyway.

For anyone pricing UK political risk, that's the signal worth isolating from the noise of conference rhetoric: it isn't just what Reform says it would cut, but which constraints on executive power it's willing to discard to cut it.

What's next

Reform's conference concludes against the backdrop of a widening donations scandal, after Channel 4 footage appeared to show senior figures discussing how to disguise foreign donations — a separate story testing the party's institutional discipline in the here and now, rather than in a hypothetical government. The next marker for the spending pledge will be whether Reform publishes a department-by-department costing before facing OBR-style scrutiny; the next marker for the institutional threats is simply whether the party's poll lead holds into an election where these promises would need to be kept.
