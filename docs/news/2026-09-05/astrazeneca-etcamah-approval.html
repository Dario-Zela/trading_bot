AstraZeneca wins FDA approval for breast cancer drug after panel snub

In one sentence
The FDA approved AstraZeneca's Etcamah for ESR1-mutated breast cancer despite its own panel's 6-3 vote against it, with AstraZeneca eyeing $5bn in sales analysts peg closer to $2.3bn.

AstraZeneca's oral breast cancer drug has cleared the US Food and Drug Administration — a reversal that few in the biotech world expected after the agency's own advisory panel voted against it three months ago. The drug, camizestrant, sold under the brand name Etcamah, received accelerated approval on September 4 for a specific genetic subtype of metastatic breast cancer, and AstraZeneca says it could eventually bring in more than $5 billion a year.[^1] That the approval came at all, given what preceded it, is the story.

A panel says no, the agency says yes

In June, the FDA's Oncologic Drugs Advisory Committee (ODAC) voted 6 to 3 against camizestrant, concluding that the evidence did not clear the bar for a "clinically meaningful benefit."[^2] The FDA delayed its decision by roughly three months to review additional data AstraZeneca submitted, including analyses tracking how quickly a molecular marker called circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) cleared from patients' blood after starting treatment, and how that clearance related to longer-term outcomes.[^3]

That delay ended this week with an approval the panel itself had recommended against. It is a rare sequence — advisory committees are consultative, not binding, but the FDA has historically aligned with ODAC's votes in the overwhelming majority of oncology cases. When it doesn't, the reasoning usually turns on exactly this kind of gap: a panel unconvinced that early intervention translates into benefits patients can feel, against internal reviewers persuaded by the raw numbers.

The indication is narrow by design. Etcamah, used alongside a CDK4/6 inhibitor (palbociclib, ribociclib, or abemaciclib), is approved for adults with hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative advanced or metastatic breast cancer who develop an ESR1 mutation — a resistance mechanism that emerges in roughly one in three patients on standard first-line hormonal therapy.[^4] Camizestrant is designed to work even after that mutation makes older endocrine drugs ineffective, and the drug is now the first cancer therapy the FDA has approved based on catching that resistance mutation in a routine blood draw, before a scan shows the tumor has actually grown.[^5]

"This marks the first FDA approval of a cancer therapy guided by the detection of a resistance mutation in circulating tumor DNA," said Dr. Angelo de Claro, director of the FDA's Oncology Center of Excellence.[^5]

What the panel didn't buy — and what the FDA did

The underlying dispute traces to the Phase 3 SERENA-6 trial, which randomized patients who developed an ESR1 mutation — but no visible disease progression — to either switch immediately to camizestrant or stay on their existing aromatase inhibitor until progression was confirmed on a scan. The results were striking on their face: median progression-free survival of 16.0 months on camizestrant versus 9.2 months on standard care, a 56% reduction in the risk of progression or death.[^6]

For ODAC's dissenters, though, a strong progression-free survival number wasn't the question. Their concern was whether switching drugs based on a blood test — ahead of any clinical sign the cancer was actually getting worse — meaningfully extended how long patients lived or felt well, as opposed to simply changing the calendar date on which a scan showed progression. Several members flagged that the trial wasn't designed to answer that harder question, and that treating earlier isn't automatically the same as treating better.[^2]

The FDA's own scientists evidently weighed that same tension differently, aided by the additional ctDNA-clearance data AstraZeneca supplied during the review delay. It granted approval under the accelerated pathway rather than a full approval — a distinction that matters because accelerated approvals are explicitly conditional. The agency is requiring AstraZeneca to run confirmatory studies verifying that the drug delivers real clinical benefit, with continued marketing rights contingent on those results.[^5] In effect, the FDA sided with the company's data package for now, while building in a mechanism to reverse course if the longer-term evidence doesn't hold up.

The market's more cautious math

AstraZeneca's public target — north of $5 billion in peak annual sales — sits well above what independent analysts have modeled. Consensus forecasts compiled ahead of the decision put Etcamah's 2032 sales closer to $2.3 billion, less than half the company's own projection.^7 That gap is not unusual for a newly approved oncology drug talking its own book, but it is unusually wide, and it says something about how differently AstraZeneca and outside analysts are pricing the addressable population: the label covers only patients who develop the ESR1 mutation while already on treatment, a slice of a slice of the overall HR-positive breast cancer market.

For investors who track ODAC votes as a leading indicator of approval odds — a reasonably reliable signal historically — this case is a reminder that the correlation is not a guarantee. Camizestrant is also notable competitively: it enters a market that already includes Menarini's elacestrant, an oral selective estrogen receptor degrader approved in 2023 for a similar ESR1-mutated population, meaning AstraZeneca's revenue thesis depends partly on taking share within that resistance-mutation niche rather than simply expanding it.

Etcamah already has EU approval in a broader first-line advanced breast cancer setting, cleared by European regulators without the same advisory drama that played out in Washington — a divergence that itself may be worth a separate look at how the two regulatory systems weighed the same underlying data.[^8]

What comes next

The confirmatory trials the FDA has ordered will determine whether this approval survives on a permanent basis, and AstraZeneca is also running additional studies of camizestrant in adjuvant and first-line settings that could widen the label considerably if they read out positively. Until those results land, the more immediate test is commercial: how quickly oncologists incorporate routine ctDNA monitoring into practice for HR-positive patients, since the entire premise of the approval depends on catching the ESR1 mutation before, not after, a scan shows the cancer advancing.
