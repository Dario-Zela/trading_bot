UK petrol prices hit highest level since Iran war began

Fuel pumps at a filling station forecourt — UK pump prices have climbed above 160p a litre as crude costs stay elevated. — Rama / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0 FR)
In one sentence
UK petrol has topped 160p a litre, its highest since the Iran war began, as Strait of Hormuz shipping disruption keeps crude near $96 a barrel.

UK petrol has pushed above 160 pence a litre for the first time since the early days of the Iran war, with average unleaded prices hitting roughly 162.5p and diesel around 184p, according to Fuel Finder's UK price index. The last time forecourt prices sat this high was in the panicked weeks after US and Israeli strikes on Iran shut down shipping through the Strait of Hormuz in late February — and the culprit now is the same as it was then: crude oil, not tax.

That distinction matters. The Treasury's planned 1p fuel duty rise, due this month, was scrapped by Downing Street earlier in the year, and the 5p cut introduced in March 2022 has been extended through December. Duty is frozen at 52.95p a litre. Every extra penny drivers are paying at the pump is a function of what refiners and wholesalers are charging for crude — and that number has been climbing for weeks.

Hormuz still barely moving

Brent crude settled at $96.28 a barrel this week, up 7.6% on the week and nearly 60% higher than at the start of the year, according to trading data reported by The National. The proximate cause is fresh fighting between US and Iranian forces that has kept the Strait of Hormuz — the conduit for roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil — running at a fraction of its normal traffic. Ship-tracking platform MarineTraffic recorded just four tankers transiting the strait on one day this week, against a ten-day average of around 15 and a pre-conflict norm closer to 130. Citi has raised its third-quarter Brent forecast to $86 a barrel from $80, citing delays in reopening the strait; ANZ has pushed its short-term call to $95.

The knock-on effects are visible well beyond the UK. US diesel hit a record national average of $5.85 a gallon this week, and European diesel stockpiles are running well below seasonal norms, a sign that the supply strain is not confined to crude but is squeezing refined products too.

Drivers exposed again

The RAC has tracked the swing closely. In July, its head of policy Simon Williams warned that "fuel prices are shooting up like a rocket on the back of oil being above $90," after unleaded rose from around 150p to 153.46p in the space of ten days. Diesel at the time had climbed 4p to 168.55p. Both figures now look tame next to today's levels. Williams's broader point from earlier in the summer — that the episode shows "just how exposed UK drivers are to global events affecting the cost of oil" — has aged well; it is essentially the story of the past six months compressed into one sentence.

That exposure runs in both directions. Diesel had posted its biggest-ever monthly drop as recently as June, when a ceasefire briefly held and tanker traffic through Hormuz began to recover. The reversal since has been just as sharp, underlining how thin the buffer is between calm and crisis in a market where a fifth of global supply depends on a 33-kilometre channel between Iran and Oman.

What it means for winter

For households, the arithmetic is blunt: a driver filling a 55-litre family car is now paying roughly £89 at the pump, against closer to £84 in July and under £80 before the conflict began. For energy-linked equities and transport-heavy sectors — airlines, hauliers, anyone with diesel-fuelled fleets — the relevant question is less the headline price than its persistence. A crude spike that fades in weeks is a rounding error; one that holds through the northern-hemisphere winter, when heating-oil and jet-fuel demand compete with road fuel for the same barrels, is a cost baseline that shows up in margins.

The next marker to watch is the state of US-Iran negotiations over reopening the strait, alongside any fresh OPEC+ signalling on output. Citi's and ANZ's upgraded forecasts assume the disruption extends through the third quarter; a durable ceasefire would likely unwind much of the premium quickly, as it briefly did in June. Absent that, the Treasury's Autumn Budget decision on fuel duty beyond December — currently the only lever within London's control — becomes the more consequential date for UK drivers than anything OPEC does next.
