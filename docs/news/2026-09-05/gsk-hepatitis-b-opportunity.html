GSK's hepatitis B programme eyed as £2bn opportunity

In one sentence
Analysts are recirculating GSK's own £2bn peak-sales forecast for its hepatitis B drug — approved so far only in Japan, with no independent modelling behind the number.

GSK says its experimental hepatitis B drug bepirovirsen could bring in more than £2 billion a year at peak sales — a company forecast that a fresh round of analyst commentary has repackaged this week as evidence of a coming blockbuster. The claim is GSK's own, made when the drug won its first global approval in Japan on 28 August[^1]. What's new is less the number itself than the attention it is now getting, in a week when UK pharma has had good news to spare.

What the drug actually does

Bepirovirsen, to be sold as Hibsago, is designed to do something current hepatitis B treatment cannot: stop, rather than merely suppress, the virus. Standard care is a lifelong course of antivirals that keeps viral replication in check but rarely clears the infection — functional cure rates on existing therapy sit around 1%[^2]. In GSK's pooled B-Well phase 3 trials, 19% of patients who took a six-month course of bepirovirsen achieved a functional cure, rising to 26% in patients who started with lower levels of hepatitis B surface antigen, against 0% in the untreated comparison arm[^2]. Those results were published in the New England Journal of Medicine in May.

Japan's health ministry approved the drug under its SENKU fast-track scheme for innovative medicines, making Hibsago the first functional-cure treatment for chronic hepatitis B licensed anywhere[^1][^3]. It is only Japan, for now — GSK's US application is under FDA priority review with a decision expected in October, and a Chinese filing is also under review[^3][^4]. Roughly 250 million people worldwide live with chronic hepatitis B, most of them in Asia, which is why a Japan-first approval is more than a formality: it opens the largest addressable market before the drug has cleared either of the other two.

Where the £2bn figure comes from

The figure being circulated — including in the report that put it back in front of investors this week — is not an independent analyst estimate but GSK's own peak-sales guidance for the drug, restated alongside the Japan approval news[^1]. GSK has folded that forecast into a broader ambition of more than £40 billion in annual group revenue by 2031, a target it raised from £38 billion earlier this year[^5]. The company has also flagged that more than half of that 2031 number still depends on drugs already on the market, which makes newer pipeline bets like bepirovirsen important to the growth story but not yet load-bearing.

What is missing from the commentary doing the rounds is any independent sell-side modelling of the number — no disclosed pricing assumptions, no addressable-population estimate outside Japan, and no view on how a 19% cure rate might be priced or reimbursed against a cheaper, if less curative, standard of care. Until the FDA rules in October, the £2 billion remains a single company's internal projection for a drug approved in one market.

A strong week for UK pharma

The bepirovirsen story lands alongside AstraZeneca's FDA approval this week of camizestrant, sold as Etcamah, for a genetically defined form of metastatic breast cancer — a nod from the agency after its own advisory panel had voted against the drug. AstraZeneca is guiding to more than $5 billion in peak annual sales for that one. Two large-cap UK pharma names with two separate multibillion-pound forecasts in the same week is unusual, and it is doing some of the work of making GSK's number feel more solid than the evidence, on its own, supports.

The next real test is the FDA's bepirovirsen decision, due in October. A US approval would put a number on the table backed by the world's largest pharmaceutical market rather than Japan alone, and would give analysts an actual regulatory milestone to model against instead of a company's own peak-sales slide.
