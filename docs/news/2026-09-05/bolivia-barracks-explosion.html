At least 10 killed in explosion at Bolivian military barracks

In one sentence
A fireworks stockpile exploded at a Bolivian army base near La Paz, killing at least 10 and injuring 62, with seven still missing.

A stockpile of fireworks belonging to Bolivia's defence ministry detonated inside a military barracks in Viacha, a garrison town roughly 30 kilometres from La Paz, on Friday afternoon, killing at least 10 people and injuring more than 60. Seven people remained unaccounted for as rescue crews picked through the wreckage into the weekend, according to police.

The blast tore through the base at around 2:30pm local time, sending a column of black smoke over Viacha and shattering windows in homes hundreds of metres away. Rita Nebraska, the town's health director, said "between 10 and 15 people were believed to have died" as the toll was still being confirmed, while 62 people were treated for injuries — about 52 of them civilians caught by flying glass and debris rather than soldiers on the base itself.

A depot too close to a town

The material that detonated was pyrotechnic stock assigned to the armed forces — the kind of fireworks used in Bolivia's frequent civic and religious festivals — stored, apparently for some time, inside the Regimiento de Artillería Mecanizada 2 "Bolívar" facility that sits at the edge of Viacha's residential streets. Deputy Defence Minister Ernesto Justiniano said an investigation would look at "the cause of the explosions [and] how the pyrotechnic material was stored," a question that will be uncomfortable for a government already under pressure over basic competence.

Firefighters warned after the first blast that unburned material and heat sources remained on site, raising the risk of a second detonation, and told residents to stay at least 150 metres back — a warning that came too late for the homes already damaged. A girl with third-degree burns was among 14 of the wounded transferred to hospitals in La Paz.

A government already stretched thin

The timing lands awkwardly for President Rodrigo Paz, who took office in November 2025 and has spent much of the year managing a separate crisis: road blockades and protests over fuel-subsidy cuts that, by his own ombudsman's count, had already killed 14 people between May and June. Paz declared a state of emergency in June and pushed through a law restoring the military's authority to deploy in the streets alongside police — the same armed forces now under scrutiny for how they warehoused explosive material next to civilian housing.

None of this points to a crisis with cross-border reach. Bolivia's lithium reserves, the resource that occasionally puts the country on investors' radar, sit hundreds of kilometres away in the Salar de Uyuni and nothing in the reporting so far ties this incident to extraction, processing, or export infrastructure. There is no read-through to prices, no supply chain disrupted, no market moved. What the Viacha blast does is add one more data point — alongside the blockades, the subsidy fight, and the emergency decrees — to a picture of a state whose institutions, from fuel policy to munitions storage, are being tested and found wanting.

Investigators are expected to report initial findings on the cause and storage protocol in the coming days, and the final toll may still rise given the seven still missing. The more durable story is whether an administration already fighting to hold the streets can now also show it can secure its own barracks.
