Airbus shares rise as labour deal eases backlog pressure

Final assembly line 3 at an Airbus plant, similar to the Getafe, San Pablo and Tablada sites covered by the new labour deal. — DearEdward (Edward Russell) / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 2.0
In one sentence
Airbus shares rose after a preliminary deal eased a Spanish labour dispute — but a worker referendum, not the announcement, will decide if the risk is really gone.

Airbus shares edged up roughly 0.7% to $57.82 on Friday after the planemaker reached a preliminary labour agreement covering three Spanish plants, removing — for now — the strike threat that had shadowed its record order backlog through the summer.

The backlog itself is the number that matters: 9,222 aircraft as of June 30, an all-time high and, at current delivery rates, something like a decade of work already booked. For a company whose stock case rests almost entirely on converting that order book into cash, a five-week production shutdown at three sites was not a rounding error.

What was actually agreed

The deal, struck Thursday after direct intervention from Spain's Ministry of Industry and Tourism, covers the Getafe, San Pablo and Tablada sites — roughly 15,000 workers, about 8.9% of Airbus's global headcount. Industry Minister Jordi Hereu, who convened the talks after Spain's official mediation service (SIMA) failed to break the deadlock, called it "an excellent preliminary agreement in which workers come out ahead in their salary and working conditions" while preserving competitiveness. Airbus chief executive Guillaume Faury attended the session in Madrid.

On paper, the terms split the difference. Workers get a real-terms pay rise — CPI plus roughly 2 percentage points through 2028, tapering to CPI plus 1.95% in 2029, according to Spanish union reporting — plus a reported 7.6% wage adjustment and full retention of existing remote-working arrangements. Four unions — UGT, CCOO, ATP and SIPA — signed off on the preliminary text. None of that touches shift patterns, headcount, or the automation and tooling investment that actually determines how fast jets come off the line; the deal buys labour peace, not necessarily extra throughput.

Why it got this far

The dispute had teeth. CCOO had announced plans for an unlimited strike at Getafe starting after September 7 absent a resolution, and the five weeks of stoppages already recorded before Thursday's deal rippled well beyond Airbus's own payroll: roughly 150 supplier firms in Andalusia and some 16,400 jobs in the regional auxiliary industry were caught with parts they could build but not deliver. That is the mechanism by which a pay dispute at three Spanish sites becomes a market-moving story — Airbus can absorb a few weeks of disruption, but a components bottleneck compounds down the supply chain faster than it clears.

The catch

The agreement is preliminary. Workers still have to ratify it in a binding referendum, and Spanish labour disputes have stalled at this exact stage before. Until that vote is counted, the stock's modest pop reflects relief that a deal exists, not certainty that it holds. A no vote would put the production risk straight back where it was a week ago — only later in the year, with less runway before Airbus's roughly 870-jet delivery target for 2026 becomes mathematically difficult.

That target is the real test. Airbus delivered 351 aircraft in the first half; hitting 870 for the year requires the second half to run meaningfully hotter, a plan that had already assumed uninterrupted output from Getafe, San Pablo and Tablada. A ratified deal removes the labour variable from that equation. It does nothing to address the engine and structures supply constraints that have been the more persistent drag on the ramp-up all year.

What's next

The referendum date has not been made public; expect one within days given the September 7 strike deadline the deal was designed to pre-empt. Beyond that, Airbus's next scheduled checkpoint is its Q3 delivery and order update, which will show whether the second-half production ramp is actually accelerating or whether the backlog is simply getting older.
