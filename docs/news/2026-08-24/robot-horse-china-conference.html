Robot horse and rider steal the show at Chinese robotics conference

In one sentence
A Beijing startup's $43,000 rideable robot horse stole headlines at China's robot conference, but it's Unitree's Shanghai IPO surge that shows where real money is landing.

The horse walked in mechanical, deliberate steps across the exhibition floor in Beijing, a humanoid robot in the saddle, reins in its metal hands. It was not a stunt cooked up for a keynote — it was a product demo. The four-legged machine underneath, called the Qiji X1, is built by a Beijing startup named DaxAI Robotics, and as of this month you can buy one for 289,000 yuan, or about $43,000.

The scene, staged at the 2026 World Robot Conference, which opened in Beijing on 19 August and runs through the weekend, was the standout image from a five-day event drawing a record 300-plus exhibitors and, organisers say, 271,000 in-person visitors. It also happened to be exactly the kind of story that travels well and explains almost nothing: a robot horse is a better photograph than a balance sheet.

What the machine actually does

Strip away the novelty and the Qiji X1 is a load-carrying quadruped with a saddle bolted on. DaxAI, founded around 2025 by Wang Kun, a veteran of autonomous-driving and robotics work, says the X1 carries up to 300 kilograms at roughly 10 km/h, with a range of about 40 kilometres, and is built to cross gravel, mud and steep slopes that would stop a wheeled vehicle. A wheel-legged sibling, the XS, sells for 359,000 yuan (about $53,000). DaxAI pitches both for off-road haulage and emergency-rescue logistics — getting supplies, or people, over terrain a truck can't reach — rather than for anyone's daily commute. Even boosters describe the riding motion as noticeably stiff next to a real horse, which is the honest way to put it: this is a proof of concept with a price tag, not a finished consumer product.

The robot horse shared the spotlight with a related event a few days later, the World Humanoid Robot Games, where a humanoid perched on a similar mount drew a bow and released an arrow at the opening ceremony — closer to circus act than industrial showcase, and a reminder that this particular week in Beijing was built for spectacle as much as substance.

The context that actually moves money

The conference itself is not nothing: China says its humanoid makers shipped 90% of the world's units last year across more than 330 models, with first-quarter 2026 exports up 210% year-on-year. But the number that mattered to investors landed a few days before the horse did, when Unitree Robotics — the quadruped and humanoid maker best known for backflipping robot dogs — priced its Shanghai listing at 150.8 yuan a share, valuing the company near $9bn, and then surged as much as 629% on its trading debut. That is capital markets pricing in a robotics bet with real revenue behind it: Unitree reported 1.7bn yuan in 2025 sales and has delivered some 18,000 units. DaxAI, by contrast, has disclosed no funding round, no production numbers beyond the sticker price, and no filing of any kind.

What to watch

None of that makes the horse worthless as a signal — China's robotics sector is visibly scaling, and DaxAI's list price suggests it expects buyers beyond hobbyists, plausibly logistics or disaster-response agencies drawn by the terrain claims. But a demo lap around a convention hall is not a purchase order. The event to actually watch is Unitree's post-IPO run as the first humanoid-robot maker listed in mainland China, since its share price will now serve as the market's real-time verdict on how much of this industry's promise investors are willing to pay for today. The World Robot Conference closes this weekend; whether DaxAI ever discloses a funding round or a customer name is the next test of whether the horse is a business or just good theatre.
