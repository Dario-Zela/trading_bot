UK borrowing overshoots forecasts, squeezing Healey ahead of October Budget

Chancellor John Healey, whose first Budget on 28 October now faces a tighter fiscal squeeze than forecast. — Lauren Hurley / No 10 Downing Street (Open Government Licence)
In one sentence
July borrowing of £1.8bn against a forecast £500m surplus has cut Chancellor Healey's fiscal headroom to roughly £17bn ahead of his 28 October Budget.

Britain borrowed £1.8 billion more than it raised in July, when the government's own forecaster had expected a small surplus. The gap — roughly £2.3 billion against the Office for Budget Responsibility's £500 million surplus projection — is small in the context of a £2.8 trillion economy. But its timing is not. It lands nine weeks before Chancellor John Healey delivers his first Budget, and it confirms what bond investors have suspected since he took the job in July: the fiscal cushion he inherited is thinner than advertised, and getting thinner.

The Office for National Statistics said public sector net borrowing, excluding state-owned banks, came in at £1.8 billion in July, up £0.7 billion — nearly 69% — on the same month last year. Most City economists had pencilled in a broadly balanced month; the OBR's March forecast had gone further, projecting a £500 million surplus. Instead the Treasury ran a deficit. For the financial year to date, borrowing stands at £56.7 billion — £6 billion below the same point in 2025, but still £2.3 billion above where the OBR said it should be.

Where the money went — and came from

The ONS attributed the miss chiefly to spending, not weak receipts. Central government outlay on social benefits rose £2 billion year-on-year, and spending on goods and services — largely public-sector pay and procurement — rose £1.2 billion, both consistent with above-target inflation feeding through into index-linked budgets and wage settlements. Tax receipts, if anything, were the bright spot: self-assessed income tax hit a record for the month, and total income tax receipts reached £38.6 billion, £2.2 billion higher than a year earlier. July's PAYE and self-assessment haul of £17.1 billion was itself up £1.7 billion on 2025.

That split matters for how Healey frames the Budget. A revenue shortfall would point toward tax measures; a spending overshoot, driven substantially by inflation-linked commitments the Treasury does not fully control month to month, is harder to close with a single lever. Debt itself remains eye-watering by historical standards — public debt sits at roughly 94.1% of GDP, edging down slightly on an annual basis even as the cost of servicing it has topped £100 billion a year since 2022, a bill that rises automatically whenever gilt yields climb.

And gilt yields have climbed. Ten-year UK borrowing costs have held above 5% through most of August — a level last sustained before the financial crisis — and 30-year yields touched roughly 5.75%, their highest in two months, after Prime Minister Andy Burnham said on his first full day in office that he would look at "any flexibility" within the fiscal rules. Traders read that as a signal of looser borrowing discipline ahead of a Budget already under strain, and sold gilts accordingly. Every extra basis point on the curve compounds the arithmetic Healey has to solve on 28 October.

The headroom problem

Rachel Reeves left her successor roughly £20 billion of fiscal headroom against her own rules when she departed the Treasury. Economists say markets have already eaten into a chunk of it. Ashley Webb of Capital Economics estimated that higher market interest rates alone had trimmed the chancellor's buffer from around £24 billion in March to about £17 billion now — before accounting for July's borrowing miss or any new spending commitments.

And there have been new commitments. Within a day of becoming prime minister, Burnham announced plans to end rough sleeping and remove VAT from household energy bills — pledges that analysts at City AM costed at roughly £22 billion over the parliament, a sum that has to come from further borrowing, from tax rises, or from eating further into the headroom Reeves left behind. Thomas Pugh, chief economist at RSM UK, put the constraint bluntly: "the commitment to sticking to the fiscal rules means further tax rises are inevitable come the autumn Budget," adding that the risk runs both ways — "more borrowing to spend fuels inflation and pushes up gilt yields further, leaving the new chancellor having to borrow more just to stand still."

Healey, for his part, has tried to pre-empt the tax-rise narrative rather than confirm it. Announcing the Budget date, he pledged fiscal discipline as "the bedrock of our UK economic stability and national security," and said he was "committed to meeting our fiscal rules, with a buffer against global uncertainties" — while separately ruling out increases to income tax, VAT or National Insurance, the three levers that between them raise most of Treasury revenue. That leaves a narrower set of options: smaller, more targeted tax changes, further scrutiny of departmental spending, or a rules reinterpretation of the kind Burnham has already floated and that gilt markets have already punished once.

What the numbers foreclose

If this would be Labour's third tax-raising Budget in as many years — after a £41.5 billion package in 2024 and £26 billion in November 2025 — the political room to ask voters for a fourth round is not obviously larger than it was a year ago, even as the fiscal case for it hardens. Some analysts now expect the deficit to exceed 4% of GDP for a seventh consecutive year, a run that itself narrows the argument for any give on either spending or tax relief in October, regardless of which the government would prefer.

The next hard data point is September's public finances release, due in October just ahead of the Budget itself, alongside an updated OBR forecast that will tell Healey exactly how much headroom he has left to work with. Markets will also be watching how far gilt yields move between now and 28 October — every sustained rise makes the £100 billion annual debt-servicing bill larger still, and narrows the chancellor's options further before he has said a word from the despatch box.
