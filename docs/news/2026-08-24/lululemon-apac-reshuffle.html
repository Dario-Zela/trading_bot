Lululemon promotes China head to lead APAC, hires ex-Louis Vuitton executive

A Lululemon store in Shenzhen — China has become the company's fastest-growing market — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Lululemon merged its China and APAC leadership under an eight-year China veteran and hired an ex-Louis Vuitton China CEO to run the region day to day.

Lululemon is putting its Asia-Pacific business under a single command and handing the keys to a China veteran, while poaching a Louis Vuitton alumnus to run day-to-day operations across the wider region. The moves, announced this week, formalise what the numbers have been saying for two years: China is no longer a satellite market for the Vancouver-based athleisure brand — it is close to becoming the whole point.

San Yan Ng, who has run Lululemon's China business for the past eight years, has been promoted to regional president of China and APAC, a newly unified role that folds the company's fastest-growing market into command of the broader region. Before Lululemon, Ng spent twelve years at Loewe, rising to managing director for Asia-Pacific, then moved to Tory Burch to oversee Asia retail and wholesale — a resume built entirely in premium and luxury retail, not sportswear.

Reporting to Ng in the new structure is Jeffrey Hang, joining as senior vice president and general manager of APAC. Hang's most recent job was managing director for Bulgari across Southeast Asia, India, Australia and New Zealand; before that he spent years as senior vice president and chief executive of Louis Vuitton China. Between them, Ng and Hang bring roughly three decades of luxury-sector experience to a company that sells $128 leggings — a hire pattern that reads less like a retail reshuffle and more like a statement of intent about who Lululemon thinks its customer now is.

Why China, why now

The appointments track a business performance that has outrun the rest of the company by a wide margin. Lululemon's fiscal 2025 results, reported in March, showed China Mainland revenue up 24% in the fourth quarter and 29% for the full year, against total company revenue growth of just 5% to $11.1 billion. First-quarter fiscal 2026 results in June showed China Mainland revenue up 30% (23% in constant currency), even as the Americas business shrank 3% and operating income there fell 37%. Management has guided to roughly 20% China growth for the full year.

Lululemon ended fiscal 2025 with more than 170 stores on the Chinese mainland and has said the bulk of its 25 to 30 planned international store openings this year will land there. Against that backdrop, consolidating China and APAC leadership under one executive — rather than treating China as one market among several reporting into a broader Asia head — looks like an organisational chart catching up to where the revenue already is.

The luxury poach

The more pointed signal is who was hired to help run it. Recruiting a former Louis Vuitton China chief executive to a mid-market athleisure brand is not a conventional move, and it lands at a moment when Lululemon is trying to defend premium pricing against a wave of cheaper domestic activewear competitors in China, from Anta to Bosideng-owned brands. Hang's brief — general manager across a region spanning China, Hong Kong, Macau, Singapore and Japan — puts someone schooled in luxury retail discipline (store experience, brand scarcity, clientele management) in charge of exactly the kind of premium positioning Lululemon needs to hold if it wants to keep charging luxury-adjacent prices for stretch fabric.

Interim co-chief executive André Maestrini framed the changes around the region's importance to the company's growth ambitions, calling China and APAC among the world's most dynamic consumer markets. The appointments also land inside a broader leadership transition: Lululemon is currently run by Maestrini and chief financial officer Meghan Frank as interim co-CEOs following Calvin McDonald's departure, with former Nike executive Heidi O'Neill set to take over as chief executive on September 8.

What it signals for the sector

For a company whose home market is stumbling, betting the org chart on China is a concentration risk as much as a growth strategy — a downturn in Chinese consumer spending would now hit Lululemon's most important growth engine directly. But the leadership picks also point to something broader playing out across retail: athletic and luxury brands are converging on the same Asian consumer, competing for the same real estate, the same store experience, and increasingly the same executive talent pool. Europe's luxury houses have separately been talking up early recovery signs in Chinese demand this year; Lululemon's bet is that it can capture some of that same spending without discounting its way there.

The next test comes with second-quarter results, expected in September, and with early data on how Hang's store network performs once he's had a full quarter to influence merchandising and store rollout. Investors will also be watching whether Lululemon's premium positioning in China survives a slowing domestic economy and mounting competition from local sportswear brands undercutting it on price.
