New electronic exclusion-zone scheme for offenders to start in October

An electronic ankle tag used for GPS monitoring of offenders. — Monique / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
GPS 'restriction zones' for serious offenders launch in October, but the watchdog overseeing the tagging system says it's already struggling to cope.

From October, some of England and Wales's most serious sexual and violent offenders leaving prison will be confined to GPS-monitored "restriction zones" — areas a few miles wide that they cannot leave without triggering an alert to probation officers. Step outside the boundary, and the consequence can be a trip straight back to prison.

Justice Secretary Alex Norris announced the October start date this month, framing it as a shift in whose life gets constrained after a conviction. "Offenders should carry the consequences of their crimes, not their victims," he said. "No victim should fear the shadow of an abuser on their back."

The scheme is a variation on a familiar idea. Courts already impose exclusion zones — bans on entering a specific street, a victim's workplace, a named postcode. Restriction zones flip the logic: rather than naming places an offender can't go, probation officers and victims jointly agree the (usually larger) area an offender must stay inside, using a GPS ankle tag to enforce it. Officers can set the boundary for as long as they judge necessary and adjust it as risk changes.

The system doing the enforcing is already under strain

The technical and human infrastructure behind this — enforced by Serco under a roughly £200m, six-year Ministry of Justice contract awarded in 2023 — is the part worth watching. The National Audit Office reported in July that electronic monitoring "is not working as intended," warning of risks to public protection. Serco met its target for turning up to fit tags on time in February, but succeeded in actually fitting one within two attempts only 62% of the time. HM Prison and Probation Service was, as of March, reviewing roughly 8,900 cases — nearly a quarter of everyone required to be tagged — to work out how many people were going unmonitored. Probation itself is short an estimated 2,200 staff.

The NAO's conclusion was blunt: further expansion won't be safe or effective unless the Ministry of Justice and its partners fix governance, data quality and system-wide inefficiencies first. The government's response is to throw money and headcount at the problem — up to £700m in extra probation funding by 2028-29, and more than 1,300 new trainee probation officers due to start this year — rather than to slow the rollout.

For a technology story, the interesting gap is what's still missing: no vendor beyond Serco has been named for the restriction-zone software layer itself, and the Ministry of Justice has not published a breakdown of what the October expansion will cost beyond the existing tagging contract. Serco is listed on the London Stock Exchange, making it the one publicly traded company with a direct, quantifiable stake in how smoothly this scales. Whether "tens of thousands more offenders tagged," as ministers have promised, is achievable on the current contract — or requires a new one — should become clear once the scheme is live and the NAO, or MPs on the Public Accounts Committee, take another look.
