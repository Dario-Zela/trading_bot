Resolution Foundation says UK productivity is growing faster than ONS data shows

The Bank of England, whose rate-setters watch productivity data closely for signs of the economy's non-inflationary speed limit. — David Iliff / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
A thinktank reassessment using alternative workforce data suggests UK productivity has risen 1.1% annually — well above the gloomier official figures.

Britain's productivity problem may be less dire than the official statistics suggest — according to the people who compile some of those statistics' harshest critics. The Resolution Foundation, the living-standards thinktank, says a reassessment of the workforce data underlying UK productivity figures points to output-per-hour growth averaging 1.1% a year over the two years to the end of June 2026. That is up from a 0.7% annual decline over the previous two years, and above the sluggish 0.7% average the UK managed through the late 2010s. If it holds up, it would be the best sustained run since before the 2008 financial crisis — and a rare piece of good economic news for Chancellor John Healey ahead of the October Budget.

A quarrel over who's working

The gap comes down to how many people are actually in jobs. The Resolution Foundation argues the Office for National Statistics' Labour Force Survey — the traditional bedrock of UK employment data — has been badly undermined by collapsing response rates since the pandemic, making it an unreliable guide to how many hours the economy is actually working. Instead, the Foundation cross-references HMRC payroll data and other administrative sources, which it says paint a more accurate picture of the workforce.

"While official figures suggest that the output of workers has worsened further in the mid-2020s, our more accurate productivity measure suggests that it has been improving in recent years," said Simon Pittaway, an economist at the Foundation. Tellingly, the ONS's own headline productivity release still shows output per hour up just 0.4% in the year to the first quarter of 2026 on the standard survey measure — though the ONS itself has flagged the same data-quality problem and now publishes an experimental alternative, based on HMRC real-time information, which also shows a notably stronger 2.1% for that quarter. That the statistics office's own workaround points in the same direction, even if not to the same number, lends the Foundation's critique some institutional cover.

The Foundation was careful to rule out an easy explanation: it found no evidence the gain simply reflects fewer low-productivity hospitality and retail jobs after minimum-wage rises — the employment share in those sectors is little changed from the late 2010s. "The UK's productivity recovery has been achieved by the same workers, doing the same jobs, and working in the same sectors," Pittaway said, adding that Britain's post-crisis productivity record "explains a lot of its economic stagnation and weak living standards growth." Morgan Stanley's own estimate of private-sector productivity growth, at around 1.8%, sits in similar territory, adding a second independent voice to the revisionist camp.

Why the Bank will care

Productivity growth feeds directly into the Bank of England's estimate of the economy's non-inflationary speed limit — how fast output and wages can rise without stoking inflation. A genuine upgrade would give the Monetary Policy Committee more room to look through wage growth, at a moment when unemployment has climbed to 5.1%, its highest in a decade — a reminder that some of the productivity gain may simply reflect fewer people in work rather than more output. The next hard test comes when the ONS publishes its own quarterly productivity figures alongside labour-market data in the coming months; until the two data sets converge, the Bank has reason to treat this upgrade as promising rather than proven.
