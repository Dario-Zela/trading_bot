Shein prices Hong Kong IPO at up to $27bn, a fraction of its 2022 peak

Exchange Square in Hong Kong's Central district, home to the Hong Kong Stock Exchange, where Shein begins trading 1 September. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Shein's Hong Kong IPO prices up to $27bn — down more than 70% from its 2022 peak — after failed New York and London listings.

Shein's long march to a public listing ends, for now, not in New York and not in London but on the trading floor of Hong Kong's Exchange Square — and at roughly a quarter of the price tag investors were once willing to pay.

The fast-fashion retailer opened books to investors on 24 August for a Hong Kong initial public offering that will price on 28 August and begin trading on 1 September. At the top of its indicated range — HK$47.60 to HK$49.50 a share — the sale would value the company at just over $27 billion. Shein is offering 279 million shares to raise up to HK$13.85 billion, or about $1.76 billion. Set against the roughly $98 billion valuation private investors assigned Shein in its 2022 fundraising round, the top of this range represents a decline of more than 70%.

A three-year odyssey, two dead ends

Shein's route to Hong Kong runs through two abandoned listings. The company first explored a New York IPO, then confidentially filed with the UK Financial Conduct Authority in June 2024 for a London listing. The FCA cleared that proposal in early 2025 despite objections from civic groups over labour and environmental practices. But a London listing also requires sign-off from China's securities regulator, the China Securities Regulatory Commission, because Shein's operational base and much of its supply chain sit in mainland China. That approval never came.

The sticking point, according to reporting at the time, was disclosure. Shein's UK prospectus described links between its supply chain and China's Xinjiang region — where cotton production has been tied to allegations of forced labour — as a risk factor, language the CSRC would not accept in an outbound filing. Shein shelved the London plan in May 2025. Its Hong Kong filing takes a different tack: rather than naming Xinjiang or forced labour directly, it refers only in general terms to risk from "negative publicity associated with our brand, business partners or industry." The CSRC approved the Hong Kong listing on 10 July.

The pattern is not unique to Shein. Other Chinese consumer and tech names have found Western exchanges newly inhospitable to disclosure standards that regulators in Beijing and London or Washington can't agree on — a gap that has pushed several large IPO candidates back toward Hong Kong, which sits inside China's regulatory perimeter but retains an internationally recognisable listing regime.

The numbers behind the discount

Shein's financials, disclosed ahead of the filing, help explain why investors balked at a higher price. The company posted $41.8 billion in revenue for 2025, up about 8% — a sharp deceleration from 20.7% growth in 2024 and 41.1% in 2023. Net profit fell 38.7% to $2.06 billion. In the first quarter of 2026 the company swung to a $99 million loss, against net income of $395 million a year earlier, a reversal driven partly by a $328 million fair-value charge on convertible redeemable preferred shares and partly by trade policy: the US ended its de minimis exemption for low-value parcel imports in May 2025, exposing Shein's US-bound goods to tariffs of 10% to 87.5%. US revenue, Shein's largest market, dropped 14.3% to $2.04 billion in the quarter.

Research firm Coresight now expects Shein's growth to slow to roughly 2% this year. Five investors who reviewed the company's recent financials or sat through IPO presentations told Reuters they were not convinced Shein could return to the growth trajectory that justified its 2022 valuation. Shein had initially sought a valuation of at least $30 billion for the Hong Kong deal before trimming its range under investor pushback, and the listing itself slipped by several days — Shein had targeted completing the process by the end of August — after what sources described as delays in taking investor orders.

Cornerstones, mostly familiar names

A group of cornerstone investors has agreed to buy about $383 million of shares — roughly 22% of the offering — led by existing Shein backers Boyu Capital, Tiger Global and General Atlantic, alongside Tencent, Greenwoods, Taikang Life and UBS Asset Management. The presence of Tencent gives the deal a second major Chinese technology name as anchor, following Alibaba's own record $10.2 billion Hong Kong share placement to fund AI infrastructure, priced the same week. But most of Shein's cornerstones are already shareholders or connected to existing investors rather than new institutional money, and reporting has suggested underwriting banks considered committing their own capital as cornerstones — a sign that outside demand at the target price was not a given.

Even so, the deal is large by the standards of a slow year for Asian new listings. At $27 billion it would be Hong Kong's biggest new share sale of 2026, ahead of autonomous-driving firm Momenta Global's $751 million float in July, and the third-largest IPO across Asia behind two mainland Chinese onshore listings — chipmaker CXMT and China Resources New Energy, which raised $9.8 billion and $3.6 billion respectively.

What the valuation signals

Shein founder Sky Yangtian Xu built the company from a Nanjing-based online retailer into the world's largest fast-fashion platform by leaning on a build-to-order manufacturing model that turns cheap goods into revenue at extraordinary speed. That model is also the source of most of its regulatory trouble: labour conditions in its supplier network, environmental waste from ultra-cheap disposable clothing, and the cotton-sourcing questions that sank its Western listing plans. Public investors, unlike the private growth funds that bid Shein up in 2022, now have to price all of that risk into a single number — and they have priced it at less than a third of the peak.

The stock begins trading 1 September. Its early performance will be watched less for what it says about Shein specifically than for what it signals about appetite for Chinese consumer names carrying supply-chain and geopolitical baggage — a test that Alibaba's oversubscribed AI-linked placement this week did not have to sit.
