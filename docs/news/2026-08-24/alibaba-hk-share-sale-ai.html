Alibaba launches record $10bn Hong Kong share sale to fund AI buildout

In one sentence
Alibaba raised $10.2bn in Hong Kong's largest-ever share placement — all of it for AI chips and infrastructure — even as its stock fell 10% on the news.

Alibaba spent the first three weeks of August explaining to investors why its profit had collapsed by three-quarters. On Monday it asked them for $10 billion anyway — and got nearly three times that in orders.

The Hong Kong-listed e-commerce and cloud group priced a placement of 710 million new shares at HK$112.70 apiece late Friday, raising HK$80 billion, or roughly $10.2 billion. It is the largest primary follow-on offering ever completed by a company listed in Hong Kong, and the third-largest anywhere in the world this year, behind only Alphabet and Intel. Every dollar of it, the company said, is earmarked for what it calls "full-stack" AI: chips, data-centre infrastructure, and the models that run on top of them.

Investors did not treat the news gently. Alibaba's Hong Kong shares fell more than 10% when trading opened, and its New York-listed stock slid alongside it — the market's way of saying that dilution, even for AI, still has a price.

A discount, and a rush anyway

The placement itself was a study in contrasts. Alibaba sold the new shares at an 8.4% discount to Friday's Hong Kong close and a 3.6% discount to the New York close — a meaningful markdown by the standards of a company with a market capitalisation north of $300 billion. Yet the deal's order book, run by Morgan Stanley, HSBC, UBS and CICC, filled within hours and topped out near $28 billion against a $10 billion target, nearly three times oversubscribed. Close to $6 billion of that demand came from sovereign wealth funds and long-only institutions in the Middle East, Europe and Asia, according to people familiar with the allocation.

That gap — a stock that fell on the announcement but a book that filled almost instantly — is the story in miniature. Existing holders are being diluted by roughly 3.7% (710 million new shares against 19.17 billion already outstanding) and are voting with their feet in the short term. New capital, much of it patient and sovereign, is buying into the idea that Alibaba's AI buildout will eventually pay for itself. Both reactions can be rational at once.

Why the cash is needed now

The timing was not incidental. Four days before the placement priced, Alibaba reported that net income for the June quarter had dropped more than 75%, to about 10.5 billion yuan ($1.6 billion), while free cash flow ran negative to the tune of $6.6 billion. Quarterly capital spending approached $10 billion on its own. Revenue still climbed 9%, roughly in line with estimates, and the cloud division — the unit absorbing most of the AI spend — grew 45% to 48.4 billion yuan, its fastest pace in 22 quarters.

Chief executive Eddie Wu has framed the spending as a bet the company cannot afford to sit out, calling AI a "once-in-a-generation" opportunity and telling investors that AI-related capex should reach breakeven within three years at current gross margins. That commitment sits inside a much larger number: in early 2025 Alibaba pledged at least 380 billion yuan (about $53 billion) to AI and cloud infrastructure over three years, a sum it says already exceeds the company's total AI and cloud spending over the prior decade. Roughly half of that had been deployed before this latest raise. Chairman Joe Tsai, notably, has also been one of the more public skeptics of a US AI "bubble" even as his own company accelerates its build-out — a tension that has not gone unnoticed by analysts tracking the sector's capital intensity.

The chip angle

The "full-stack" framing matters because it signals Alibaba is not simply renting capacity from Nvidia and calling it a strategy. The company has been expanding use of its own inference chips, part of a broader Chinese push toward semiconductor self-sufficiency that Morgan Stanley estimates has lifted the domestic AI-chip supply ratio from around 20% in 2023 to over 40% this year. Washington's export posture has been unsteady — Nvidia's H200 was cleared for sale to China in a January rule change after months of restriction — and that unpredictability is precisely why Chinese megacaps have kept funding parallel, homegrown chip supply even as US options reopen. A record equity raise dedicated explicitly to chips reads as a hedge against the next policy reversal as much as a growth plan.

Hong Kong's moment

The deal also lands in the middle of a broader revival. Hong Kong's exchange has raised roughly $41 billion in IPO proceeds so far in 2026, already surpassing all of 2025, and PwC expects the full-year figure to reach HK$380 billion — putting the city back among the world's top listing venues after several lean years. Alibaba's placement is a secondary share sale rather than an IPO, but it lands the same message: mainland technology companies are increasingly choosing Hong Kong, not New York, as the venue for raising serious capital, sovereign wealth funds included. It follows Shein's roughly $27 billion Hong Kong IPO pricing and adds to a run of listings that has made the exchange procurement teams' newest item to watch.

What's next

Alibaba's next quarterly results, due in November, will be the first real test of whether Wu's breakeven timeline is credible or aspirational — investors will be watching free cash flow and cloud margins as closely as revenue growth. In the nearer term, the settlement of this placement and its absorption into Alibaba's float will offer an early read on whether Monday's post-announcement selloff was a one-day dilution reflex or a more durable repricing of how much the market is willing to pay for AI capacity that has yet to turn a profit.
