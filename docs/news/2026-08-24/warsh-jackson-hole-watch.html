Bond markets brace for Warsh's Jackson Hole debut as gilts hold near 5%

Kevin Warsh, who became Federal Reserve Chair in May 2026, delivers his first Jackson Hole keynote on 28 August. — Federal Reserve Board of Governors / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Bond markets are holding near multi-year yield highs ahead of Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's Jackson Hole debut on 28 August, a test of his data-dependent, low-guidance approach.

Gilts are doing what markets do best before a big speech: nothing, deliberately. The UK 10-year yield has held close to 5.05% through the week, unmoved by a July inflation print that came in exactly where forecasters expected. The stillness is the tell. Traders on both sides of the Atlantic are parked in front of the same event — Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh's first Jackson Hole keynote, on Friday 28 August — and nobody wants to commit capital before he speaks.

That's a lot of weight for one speech to carry. But Warsh has made it that way himself.

A chairman who doesn't tell you what's coming

Warsh took the Fed's gavel on 22 May, promising what he called a policy "regime change" to purge inflation as a tax on ordinary households. In practice, regime change has meant stripping out the forward guidance investors had come to rely on — no more careful signalling of the Fed's next move, no more dot-plot hand-holding. The Fed responds to data as it lands, and data alone.

The cost of that approach has shown up at the long end of the Treasury curve. The 30-year yield touched a 19-year high above 5.3% in mid-August, and 10-, 20- and 30-year yields have climbed roughly 20 basis points since the July FOMC meeting alone. Molly Brooks, US rates strategist at TD Securities, has flagged what she calls an "asymmetric risk" heading into Jackson Hole: a dovish surprise from Warsh could move markets further than a hawkish one, because positioning has already priced in a chairman who says little and means it. Inflation, meanwhile, is stuck around 3.4% — well above target — which is precisely why the silence unnerves rather than reassures.

The credibility test

Jackson Hole has effectively become a referendum on whether Warsh's inflation-fighting talk survives contact with a bond market that keeps selling. A Bank of America fund manager survey found 69% expect him to strike a neutral tone — neither confirming nor ruling out the September rate move markets are pricing at roughly one-in-three odds. Goldman Sachs has warned separately that the minimal-guidance framework itself risks amplifying noise into instability, exactly the dynamic playing out in long-dated paper over the past month. The speech lands just 19 days before the 16 September FOMC decision, leaving little room for the market to reprice calmly if he says either too much or too little.

Gilts wait on the same signal

Across the Atlantic, the UK curve is telling a parallel story of a market on hold rather than a market convinced. July CPI landed at 2.9%, up from 2.6% in June but bang in line with consensus, with core inflation flat at 2.6%. Payroll employment fell over the same period, muddying the picture further. Traders are still pricing one Bank of England rate rise by year-end and another by next April — but the 10-year gilt's refusal to move much off 5% suggests conviction is thin. UK rate-setters take their cues partly from where global term premia settle, and nothing settles that faster than what the world's most important central banker says on a Wyoming hillside.

What ties it together

The common thread across gilts and Treasuries is the same: a market that has run out of patience with vagueness and is waiting for Warsh to either confirm the inflation fight is real or concede that data-dependency is just uncertainty by another name. If Friday's speech offers something concrete on the path of rates, duration-sensitive positioning — badly bruised over the past month — has room to stabilise. If it doesn't, strategists like Brooks expect the long end to keep bleeding into the September FOMC meeting on the 16th, with gilts likely to take their direction from whatever Treasuries do first.
