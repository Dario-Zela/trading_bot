Hedge funds ramp up dollar shorts as Bessent's bond-buyback plan takes shape

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, whose expanded bond-buyback plan has rattled currency markets. — U.S. Department of the Treasury (public domain)
In one sentence
Hedge funds are building dollar short bets after Bessent's expanded bond-buyback plan triggered the greenback's worst day in weeks.

Currency traders have a name for what happened to the dollar this past week: a slow leak that briefly became a puncture. On Wednesday the Bloomberg Dollar Spot Index posted its worst single session in nearly three weeks, sliding roughly 0.8% against a basket of major currencies to a three-month low, after Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said his department would "at least double" the size of its buybacks of long-dated government bonds. Hedge funds, rather than waiting to see how the plan actually works, are already building bearish dollar positions around it.

The mechanics of the move are narrow — a debt-management tweak, not a currency policy. But traders have read it as something bigger: a signal that Washington will tolerate a softer dollar if that's the price of keeping a lid on borrowing costs.

What Bessent actually announced

The plan itself is technical. Starting September 9 and running through November 4, the Treasury will roughly double the size of its buyback operations in 10-, 20- and 30-year debt, lifting the ceiling on individual operations from around $2 billion to at least $4 billion. Bessent has since suggested to CNBC that the total could go higher still. The buybacks target a segment of the market — bonds maturing beyond ten years — that has effectively seen a buyers' strike since late June, as investors demanded more compensation to hold paper from a government whose debt load keeps growing. The 30-year yield touched levels last seen in 2007 before the announcement, and briefly retreated before climbing back within a day.

It is, in effect, the Treasury acting where the market wants the Federal Reserve to act — buying duration to hold long yields down, using its own balance sheet rather than monetary policy. That puts it in awkward proximity to Fed Chair Kevin Warsh, who has been trying to let markets set yields without central-bank interference ahead of his Jackson Hole debut. Bessent's intervention makes that harder: if the Treasury is already suppressing the long end, it becomes difficult to read what yields are actually saying about inflation risk or fiscal sustainability.

Why currency desks read it as dollar-negative

The logic connecting a buyback program to currency weakness runs through investor psychology rather than any direct FX mechanism. Suppressing yields makes dollar-denominated debt less attractive relative to alternatives elsewhere, at the same time signalling that policymakers are more focused on smoothing the path for further borrowing than defending the currency. Audrey Childe-Freeman at Bloomberg Intelligence framed it as an attempt to suppress market pricing of US fiscal-sustainability concerns — precisely the kind of move that tends to unsettle currency markets rather than reassure them.

"The dollar certainly is the biggest casualty," said Gerald Gan of Reed Capital in Singapore. Amir Anvarzadeh at Asymmetric Advisors put it more bluntly: "the sacrificial lamb is the dollar." The yen, Swiss franc and New Zealand dollar were among the biggest gainers in the move — a pattern consistent with investors rotating toward currencies seen as insulated from US fiscal strain, and notable given Washington intervened directly to support the yen just weeks earlier.

Positioning ahead of the details

What makes this more than a one-day wobble is that speculative accounts are now leaning into the trade before the plan's full contours — how large buybacks eventually get, whether the Fed facility Bessent has floated for further intervention gets used — are public. According to Bloomberg reporting, hedge funds have been increasing bearish dollar bets since the initial announcement, positioning for the possibility that Wednesday's move was the first of several similar steps rather than a one-off. Evercore ISI analysts noted the buyback expansion could also force some near-term short-covering in Treasuries — investors caught wrong-footed on rates — while simultaneously discouraging traders from going "max short" the currency for fear Bessent ambushes them again with a bigger number. That tension, between short-term covering and medium-term dollar bearishness, is part of why the moves this week have been choppy rather than one-directional.

The episode also revives a theme Bessent himself raised earlier this year: that persistent, large-scale intervention to manage US borrowing costs risks accelerating what he has called de-dollarization pressure, as countries like China and India build payment channels that route around the dollar. A debt-management tool aimed at domestic bond investors is being read, rightly or not, as evidence for that thesis.

What's next

The first real test comes September 9, when the enlarged buyback operations begin and the market can judge whether they hold long yields down for longer than a day, as the initial announcement failed to do. Between now and then, Warsh's Jackson Hole remarks will be parsed for any sign the Fed is uncomfortable being upstaged on the yield curve, and further hedge-fund positioning data will show whether this week's dollar short-building was a trade on the news or the start of a sustained bet against the currency.
