WSJ: Russia quietly tests readiness for fresh mobilization drive

In one sentence
Russia is quietly stress-testing conscript logistics, WSJ reports, but any mobilization order looks set to wait until after September's Duma vote.

Russia's armed forces spent part of July running a quiet logistical drill: could the system built to absorb a mass wave of new conscripts actually cope, if the order came? According to The Wall Street Journal, exercises in the Kaliningrad region and elsewhere tested the unglamorous machinery of mobilization — receiving, housing, feeding and arming large numbers of men — without any public announcement that a new call-up was imminent.

Russia's military has been stress testing its ability to absorb a mass of new recruits in case the Kremlin orders a fresh mobilization push for the first time since 2022.

That is the sentence doing the work here. No decision has been made, the Kremlin and Russia's defence ministry both declined to comment on the report, and Dmitry Medvedev, deputy chairman of the Security Council, has repeatedly dismissed mobilization talk as "nonsense" and a Ukrainian "lying provocation." But testing the plumbing in advance of a decision is itself a signal — the kind of preparatory step militaries take when they want the option available on short notice, not when they've ruled it out.

The arithmetic behind the drill

The pressure is coming from the casualty ledger. Ukraine's General Staff put Russian losses at over 42,000 killed, wounded, missing or captured in July alone — a monthly high for 2026 — with the year's average running near 34,000 a month and cumulative losses since January topping 160,000. Voluntary contract recruitment, the mechanism Moscow has leaned on since the unpopular 2022 draft, is no longer keeping pace: for the first time since the invasion began, Russia is reportedly losing troops faster than it is signing them up, even after raising enlistment bonuses.

That gap is what a fresh mobilization would exist to close. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky said this week that Russian planning points to roughly 300,000 additional troops, deliberately structured as a "peripheral mobilization" that avoids Moscow and St Petersburg to limit political blowback in the two cities most sensitive to public opinion — with a further escalation toward 500,000 floated for 2027. Zelensky offered no public evidence for the figures, and Moscow has not confirmed them, but they track with the WSJ's separate reporting that any new wave would likely draw on Russians with little to no military background, unlike the reservists and veterans mobilized in 2022.

Why the Duma comes first

The timing tells its own story. State Duma elections run September 18-20, and the Kremlin is not expected to announce anything before votes are counted — a decision that would reintroduce, on the eve of a national poll, the same scenes of queues at border crossings and family send-offs that made the 2022 mobilization one of the more visibly unpopular moments of the war. Medvedev's public denials are consistent with an administration managing the story until the election is safely past, not necessarily with an absence of planning underneath.

Kaliningrad's inclusion in the drills adds a second layer analysts are watching separately from the Ukraine front: the exclave sits between Poland and Lithuania, adjacent to the Suwałki Corridor that would be the flashpoint in any NATO contingency, and testing mobilization logistics there suggests planning horizons that extend beyond the current war's immediate battlefield needs.

The next marker is the Duma vote itself. If the Kremlin follows the pattern its own officials are describing — quiet preparation, public denial, and a decision deferred until the ballots are counted — the period immediately after September 20 is when any formal mobilization order, if one comes, would surface.
