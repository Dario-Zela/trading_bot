Photo of Disraeli among missing parliamentary artworks, FoI request reveals

A photographic portrait of Benjamin Disraeli, held by the National Portrait Gallery — a photograph of the former prime minister is among the works Parliament cannot currently locate. — National Portrait Gallery / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
An FoI request has found more than 65 works missing from Parliament's own art collection, including a Victorian photograph of Benjamin Disraeli.

Somewhere in the vast, cluttered inventory of the Palace of Westminster, a photograph of Benjamin Disraeli has gone astray. So has an engraving of William Wilberforce, a satirical cartoon of Barbara Castle drawn as Florence Nightingale, and a photograph of Margaret Beckett, Britain's first female foreign secretary. In total, more than 65 works from Parliament's own art collection cannot currently be located — a fact that only came to light because someone thought to ask.

The figures emerged from a Freedom of Information request submitted by the Press Association, which asked Parliament to account for its holdings since the start of 2024. The Disraeli photograph, taken by the prolific Victorian portraitist John Jabez Edwin Mayall — who also sat Queen Victoria, Charles Dickens and Karl Marx in front of his camera — is the most eye-catching name on the list, but hardly the only one of note. The missing Wilberforce piece is a 19th-century engraving by William Holl; there is also a cartoon by Isaac Cruikshank and, more recently, a Martin Rowson cartoon from 1995 depicting John Major lancing the "boil" of that year's Conservative leadership challenge.

A working collection, not a vault

Parliament's response to the disclosure was measured rather than alarmed. A spokesperson said there was "little evidence of works of art going permanently missing from the Parliamentary Art Collection," pointing out that the roughly 8,500-piece collection — split between the Commons and the Lords, and covering everything from oil paintings to coins and wallpaper — is a working one. Pieces are moved between offices, committee rooms and storage on a rolling basis, get mislaid in the shuffle, and often turn up again: five of the works reported missing this year have already been found. None, Parliament insists, has been formally written off or booked as a financial loss.

That explanation has some historical cover. A near-identical FoI request in 2018 turned up 224 unaccounted-for works, including portraits of William Pitt the Younger and a wartime painting by William John MacLeod depicting the aftermath of the bombing of Parliament. Most of those, eventually, resurfaced too. Set against that precedent, this year's total of 65-odd looks less like a fresh scandal than a recurring administrative quirk of running an active museum spread across a working legislature.

Why the gap still matters

Still, the pattern is worth pausing on. An institution that catalogues its own history as carefully as Parliament does — the Speaker's Advisory Committee on Works of Art has overseen the collection since 1841 — is nonetheless unable, at any given moment, to say with certainty where every item in it sits. That is not evidence of theft or negligence so much as a reminder that inventory control is hard even for bodies whose entire self-image rests on institutional memory. A Georgian engraving stored in the wrong archive box carries no financial consequence for Parliament. The habits of mind that let it go missing, though, are the same ones that show up in less forgivable places when the assets being tracked are documents, contracts or public money rather than portraits.

What happens next is mostly a matter of patience. Parliament says its art teams periodically audit and relocate works as offices change hands, so a further round of "discoveries" — as happened with five pieces already this year — is the likeliest outcome, rather than any formal loss report. The next test of how seriously the gap is being taken will be whether a subsequent FoI request, whenever one is filed, shows the list shrinking or simply refreshing itself with new names.
