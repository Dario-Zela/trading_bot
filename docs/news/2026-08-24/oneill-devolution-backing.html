Jim O'Neill rules out government role but backs Burnham's devolution agenda

Jim O'Neill, Baron O'Neill of Gatley, the former Goldman Sachs chief economist and one-time Treasury minister. — Roger Harris / UK Parliament, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Lord Jim O'Neill turned down a formal job in Andy Burnham's government but praised its devolution plans, saying the blind-trust rules — not policy — put him off.

Jim O'Neill has spent four decades telling other people how economies should be run — as Goldman Sachs' chief economist, as the man who coined BRIC, as a Conservative Treasury minister under David Cameron. So there was a certain shape to the story when word went round Westminster that Andy Burnham wanted him back in government, this time as chief economic adviser. And there was a certain shape, too, to O'Neill turning it down.

"I've decided to not take a formal role," O'Neill said, adding that it wasn't down to any rift with the prime minister over policy.

The sticking point, by his own account, was duller than intrigue: a blind trust. Taking an official post would have meant handing control of his business interests to a trustee for the duration, the standard price of ministerial propriety. O'Neill, who chairs several private ventures and still sits on assorted boards, wasn't willing to pay it. "I don't want the constraints of a blind trust and all the constraints that would be necessary," he said — adding, with the air of a man who has done this job once already and doesn't miss the paperwork, that he was enjoying his current arrangements "too much."

Backing the policy, not the payroll

What makes the episode worth a second look is what O'Neill said next. Devolution — the transfer of fiscal and planning power away from Whitehall to city-region mayors — is the policy Burnham has staked his premiership on, and O'Neill is unambiguously for it.

"I'm a massive fan of the focus on devolution and I hope Andy and his team will be bold on this and everything else," he said, offering instead to "be a voice if they want to talk to me — that is, occasionally."

That's not a small endorsement. O'Neill has been arguing for fiscal devolution to England's cities since long before Burnham held any office at all — his 2014 review for George Osborne, "Fixing the Foundations," helped lay the intellectual groundwork for the Northern Powerhouse. He chairs the advisory board of the Greater Manchester Local Enterprise Partnership and grew up in Gatley, a few miles from the mayoral office Burnham once ran before Downing Street. If anyone in British public life has skin in this particular game, it's him.

Which is what makes the abstention notable rather than merely procedural. O'Neill isn't withholding support for the devolution agenda — he's withholding his name from the government executing it, on terms that have nothing to do with the policy itself and everything to do with the personal cost of formal office. Read generously, that's a seasoned operator choosing influence over title, preferring to needle a government he broadly likes from outside its structures than be bound inside them. Read less generously, it's a reminder that persuading a well-regarded economist to say nice things about your flagship policy is a lower bar than persuading him to bet his own arrangements on delivering it.

Either way, Burnham gets the quote without the appointment, and O'Neill gets to keep saying, as he has said in one form or another since 2001, that Britain's cities are underpowered — without having to answer for whether Downing Street actually fixes that.

The next marker will be the government's devolution white paper, expected to set out how much fiscal latitude mayors actually get. O'Neill's applause from the sidelines will only mean as much as what's in it.
