Europeans favour air conditioning over emissions cuts as heatwaves intensify

Air conditioning units clustered on a residential building facade — a familiar sight in southern Europe, still rare in the UK. — Tiia Monto / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
YouGov polling in six European nations finds majorities favour mandating air conditioning in new homes despite its climate cost — with the UK, at 11% uptake, furthest behind but no less keen.

Four heatwaves have swept across Europe this year, several pushing temperatures close to or above 40C, and a new poll suggests the public has reached a verdict on what should happen next: install more air conditioning, climate arithmetic notwithstanding.

YouGov surveyed adults in France, Germany, Italy, Poland and Spain in mid-July, and in the UK in early August. Across all six countries, between 61% and 73% said air conditioning should be installed as standard in new homes. A further 65% to 75% backed making it easier to fit AC without needing permission from a council or landlord, and 52% to 66% supported government subsidies to help pay for it.

Set against that is a trade-off most respondents resolved without much hesitation. In every country except Italy, 41% to 48% said encouraging more air conditioning to reduce heatwave harm should be prioritised over cutting CO2 emissions, against just 20% to 33% who put emissions first. Cooling a home mechanically is estimated to account for roughly 4% of global greenhouse gas emissions — on some estimates, close to double the aviation industry's share — a fact that appears to weigh little against the immediate discomfort, and danger, of extreme heat. More people across the six countries thought expanding air conditioning would meaningfully cut heatwave harm (29% to 61%) than thought it would meaningfully move their country's climate figures (19% to 40%).

Britain lags, but wants in

The starkest divide in the poll is not about attitudes but about access. Current air conditioning take-up stands at 63% in Italy and 58% in Spain, against 30% in France, 23% in Poland, 16% in Germany and just 11% in the UK — the lowest of the six, and consistent with separate estimates that put British household AC ownership in the low single digits a few years ago. Cost was cited by 51% to 75% of respondents as the main barrier to owning a unit, far outweighing climate concerns, which only 27% of French respondents named as a factor.

The gap matters because it tracks with outcomes. Between 79% and 85% of people with air conditioning said they kept their homes comfortable during recent heatwaves, compared with just 35% to 47% of those without it. Between 30% and 52% said they had feared for a household member's health at or near 40C — fear that appears to be driving the appetite for mandates even in countries, like the UK, where the building stock and planning system have historically resisted cooling infrastructure.

The policy question now sits with Brussels. The European Commission's Electrification Action Plan, published in July, nudged the bloc toward heat pumps and efficient cooling, but the companion Heating and Cooling Strategy — meant to set the terms for how the EU squares rising cooling demand with its emissions targets — has slipped to the third quarter of 2026. Until it lands, national governments are left to referee the same trade-off the public has already settled in its own favour: comfort now, emissions later.
