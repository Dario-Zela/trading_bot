Students paid up to £2,000 to play video games in new esports research scheme

Whitelands College, one of the University of Roehampton's constituent colleges, which runs the UK's first esports scholarship scheme — AndyScott / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
A UK university scholarship pays talented student gamers up to £2,000 a year — genuine institutional buy-in for esports, but years behind the money that already backed the sector.

It sounds like the pitch of a bored teenager: get paid to play video games. But the actual offer, from the University of Roehampton in south-west London, is more measured than the headline suggests — a £2,000-a-year scholarship for students who can prove they are genuinely good at competitive gaming, not a stipend for anyone willing to sit down with a controller.

Roehampton launched the scheme in 2018, the first UK university to do so, and has now handed out more than 100 of the awards. Up to 20 scholarships go out each year, plus four reserved under a Women in Esports scheme — Europe's first — aimed at a competitive scene still dominated by men. Recipients must hit minimum in-game rankings (Diamond tier in League of Legends, for instance), compete in weekly university tournaments, represent Roehampton at live events, and keep up their coursework. It is closer to a sports scholarship than a research grant: the money buys competitive commitment, not lab time.

"This money was crucial to me as it gave me the opportunity to study in a very expensive city without needing part-time work," said Cameron Dray, a former Roehampton esports scholar.

Academia catches up

What has changed since 2018 is the company Roehampton now keeps. In February, Loughborough University — a heavyweight in sports science — signed a memorandum of understanding with the British Esports Federation and opened a 40-machine Esports Gaming Lab as part of its DigiLabs campus, with an explicit remit to research performance, coaching and safeguarding standards across the sector. Staffordshire University has run the UK's first esports degree since 2017. None of this amounts to a single coordinated "research scheme" — it is several universities independently deciding esports is worth institutional attention, nearly a decade after Roehampton made the first move.

That lag is the real story. Ukie, the UK games trade body, valued the domestic esports sector's economic contribution at £111.5m in gross value added and over 1,200 jobs as far back as 2020; broader UK esports and gaming market estimates now run into the billions, with double-digit annual growth. Venture capital, publishers and sponsors priced in esports' commercial potential years before a psychology department did. Universities are not spotting an opportunity here so much as formalising one that already has a stock price.

Why the money still matters

For students, the practical case is straightforward: London is expensive, and £2,000 covers a meaningful chunk of living costs for someone already spending 20-plus hours a week on ranked play. For the sector, scholarships and lab partnerships do a quieter job — they signal to parents, employers and government bodies that competitive gaming is a legitimate field of study rather than a hobby with a degree bolted on, a stigma esports courses have fought since Staffordshire's launch drew tabloid mockery in 2017.

What is genuinely new, and worth watching, is the shift from scholarship money — paying good players to keep playing — toward research infrastructure like Loughborough's lab, which exists to study players rather than simply fund them. That is a different kind of institutional bet, and one still in its early innings.

The next marker is whether Loughborough's lab produces published findings, and whether other Russell Group universities follow with dedicated esports research funding rather than scholarship schemes alone. Until then, the money on offer is real, but it is paying for talent, not answering questions about what gaming does to the people — or the economy — around it.
