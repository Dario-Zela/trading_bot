Europe's luxury giants spot 'green shoots' in China demand

Louis Vuitton's ship-shaped "The Louis" flagship store in Shanghai's Jing'an district — Supanut Arunoprayote / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
LVMH, Hermès and Kering are citing early signs of stabilizing Chinese demand after an 18-month slump, though the recovery remains uneven and unconfirmed.

The Chinese shopper who spent much of the past eighteen months trimming spend on handbags, watches and cosmetics is showing tentative signs of coming back — and Europe's luxury houses, which have spent that stretch bracing for worse, are starting to say so out loud.

LVMH, Hermès and Kering all pointed to improving Chinese trends in their most recent results, according to a Bloomberg report this week, with executives and analysts converging on similar language: not a recovery, but the first believable signs one might be coming.

What the numbers actually show

LVMH's fashion and leather goods division — the group's largest and the one most exposed to discretionary Chinese spending — returned to 1% organic growth in the second quarter, reversing a 2% decline in the first. Group-wide, Asia excluding Japan grew 6% on a like-for-like basis in the first half, though the pace slowed from 7% in the first quarter to roughly 4% in the second, a reminder that the improvement is uneven rather than a straight line up. TD Cowen analyst Oliver Chen described China as "stabilizing after several quarters of deterioration."

Hermès, which has weathered the downturn better than most rivals thanks to its waiting-list model for handbags, grew revenue 6.1% at constant exchange rates in the first half to €8.16 billion. Its Asia-Pacific division excluding Japan grew a more modest 2.4% at constant rates, with management citing "continued growth in Greater China" while adding they were "seeing the Chinese market stabilising, but not yet seeing a decisive turnaround." The company has kept investing regardless, opening a store in Beijing's Sanlitun district this year and expanding its Hong Kong and Taipei locations.

Kering, the group hit hardest by the slowdown as it works through a rebuilding of Gucci, posted its first positive quarterly result in the region in two years. Chief executive Luca de Meo has called China a "top strategic priority," and the company is now expected to return to regional sales growth by the fourth quarter. Richemont, owner of Cartier, has leaned on a comeback in Hong Kong and Macau tourism rather than mainland spending directly. Burberry, smaller than the others but a useful bellwether, reported a 9% jump in Greater China retail sales last quarter, which it attributed partly to demand from younger, Gen Z shoppers.

The catch

The recovery, such as it is, remains lopsided and fragile. Sales at the twenty-five largest luxury labels operating in China fell more than 10% in July, as Beijing's efforts to curb capital outflows and tax offshore wealth continued to dent spending among the country's wealthiest citizens — precisely the cohort that drives high-ticket purchases. What growth exists looks distinctly K-shaped: high-net-worth consumers and categories like premium cosmetics are recovering faster than the broader middle-class spender who powered the sector's boom years through the late 2010s. Deutsche Bank's Do-Hyun Yoo put it plainly: without a genuine improvement in Chinese consumer confidence, "the path to reacceleration remains uncertain."

That caveat matters for how the numbers should be read. China accounts for roughly a quarter to a third of global luxury spending once tourist purchases are included, so even a modest change in trajectory moves group-level results for LVMH, Kering and Hermès more than almost anything else in their business. It is also why LVMH shares are still down around 30% for the year despite the improving second-quarter print — investors had already been burned once by premature optimism about a China rebound, most recently when hopes for a post-pandemic snapback faded through 2023 and 2024.

What's next

The next real test comes with third-quarter trading updates in October, which will show whether the sequential improvement seen through the summer survives the government's continued crackdown on capital flight — the single biggest swing factor for the segment of shoppers actually driving the numbers. Kering's promised return to regional growth in the fourth quarter is the most concrete marker on the calendar; a miss there would undercut the broader stabilization narrative fastest.
