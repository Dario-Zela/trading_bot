HMRC crypto 'nudge letters' triple in two years as tax crackdown widens

HMRC's regional centre in Croydon, one of the tax authority's main processing hubs. — Vicki Couchman / HM Revenue & Customs, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
HMRC's crypto warning letters have nearly tripled to 81,000 in two years, just as new international data-sharing rules close off offshore hiding places.

HM Revenue and Customs sent roughly 81,000 warning letters to UK cryptocurrency holders in the past tax year, according to figures obtained under a Freedom of Information request and first reported by the BBC. That is nearly triple the number sent two years ago, and the increase is no accident of timing — it lands just as international rules that will hand HMRC automatic visibility into offshore crypto accounts start to bite.

The so-called nudge letters are not tax bills. They tell recipients HMRC holds information suggesting a return may be missing or wrong, and give them a chance to correct their affairs before a formal enquiry opens. But their volume has moved in one direction only: about 27,700 were sent in the 2023-24 tax year, rising 134% to 64,982 in 2024-25, then climbing a further 25% to roughly 81,000 in the year just gone, based on data compiled by accountancy firm UHY Hacker Young.

"The tax treatment of cryptocurrency in the UK is complex, and many individuals do not fully understand their reporting obligations or recognise when transactions give rise to taxable income or gains that must be disclosed to HMRC," said Neela Chauhan, a partner at UHY Hacker Young, who filed the request.

Why the timing matters

The letters are arriving ahead of a much bigger shift in HMRC's ability to see what UK residents actually hold. From 1 January 2026, cryptoasset service providers operating in the UK began collecting data under the Cryptoasset Reporting Framework (CARF), an OECD-designed standard that mirrors the automatic exchange rules already in place for bank accounts. Exchanges' first reports to HMRC are due between January and May 2027, covering the 2026 calendar year, and the UK is one of 52 jurisdictions committed to exchanging that data by the same deadline.

Under CARF, UK-based platforms must collect identifying details and transaction summaries for all users and report on those who are tax resident in the UK or in another participating country — closing off a route that offshore or foreign-domiciled exchanges previously offered for holders keen to stay under the radar. Providers that fail to carry out adequate checks face penalties of up to £300 per user. HMRC has estimated CARF will raise an additional £350 million in tax revenue between 2026 and 2030.

Reading the surge

The letters are not random. HMRC generates them from data it already holds — including from UK exchanges under existing reporting duties, and increasingly from the preparatory data-sharing that CARF has set in motion. The scale of the increase suggests the tax authority believes a substantial pool of capital gains and income from crypto trading during the 2023-24 bull run has gone undeclared, and is moving to flag it before international reporting removes any ambiguity about who owes what.

Capital gains tax applies on profits from disposing of cryptoassets above the annual exempt amount, which has been cut sharply in recent years — from £12,300 in 2022-23 to £3,000 currently — pulling far more casual investors into scope even for modest gains. Recipients who ignore a nudge letter and are later found to have underpaid face penalties ranging from 0% for careless unprompted disclosure up to 100% for deliberate and concealed evasion.

What happens next is straightforward to track: the first CARF-sourced data lands at HMRC between January and May 2027, and accountants expect nudge letter volumes to climb again once that arrives, this time addressed to a population of holders whose offshore accounts are no longer invisible.
