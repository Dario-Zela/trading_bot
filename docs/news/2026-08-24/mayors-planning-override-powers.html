England mayors to get powers to overrule councils on major planning decisions

Cranes over the Manchester skyline — one of the 13 mayoral areas gaining new planning call-in powers. — Anthony Parkes / Geograph, CC BY-SA 2.0
In one sentence
Mayors in 13 English regions will gain London-style powers to overrule councils and force through major housing schemes.

Thirteen English mayors are about to acquire a power London has had for a quarter of a century: the right to overrule the town hall next door.

Under plans confirmed this week, regional mayors — from Greater Manchester and Liverpool City Region to the West Midlands and West of England — will be able to "call in" major planning applications, taking decisions out of council hands entirely and directing them to be approved or refused. It mirrors the authority the London mayor has held since 2000, when Ken Livingstone was first handed the ability to step over borough councils on schemes of citywide significance.

The thresholds are specific rather than sweeping: developments of more than 150 homes, commercial schemes over 15,000 square metres, or any building taller than 30 metres — roughly ten storeys and up. Mayors are also expected to gain the power to grant "up-front permission" on some sites, letting building start without a developer filing an individual application at all.

A pitch built on frustration with delay

The case for the change is essentially about friction. Councils can sit on large applications for months, sometimes years, weighing objections from residents, ward councillors and conservation groups — a process defenders call democratic accountability and critics call gridlock. Housing minister Matthew Pennycook framed the new powers as "an essential part of the toolkit" mayors need to deliver homes and regeneration, language that fits neatly into the government's target of 1.5 million new homes in England this Parliament, a goal it is visibly behind on.

Ten of the fourteen devolved mayors have already backed the move — a notable show of unity for a policy that, on its face, hands them power at councils' expense. A consultation is due to be published in the coming days, which is where the sharper edges of implementation — appeal rights, ministerial oversight, exactly how "significance" gets defined case by case — will get worked out.

The pushback

Not everyone is convinced concentrating authority is the fix. Shadow housing secretary Sir James Cleverly argued the government was "stripping councils . . . of their ability to make planning decisions in their own communities", the traditional Conservative line that local control should mean local control, full stop.

The government's rebuttal is that call-in powers are a backstop, not a takeover. Councils keep the vast majority of applications; ministers retain their own intervention rights; and any call-in decision still has to comply with local plans and national policy — mayors cannot simply wave through whatever they like. The pitch is less "mayors decide everything" and more "mayors get to break the specific logjams that stall the biggest schemes."

What it doesn't fix

The more interesting caveat is the one nobody in the debate disputes: planning approval is only one bottleneck among several. Material costs, labour shortages and financing conditions all constrain how much actually gets built once permission is granted — a call-in power does nothing to a brick shortage or a stretched contractor. Recent delivery figures cited alongside the announcement — Salford running well ahead of its housing target, Leeds and Knowsley outperforming theirs too — suggest some mayoral areas are already building at pace under existing rules, which cuts both ways: it is evidence the tool can work, and evidence that a permission problem was never the whole story everywhere.

Watch for the consultation itself, expected within days, for the fine print on how call-ins interact with existing local plans — and for whether opposition councils treat this as a fight worth having or a power they quietly welcome the next time a scheme stalls on their own doorstep.
