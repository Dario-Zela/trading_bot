Infantino must remember he is European, says UEFA's Ceferin

UEFA president Aleksander Ceferin, who has accused Gianni Infantino of forgetting his European roots amid a widening FIFA governance row. — Steffen Prößdorf / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
UEFA's Ceferin says Infantino has forgotten his European roots, as FIFA's scrapped World Cup investment plan and a Trump-linked red-card row fuel a leadership crisis ahead of March's FIFA vote.

Aleksander Ceferin does not do subtlety when he is angry, and this week he was angry. Asked on the Rest Is Politics podcast about Gianni Infantino's handling of FIFA, the UEFA president delivered the kind of line that ends up in headlines rather than committee minutes: someone, he said, should remind the FIFA boss that he is European — and that he seems to have forgotten it.

"I think someone should remind him that he is a European, and that he was supported by UEFA, which he forgot."

It is a pointed jab, not a throwaway one. Infantino spent sixteen years working at UEFA's Nyon headquarters, nine of them as general secretary, before UEFA's own backing helped carry him into the FIFA presidency in 2016. Ceferin's implication is that Infantino has spent the years since acting as though that debt never existed.

What actually broke

The proximate cause is FIFA Forward Enterprise, a proposed vehicle through which FIFA would have sold a 21% stake in the commercial and ticketing rights to its competitions — including the World Cup — to a private investment company. UEFA says it learned of the plan through the media, with Ceferin finding out while on holiday. European federations voted to boycott FIFA competitions unless the scheme was dropped, and it duly was, in what UEFA officials have gleefully called Infantino's "humiliating climbdown" earlier this month.

Ceferin has not been shy about the comparison he reaches for. He has called FFE "even worse than the Super League" — a notable line from the man who spent 2021 leading football's establishment against that breakaway project, since the Super League was at least franchised chaos dressed up as ambition, whereas FFE, in his framing, was the outright sale of something that "belongs to everyone."

The dispute has since widened well past private equity. FIFA's decision to suspend Folarin Balogun's red-card ban during this summer's World Cup — clearing him to play the United States' last-16 tie against Belgium — drew accusations that Infantino had bent the rules after a phone call from Donald Trump. Ceferin skipped the final between Spain and Argentina in protest, calling Trump's intervention "more than shocking" and adding: "We have rules, and if you don't respect your own rules, then the game doesn't make any sense."

The numbers turning against him

What began as institutional friction is curdling into something closer to a leadership crisis. The English and Welsh federations have both withdrawn their letters of support for Infantino's re-election, joined by Serbia, Sweden and the Republic of Ireland. UEFA itself has formally said it has lost confidence in him. Ceferin, for his part, insists he has no interest in running against Infantino himself — but he has said publicly that Infantino should either resign or face a contested vote at the FIFA Congress in Rabat next March.

What's next

The next flashpoint is the 77th FIFA Congress, scheduled for 18 March 2027 in Morocco, where Infantino's re-election was expected to be a formality before this summer unravelled that assumption. Whether a credible challenger emerges — and whether more federations follow England and Wales in withdrawing support — will determine if Ceferin's rhetoric translates into an actual contest, or simply into eighteen months of the sport's two most powerful men trading insults in public.
