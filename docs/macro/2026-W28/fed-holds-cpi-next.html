Washington waits: the Fed holds until July 29-30, and CPI is the next tell

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C., where the FOMC holds its policy meetings. — Federal Reserve / Britt Leckman
In one sentence
The Fed holds until July 29, but a flat 3-month rate against two weeks of rising 10- and 30-year yields shows the bond market has stopped waiting on Washington.

Washington's monetary calendar has a strange shape this month: one meeting behind, one ahead, and a data release in between that will do most of the talking. The Federal Reserve held its target rate at 3.50%–3.75% on June 17 and will not meet again until July 28–29. In the five-week gap, the only scheduled economic release of consequence is the Bureau of Labor Statistics' June inflation report, due July 14. Everything the market does with rate expectations between now and the end of the month will run through that single print.

That would be unremarkable in most years. What makes it worth dwelling on now is who is running the meeting, what he isn't saying, and what the bond market has been doing regardless.

A debut without a script

June 17 was Kevin Warsh's first meeting as Federal Reserve chair, and he ran it like a man trying to prove a point. Confirmed by the Senate 54–45 — the closest vote for a Fed chair in the modern era — Warsh had spent the prior year publicly demanding what he called "regime change" at the central bank. President Trump, who nominated him, made little secret of expecting rate cuts to follow. Instead, Warsh delivered a 130-word statement stripped of any forward guidance and declined to publish his own rate projection, an unusual choice for a chair on his first outing.

"We have the capability and commitment to deliver on our price stability objective of 2%," Warsh told reporters afterward. "The commitment to deliver is strong, unanimous, and unambiguous."

The irony is not subtle. A chair installed on the premise that he would loosen policy is instead presiding over a committee where, by most counts, a majority of participants now see the next move as a hike rather than a cut, should inflation not cooperate. Markets read the silence as confirmation: CME FedWatch pricing has clustered around a 65%–70% probability of a hold on July 29, with the more likely venue for a hike pushed out to the September meeting, where futures imply roughly a two-in-three chance the funds rate is higher than it is today.

The print that decides the next five weeks

May's CPI report gave the committee reason for caution: headline inflation hit 4.2% year-over-year, a three-year high, with energy prices — driven by the closure of the Strait of Hormuz during the Iran conflict — accounting for well over half the monthly gain. Core CPI, stripped of food and energy, ran cooler at 2.9% annually, but the Fed's preferred gauge, core PCE, hit 3.4% in May, its highest reading since October 2023. Neither number gives a chair who just deleted his own forward guidance much room to sound reassuring.

June's CPI, out July 14, is the last hard data point before the July 28–29 meeting and matters for a specific reason: it will show whether the energy shock is bleeding into core prices or staying contained to the pump. The Cleveland Fed's nowcast has flagged upside risk to the June headline number. A hot core print does not force Warsh's hand on July 29 — the market still assigns that meeting low odds of action — but it would harden the case for September, and it would validate what the long end of the curve has already started pricing.

The long end stopped waiting

Here is the part that does not require the Fed to do anything at all. The 3-month bill has sat near 3.7% for weeks, tracking the funds rate almost exactly — no surprise, since it is priced directly off Fed expectations. But the 10-year has climbed for two straight weeks to around 4.56%, and the 30-year has pushed through 5%, completing a bear-steepening move that has nothing to do with the short end moving anywhere.

That distinction matters. A curve that steepens because short rates fall is a market pricing rescue — a Fed cutting into weakness. A curve that steepens because long rates rise while short rates sit still is a market pricing something else: term premium, the extra compensation investors demand for holding duration through fiscal and inflation uncertainty they can't otherwise hedge. The New York Fed's own term-premium model shows the 10-year measure turning positive for the first time since 2023, a sign investors are no longer content to be paid only for expected short rates — they want extra for the risk that the path itself is wrong, or that Treasury's issuance calendar overwhelms demand at the auction.

The dollar corroborates the story. DXY has climbed back toward 101 after dipping under 100.5 in early July, part of a run that has taken it up roughly 3% since January. A currency rallying alongside long yields, without a matching rally in growth data, points to a hawkish repricing rather than an optimism trade — the market pricing a central bank that may need to hike into an inflation problem it can't yet see the end of, not one riding a strengthening economy.

None of this requires Warsh to say a word, which is precisely the point. The committee removed its own guidance in June; the bond market has been generating its own in the weeks since.

What comes next

June CPI on July 14 is the next scheduled catalyst, followed by the FOMC decision on July 28–29, where a hold remains the heavy favorite. The more consequential date on the calendar may be the September meeting, now the market's preferred venue for the committee's first move under Warsh — should it come at all. Until then, watch whether the 10-year and 30-year continue climbing on a flat short end: that pattern, more than anything the Fed says at its next two press conferences, is what will tell the market whether this is a temporary repricing or a structural repricing of how much the government has to pay to borrow for the long run.
