EDITORIAL · WEEK AHEAD · By The Editor · 2026-07-12 · 6 min read

The rotation just rotated again — and the curve stopped listening to it

The trading floor of the New York Stock Exchange, where this week's sector rotation played out in real time. — Scott Beale / trading.biz, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Sector leadership flipped completely this week, but the bond market's bear-steepening never wavered — and three of five recent predictions on this desk just got proven wrong.

Twice in the last five weeks, this desk has published a confident call on where sector leadership was headed. Twice, the market did the opposite within days. That is the uncomfortable frame for a week in which Technology and Energy — last month's laggards — became this week's winners, cyclical darlings cooled, and the one thing that did NOT change was the bond market's insistence on pricing higher long-term rates no matter what equities did. If there is a single story in the data released this week, it is that the curve has stopped listening to the rotation altogether, and the rotation itself has stopped listening to last week's rotation.

The curve that won't listen

The 3-month/10-year Treasury spread widened to 87.4 basis points as of July 10, up from 81.7bp the week before and 70.9bp two weeks before that — a clean, monotonic bear-steepening that is now in its second confirmed week. The 10-year yield itself closed at 4.569%, up from 4.485%, while the 3-month bill barely moved, sitting near 3.70–3.86%. That combination — long rates rising while the short end sits still — is the textbook signature of a bear steepener, and it is a different animal from the curve moves investors spent the spring debating.

A bull steepener, the kind that shows up when a central bank is expected to start cutting, is a market pricing relief. What has happened since late June looks more like the opposite: investors demanding a larger term premium to hold long-dated government debt, a compensation for fiscal supply and inflation risk rather than a vote of confidence in growth. Two weeks ago this desk argued the demand-scare narrative was deepening and called for the 10-year to fall below 4.25% by July 11. It did not. The yield breached 4.45% — the level set as the falsification bar — a full week early, on July 2, and kept climbing from there. The curve was telling a different story than the one being written about it, and it has kept telling that story since.

Tech and Energy trade places — twice

The sector map underneath that curve move rotated as sharply as the curve did not. XLK, the technology sector fund, flipped from a 20-day return of -3.07% three weeks ago to +5.18% as of July 10 — the sharpest reversal of this cycle for any S&P sector fund, and one that happened almost entirely inside a single week's trading. On a 5-day basis Tech was the second-best performer in the market, up 2.87%. Energy, meanwhile, posted the single best 5-day print of any sector at +3.49%, even as its 20-day return remained the worst in the market at -5.44% — a fund still down sharply over the month, bouncing hard in the final week.

What makes this notable is not just that Tech and Energy led — it's who stopped leading. Financials, Health Care and Consumer Discretionary, last week's cyclical winners, all cooled on the 5-day window (Financials +0.16%, Health Care -1.77%, Discretionary +0.10%) while still holding solid 20-day gains of 6.66%, 5.23% and 3.30% respectively. Health Care's reversal is the sharpest of the three: it had been the market's defensive leadership story for weeks, and this was its first negative 5-day print in that stretch. Read narrowly, this looks like a market broadening rather than one reversing — more sectors are now green on the 20-day window than were a month ago, which is a risk-on signal even as the leadership baton keeps changing hands. Read more skeptically, it looks like a market that cannot settle on a thesis for more than five trading days at a time.

The dollar nobody priced in

The quietest move of the week may be the most consequential for the trades built on top of it. The dollar index sits at 100.97, essentially flat on the week (+0.11% 5-day) but up 1.02% over 20 days — a clear break from the -0.56% weekly slide the dollar was posting as recently as two weeks ago. The greenback has, by the data available, bottomed and turned.

That turn has not yet shown up in the stories being told about commodities. Oil, measured through the USO fund, posted its best weekly gain in over a month at +4.54%, even as its 20-day return remains deeply negative at -19.06% — still a fund working off a lost month, not confirming a new trend. Precious metals, which had been the primary beneficiary of dollar weakness, are already showing cracks: gold slipped 0.30% on the week and silver fell 1.94%, pushing its 20-day return to -6.43%. A firming dollar is a headwind for both the oil bounce and the metals unwind, and it is a headwind that has barely entered the commentary around either trade. If the dollar's turn holds, it complicates the reflation narrative sitting underneath this week's cyclical strength — a stronger currency squeezes exporters and emerging-market balance sheets even as domestic sectors rotate higher.

Owning the scoreboard

This desk made five predictions in the week of June 28. Three have now been falsified, two of them directly contradicted by this week's data. The call that XLK's 20-day return would fall below -5% was wrong by more than ten percentage points — the fund is now positive. The call that Health Care would outperform Technology by at least five points on a 5-day basis, repeating the prior week's 12.7-point spread, was not just wrong but inverted: Technology beat Health Care by 4.6 points instead. The 10-year yield call missed in the other direction, breaching its falsification level a week ahead of schedule.

The instinct is to treat three wrong calls in one grading cycle as bad luck, a batch of predictions that happened to land on the wrong side of noisy weeks. That is not quite right. Two of the three failures were bets on continuity — that this week's leadership would look like last week's, that a defensive rotation already in motion would keep moving. Both were wrong for the same reason: the underlying regime is not stable enough to extrapolate from a single week's print, in sectors or in rates. The steepening in the curve is the one trend that has held for two straight weeks without wobbling. Everything else — sector leadership, the dollar, credit spreads — has been noisier than the narratives built around it, and the record now reflects that plainly rather than obscuring it behind a single average.

What to watch

The Federal Reserve holds its next meeting July 29–30, and between now and then the mid-July CPI print is the data point most likely to move the curve, since two straight weeks of rising long yields with an unmoved 3-month bill suggests the market is pricing term premium and fiscal risk rather than near-term Fed policy. Whether Tech's reversal survives a second week, whether Energy's bounce widens into an actual trend change rather than a one-week short-covering rally, and whether the dollar's turn starts showing up in the commodity conversation — all three should be visible in next week's data, and none of them are foregone conclusions given how the last two weeks have gone.
