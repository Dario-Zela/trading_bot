The dollar quietly bottoms — and commodities haven't noticed yet

The trade-weighted US dollar index, whose recent stabilization around 100.80–101 marks a break from June's decline. — FRED / Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
DXY's quiet 1.02% 20-day gain marks a reversal from June's dollar weakness — and gold, silver and oil haven't fully adjusted to it yet.

The dollar spent June looking like it wanted to keep falling. A soft June jobs report — payrolls up just 57,000 against a forecast near 115,000, with the household survey shedding over half a million workers — pushed the Dollar Index (DXY) briefly under 101 in the first days of July as traders yanked forward their expectations for Fed easing. That was the dominant currency story two weeks ago: a labor market cooling faster than the Fed's rhetoric, and a dollar marked down accordingly.

It isn't the story now. As of July 10, DXY sits at 100.97 — up a modest 0.11% on the week, but up 1.02% over the past 20 trading days. That second number is the one worth sitting with. A index that was falling in early July has spent most of the past month grinding higher, and the one-week reading, taken alone, undersells how clean the reversal has been from the prior week's outright 0.56% decline. This is the kind of turn that rarely announces itself with a headline. It shows up first as a change in slope, and only later as a story everyone agrees they saw coming.

A reversal with no single cause

No single data point explains the dollar's stabilization around the 100.80–101 zone. Part of it is simple mean reversion after a sharp correction — DXY's July low near 99.64 came alongside a run of Iran-ceasefire optimism that briefly killed safe-haven demand for the currency. That optimism has since curdled. On July 7, Iranian projectiles struck two tankers in the Strait of Hormuz, President Trump declared the ceasefire "over," and CENTCOM answered with a fresh wave of strikes on Iranian military sites — roughly 80 targets, by some counts — while Washington reimposed sanctions on Iranian oil exports. Iran responded by hitting US positions in Bahrain and Kuwait. None of that is dollar-bullish in the textbook sense, but it has been enough to put a floor under safe-haven flows just as the market was pricing in an aggressively dovish Fed.

The other part is rate-expectations fatigue. The June jobs report did push a hike further into 2027 territory and took September off the table in Fed-funds futures, but that repricing largely happened in the first week of July — DXY's dip below 101 came and went quickly. Since then, the index has had nothing new to sell off on, and dollar shorts that piled in on the weak jobs print appear to be the ones now covering. A currency that stops falling on bad news is often more informative than one that rallies on good news, because it means the negative positioning has been mostly worked through.

Where the reversal isn't priced

This is the part of the story that matters beyond the FX desk: the rest of the commodity complex has not caught up to the dollar's turn. Gold and the dollar are supposed to move inversely often enough that a dollar bottom should show up as metals weakness — and it has, just not proportionately. Gold slipped 0.30% over five days, a modest pullback after retreating from January's near-$5,000 all-time high to the $4,000–4,050 range. Silver has been hit far harder: down 6.43% over 20 days, a period that maps almost exactly onto the window in which the dollar quietly stopped falling. Spot silver traded near $58.80 on July 8, off more than 4% on that day alone. If the dollar's 20-day gain is the real signal, precious metals are still catching up to it rather than leading it — silver's slide looks less like a standalone correction and more like the first asset class to notice the currency backdrop has changed.

Oil is the outlier, and the more interesting case, because its bounce is happening in spite of a firming dollar rather than because of a falling one. WTI has surged from a low near $68.80 to briefly above $76, a five-day gain of 4.54% for USO that ranks among crude's best short stretches in months. That move is not a dollar story at all — it's a geopolitical risk premium tied directly to the Strait of Hormuz, the same tanker strikes that have kept a bid under the dollar as a safe haven. Crude has already run into resistance at its 200-day moving average near $74 and snapped back once. Whether oil's bounce survives now depends on inventory data and the news cycle out of Tehran and Washington, not on what the dollar does next — a genuine divergence from the usual playbook, where a stronger dollar caps commodity prices broadly by making them costlier in other currencies.

What the divergence implies

Put together, the picture is a dollar that has quietly found a bid, a precious-metals complex that is beginning to reflect it, and an oil market trading on its own geopolitical logic that happens to be moving in the same direction as the dollar for entirely unrelated reasons. That combination won't last indefinitely. If the Hormuz situation cools — and mediators were reportedly working July 10 to walk both sides back from further escalation — oil loses its independent bid and the dollar loses part of its safe-haven premium at the same time, which would leave crude more exposed to a stronger currency than it has been. If the standoff instead escalates, both the dollar and oil could firm together, an unusual pairing that would squeeze precious metals from two directions at once.

The dollar's turn is the desk's highest-conviction call this week precisely because it is under-discussed: a one-week change of +0.11% looks like noise, but the 20-day trend underneath it is a genuine regime shift from the weakness that defined late June. Gold and silver are the assets most likely to keep adjusting to it. The next real test comes with US CPI data and the Fed's July 29–30 meeting — a hawkish surprise on either would confirm the dollar's bottom is durable rather than a technical bounce, and give the metals complex a second, harder reason to keep fading.
