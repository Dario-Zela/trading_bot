Credit says nothing much, no matter what the outperformance number claims

In one sentence
HYG beat LQD by 1.1 points this week because investment-grade's long duration ate the rate backup, not because high yield got any stronger.

On its face, it looked like a credit market shrugging off the week's rate scare. High-yield bonds, tracked by the iShares iBoxx $ High Yield Corporate Bond ETF (HYG), finished flat — a 0.0% five-day return. Investment-grade debt, tracked by the iShares iBoxx $ Investment Grade Corporate Bond ETF (LQD), fell 1.09%. Subtract one from the other and a headline writes itself: high yield beat investment grade by 1.1 percentage points, the kind of gap that usually gets read as risk appetite creeping back into credit.

It isn't that. Flat is not strength, and a 1.1-point differential built entirely on the investment-grade side of the ledger is not a high-yield story at all. It's a duration story, and duration stories and credit-risk stories look identical in a performance table and mean almost opposite things.

Two ETFs, two different jobs

HYG and LQD both hold corporate bonds, but they are not close substitutes, and the reason is on the balance sheet before it's in the credit quality. HYG's effective duration runs close to three years — call it 2.9, per iShares' own fact sheet — while LQD's sits closer to eight, some sources putting it nearer nine given how much long-dated issuance investment-grade borrowers have piled into the index in recent years. That gap alone means LQD is roughly three times as sensitive to a given move in interest rates as HYG is, independent of anything happening in credit spreads.

This week the rate move was not small. The 10-year Treasury yield backed up roughly 8 basis points, part of a selloff that accelerated after the June 16–17 FOMC minutes landed on July 8 with a notably hawkish cast — the Fed dropped language suggesting an easing bias, and roughly half the committee now sees rates higher, not lower, by year-end. A rough duration-times-yield-change estimate puts LQD's expected price hit from that move alone in the neighborhood of 60-70 basis points — the bulk of the 1.09% decline the fund actually posted. HYG's shorter duration means the same rate move should have cost it a fraction of that, which is a tidy explanation for why it came in flat rather than negative: not credit strength, just less exposure to the thing that moved.

What a real risk-on signal would look like

The tell is in what's absent. A genuine flight toward credit risk shows up as spread compression — high-yield option-adjusted spreads narrowing relative to Treasurys and relative to investment-grade spreads, buyers stepping down the credit-quality ladder because they're being paid to and are willing to take the compensation. Nothing like that accompanied this week's print. High-yield spreads remain compressed to levels reached only about 5% of the time since 2000, by one recent estimate, and there's no evidence they compressed further this week specifically. Investment-grade spreads sit near multi-decade tights of their own. Both markets are already priced for close to nothing going wrong — which is precisely the setup Loomis Sayles and others have been flagging as asymmetric: limited further upside from tightening, real downside if spreads move the other way.

What happened instead was simpler and less interesting than a rotation into risk: rates moved, the long-duration fund felt it, the short-duration fund mostly didn't. Bucket LQD's decline with this week's broader rate story — the same bear-steepening pressure showing up across the Treasury curve — and it stops looking like a credit event and starts looking like exactly what it is, a rate-sensitive instrument doing what rate-sensitive instruments do when yields back up.

The scoreboard complication

There's a second reason to resist the risk-on framing, and it's an inconvenient one for this column specifically. An open prediction from three weeks ago (W26-p05) called for HYG to post a negative weekly close in two of the three weeks running through July 18. That forecast implicitly leaned on high-yield softness as the base case — the opposite of what a spread-compression, risk-on read would imply, but also not what actually happened this week. HYG's flat print doesn't satisfy the negative-close condition, so the call now stands at zero-for-one with two weeks left to resolve. It isn't broken yet, but a flat week is not the print that call needed, and it's a reminder that the same data that undercuts an overexcited risk-on narrative also isn't cooperating with a bearish one. Credit, this week, is not really saying anything in either direction — it's rates doing the talking, and high yield's low duration just meant it had less to say.

What actually resolves this

The cleaner read on credit won't come from another week of ETF performance tables. It comes from option-adjusted spread data specifically — whether high-yield OAS is compressing, holding, or widening independent of what Treasurys do — and from primary issuance, where a genuine risk-on signal would show up as tighter new-issue concessions and oversubscribed high-yield deals rather than just quiet secondary trading. The next Fed meeting concludes July 29–30, and June CPI lands before that, both of which will do more to move the 10-year, and by extension LQD, than anything in the credit market itself. Until spreads move on their own terms, treating a duration-driven gap as a credit-risk-appetite signal is the kind of shortcut that reads fine in a headline and falls apart on the next rate print.
