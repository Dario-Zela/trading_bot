Oil bounces, gold fades, and the dollar won't cooperate with either story

An oil tanker at sea — crude has surged on Strait of Hormuz tanker strikes even as its underlying trend stays deeply negative. — Staff Sgt. Blair Heusdens, U.S. Army (public domain, Wikimedia Commons)
In one sentence
Oil's best week in months sits inside its worst trend, gold and silver fade as the dollar turns, and gas just posted the group's worst print — one shock, three directions.

Look at the commodity board this week and three different markets are telling three different stories, and none of them agree with each other. Oil is up sharply. Precious metals are sliding. Natural gas has collapsed. Normally at least two of those would rhyme — a weaker dollar lifting gold and oil together, say, or a risk-off mood pulling energy and metals down in tandem. Not this week. And a commodity complex that can't agree on a single narrative is usually a market where positioning is more fragile than it looks.

The United States Oil Fund (USO) is up 4.54% over five days — the sharpest move of any asset in the current snapshot — but still down 19.06% over twenty days, the worst trailing trend on the board. Brent crude, which cleared $76 a barrel this week for a gain of nearly 6%, owes the entire move to the Strait of Hormuz. Tanker strikes on July 7 hit a Qatari LNG carrier and a Saudi supertanker; within days the U.S. and Iran were trading strikes across roughly 300 targets, and on Saturday the IRGC declared the strait "closed until further notice." CENTCOM says traffic is flowing regardless. Whichever side is right, the oil market has spent a week pricing tail risk in the one waterway that carries a fifth of global crude, and that is a geopolitical bounce sitting inside a trend that was falling before any of this started — a distinction worth holding onto before calling it a recovery.

One shock, three transmission channels

The same event that sent oil higher is the reason gold and silver are fading. Silver is down 1.94% on the week and 6.43% over twenty days; gold has slipped 0.30%. The mechanism is straightforward once traced: renewed Middle East hostilities pushed energy prices up, energy prices revived inflation anxiety, and traders now assign better-than-even odds to a Fed hike by September rather than a cut — which lifts real yields, the standing headwind for a metal that pays no coupon. The dollar has been the beneficiary. The DXY touched a 13-month high near 101.8 in late June and has kept climbing since, with net long dollar positioning rising for eight straight weeks to roughly $40 billion, a decade high. So it isn't that oil, gold and the dollar are ignoring each other — it's that one geopolitical shock is running through three different transmission channels and pointing them in opposite directions. That's a more fragile setup than three unrelated stories, not less: unwind the shock and every channel reverses at once.

Natural gas sat outside that chain entirely and still posted the worst print in the table — UNG down 8.46% on the week and 8.15% over twenty days, after a 6% single-day plunge on July 9, the steepest since March. That one had nothing to do with Iran: Freeport LNG began maintenance that trims export demand just as EIA storage data showed a 61 bcf weekly build, above the five-year average. A market with genuinely no shared story doesn't diverge by accident — it diverges because supply and demand for gas, gold and oil are answering to entirely different clocks this week, and any trade built on one of them holding as a signal for the others is exposed the moment its particular clock resets.

The next resets are dated. CENTCOM's insistence that the strait remains open will be tested by whether tanker traffic and freight rates actually normalize in the coming days; Freeport LNG's maintenance window and the next EIA storage print will say whether gas keeps falling or has found a floor; and the Fed's July 29–30 meeting, with CPI landing before it, will settle whether the rate-hike odds now priced into the dollar and gold actually hold up.
