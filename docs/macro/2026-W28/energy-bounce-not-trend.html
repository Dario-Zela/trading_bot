Energy's best week in a month still hasn't erased a lost month

The Deepsea Delta oil platform in the North Sea — energy stocks track crude, and crude has been trading on Middle East shipping risk, not fundamentals. — Erik Christensen / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
XLE's +3.49% week — the sector's best in a month — barely dents a -5.44% 20-day slide that's still the worst of any sector tracked.

Energy stocks had their best week in a month. Nobody who has watched the sector's last twenty trading days should mistake it for a recovery.

The Energy Select Sector SPDR Fund — the ETF known by its ticker, XLE, that tracks Exxon Mobil, Chevron and the rest of the S&P 500's oil-and-gas names — gained 3.49% over the past five sessions, the strongest one-week print of any sector in the market. It was also, by a wide margin, the most predictable. Crude oil did almost all of the work: the United States Oil Fund (USO), a proxy for West Texas Intermediate futures, jumped 4.54% over the same span as traders priced in a fresh geopolitical risk premium tied to the Strait of Hormuz.

But sit the five-day number next to the twenty-day one and the enthusiasm curdles. XLE is down 5.44% over the past month — still the worst 20-day performance of any sector tracked, bounce included. USO's own trailing month reads worse still, at -19.06%. One good week has closed only a fraction of the hole the sector dug for itself over the prior three.

What actually moved oil

August WTI crude traded as high as $76.08 a barrel this week before settling back near $71.84 on Thursday, a gain of roughly 4.9% and the contract's best week in over a month. The move had nothing to do with energy-sector fundamentals and everything to do with shipping risk. Traffic through the Strait of Hormuz — the chokepoint that ordinarily carries close to a fifth of the world's seaborne oil and gas — has been effectively strangled since late February, when the United States and Israel launched air strikes on Iran and Iran's Revolutionary Guard Corps responded by boarding merchant vessels and laying mines in the strait. Renewed tension this week, even amid reports of a memorandum of understanding aimed at reopening the waterway, was enough to send traders back into crude with a fatter risk premium attached.

That is the same dynamic the Bot Tribune's Risk desk has been flagging for weeks: this is a geopolitics trade wearing an energy-fundamentals costume. OPEC+ actually added supply this week, and U.S. crude inventories posted an unexpected build — both bearish signals that the market shrugged off entirely in favor of headline risk out of the Gulf. That matters for how durable the bounce is. A rally built on fear of a shipping disruption unwinds the moment the disruption looks less likely, not on any change in how much oil is actually flowing.

The 20-day hole is still the loudest number

Energy's -5.44% trailing month is not just bad by the sector's own recent standards — it is the single worst 20-day print across every sector the Bot Tribune tracks. That gap did not open this week; it is the residue of a selloff that ran through most of June, one severe enough that two weeks ago the sector rotation story was effectively the mirror image of today's: energy was leading and tech was lagging behind. Both have now flipped. Tech, the subject of a separate piece in this edition, went from 20-day laggard to weekly leader in the same stretch that energy did the reverse — a reminder that the rotation itself is the more persistent story here, not either sector's individual trajectory.

Doing the arithmetic on the deficit is unforgiving. A security down 5.44% over twenty days needs roughly a 5.8% gain just to get back to where it started — this week's 3.49% covers a little over half that ground, and only if nothing gives it back. Energy has had a genuinely strong 2026 overall — XLE has carried a year-to-date gain north of 20%, powered by Exxon and Chevron's combined 42.5% weight in the fund and their resilience through the Hormuz disruption — but that YTD cushion is exactly why a trader should be careful about reading too much into any single week. The sector was already elevated before June's selloff started; this bounce restores some of that altitude without confirming the selloff is over.

What the prediction market is telling on itself

The clearest evidence that this is a live, contested call rather than a settled story sits in the Bot Tribune's own prediction ledger. Two weeks ago the desk staked out W26-p04: USO below $100 by July 18. As of Thursday, USO was trading at $108.82 — comfortably above the target, and moving further away from it with every day the Hormuz risk premium holds. That prediction is now under real pressure, and it is under pressure for the same reason the XLE story resists a tidy headline: geopolitical premiums don't decay on a schedule, and a call that looked reasonable when the strait crisis seemed to be cooling can be undone by a single week of renewed tension.

The caution here is the same one the Risk desk has applied elsewhere in this edition to the broader oil call — a scoreboard that shows more misses than hits on recent macro predictions is itself a data point about how confidently anyone should extrapolate from a five-day chart, energy included.

What to watch

The July 18 deadline on the USO prediction is the nearest concrete test, and it will turn almost entirely on whether the Hormuz memorandum of understanding translates into actual reopened shipping lanes or stalls the way earlier de-escalation attempts have. Beyond that, the next Short-Term Energy Outlook from the U.S. Energy Information Administration will show whether OPEC+'s production increase and the surprise inventory build start to outweigh the geopolitical premium in the official supply data — the fundamentals the market ignored this week while it was busy pricing fear. Until the strait's risk premium actually decays, energy's 20-day chart, not its five-day one, remains the more honest read on where the sector stands.
