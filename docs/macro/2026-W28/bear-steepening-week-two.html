Bear-steepening completes its second week, and nothing in the data explains why the short end isn't moving

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C. — the short end of the curve has stayed put through every 2026 meeting. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The 3m/10y spread has widened for a second straight week — 70.9bp to 81.7bp to 87.4bp — with the entire move in the long end while the 3-month bill sits still at 3.695%.

The Treasury market has spent two straight weeks doing something narrow enough to have a name but broad enough to matter: the long end keeps rising while the short end sits perfectly still. The 10-year yield closed at 4.569% on July 10, up from 4.485% the week before and 4.372% the week before that — three consecutive weekly increases, each one bigger than the last. Over the same stretch, the 3-month bill has barely moved, anchored near 3.695%. The gap between them, the 3-month/10-year spread, has widened from 70.9 basis points to 81.7 to 87.4 in a straight line.

One week of that pattern is noise. Two weeks of monotonic widening, in the same direction, without a corresponding move at the short end, is not. It rules out the easiest explanation — a single bad auction, a stray inflation print, a headline that spooked bond desks for a session — and points toward something structural sitting underneath the long end of the curve.

The short end has nowhere to go

Start with why the 3-month bill isn't budging, because that part is the least mysterious. The Federal Reserve has held its target range at 3.50%–3.75% since a run of three cuts in the closing months of 2025, and it has not moved at a single meeting in 2026 — not in January, not in March, not in June. A 3-month bill yielding 3.695% is simply pricing what the Fed has told the market to expect: no change through the life of the bill. There is no cut priced in, and increasingly, no cut expected at all for the rest of the year. That is a meaningfully different market than the one investors were positioned for as recently as last autumn, when futures still carried real odds of a mid-2026 easing cycle. Those odds have been ground down to almost nothing, and the front end of the curve reflects it with total indifference — it simply isn't moving, because there's nothing left to reprice.

Which means the entire steepening move, all 87 basis points of spread, is a long-end story.

A term-premium story, not a growth story

When the 10-year rises while the short end stays put, the standard interpretations are that either growth or inflation expectations are being revised up, or investors are demanding more compensation to hold long-dated debt regardless of the growth outlook — the term premium. The data lean toward the second explanation. There has been no comparable repricing in near-term growth or inflation expectations over the past two weeks; the move is concentrated almost entirely in the belly and long end of the curve, consistent with the 30-year note's own climb through 5% this month. That is a duration-risk story, not a business-cycle story.

The fiscal backdrop supports the read. The Congressional Budget Office has penciled in a federal deficit above $1.9 trillion for fiscal 2026, with more debt issuance required to fund it, and recent auctions of 2-, 5-, and 7-year notes have cleared at higher-than-expected yields with primary dealers left absorbing more of the supply than usual — a sign of soft, not eager, demand. The Treasury Department's borrowing calendar for the back half of 2026 does not get lighter from here. Investors holding a 10-year bond are being asked to underwrite a decade of that math, and they are charging more to do it.

What breaks the pattern

A term-premium move driven by supply and duration risk is stickier than a rate-expectations move, because it doesn't reverse just because a single data print comes in soft — it reverses when issuance plans change, when foreign demand for Treasurys picks back up, or when the fiscal outlook itself shifts. None of those are things a single week is likely to deliver.

The two most relevant near-term tests arrive close together. June's Consumer Price Index lands July 14, and it is the last major inflation read the Fed will have in hand before its July 28–29 meeting. A hot print would complicate the no-cuts-for-now consensus already priced into the short end and could, in theory, start moving the front of the curve for the first time in weeks. A soft print would do the opposite — reinforcing the current standoff without doing much to touch the term-premium dynamics driving the long end. Either way, the FOMC meeting two weeks later is where the market will look for any signal that the Fed's patience is wearing thin in either direction. Until then, the more instructive number to watch may not be the 10-year yield itself, but whether the 3-month rate ever moves at all.
