Bear steepener widens as the long end walks away from the front

In one sentence
The 3m10y Treasury spread has widened to 1.03 points as the 30-year sells off on its own — a term-premium story, not a Fed-policy one.

The Treasury market is sending a message that has nothing to do with what the Federal Reserve does next. Short-term rates are sitting almost exactly where they've been for weeks — the 3-month bill yielded 3.71% on August 21, in line with a fed funds rate still parked at 3.50%–3.75% and priced for at most one more cut this year. The long end, meanwhile, has simply walked away. The 30-year bond closed at 5.28% that same day, within a whisker of the 19-year high of 5.334% it touched a few sessions earlier. The gap between the two — the 3-month/10-year spread — has blown out to 1.03 percentage points, among the steepest readings of the cycle.

That combination matters because it rules out the simplest explanation. A curve steepens when traders expect the Fed to cut rates; that's the textbook "bull steepener," and it drags the whole front half of the curve down together. This is not that. The front end isn't moving. The 10-year, at 4.74%, and the 30-year are moving on their own, which is the signature of a rising term premium — investors demanding more compensation to hold long-dated paper, independent of where they think overnight rates will land.

The long end walks alone

The belly of the curve tells the same story from a different angle. The 5-year note sat at 4.42% — elevated, but nowhere near the move in the 30-year. If this were a broad repricing of the entire path of policy, the 5-year would be moving in step with the 30-year. Instead the curve is bending, not shifting, with the pivot point somewhere around the 5-to-7-year mark. That's consistent with a market that has largely settled on where the Fed is going over the medium term and is now pricing something else entirely at the long end: the cost of financing a growing pile of federal debt with a central bank that has stopped offering forward guidance.

That second piece — the absence of guidance — is not incidental. Kevin Warsh, sworn in as Fed chair in May after succeeding Jerome Powell, has explicitly moved away from the kind of dot-plot signaling his predecessors used to anchor expectations. Bond investors, faced with a Fed that won't tell them what it's thinking, have responded in the way they usually do: by charging more to hold duration.

Treasury tried to lean against it

The long end's refusal to rally has drawn the government itself into the market. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announced on August 19 that the department would at least double the size of its long-dated debt buybacks, from a $2 billion cap to $4 billion per operation, running from early September through early November. The move briefly worked: yields dipped on the announcement. It did not last. By August 21, the 30-year had rebounded above where it started, rising more than 3 basis points to 5.273%, as the initial relief gave way to the same worries that had pushed yields up in the first place — a widening deficit, soft demand at recent auctions, and a $25 billion 30-year sale on August 13 that priced at 5.216%, the highest since 2001, with a bid-to-cover ratio and primary-dealer take-up both below their 12-month averages.

The episode has also opened a visible seam between the Treasury and the Fed. Bessent's intervention is, functionally, an attempt to do part of the Fed's job — suppress long-term borrowing costs — using a tool that isn't monetary policy. Warsh has said he'd rather let the open market set rates, and critics of the buyback program argue that leaning on it to cap yields risks feeding the very inflation expectations that are pushing term premium higher in the first place. Whichever side is right, the practical effect so far has been to demonstrate that the long end is bigger than either of them: neither an accelerated buyback program nor the prospect of it has managed to hold the 30-year down for even a full week.

What would prove this wrong

The term-premium story is testable, which is what makes it worth taking seriously rather than filing as another vague "markets are nervous" narrative. If this is genuinely a story about compensation for duration risk and fiscal supply — rather than a transient reaction to one soft auction or one news cycle — the 30-year should hold above roughly 5.25% through the coming week even as the immediate auction and buyback headlines fade. A retracement back toward 5.1% or below on no new information would suggest the move was more technical than structural.

The nearest real test arrives August 27–29, when the Kansas City Fed's Jackson Hole symposium gives Warsh his first opportunity to address the long end directly in a widely watched setting. Markets will be listening for anything that reads as a return to explicit guidance, or conversely, for a defense of the no-guidance approach that would confirm the current pricing is the new normal rather than a passing dislocation. Either way, the front end is unlikely to move much on the speech. The question that matters is whether the 30-year listens.
