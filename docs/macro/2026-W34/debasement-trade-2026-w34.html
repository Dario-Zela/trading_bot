The debasement trade: steeper curve, weaker dollar, gold at records

400-ounce gold bars — the asset at the center of this week's debasement trade — Andrzej Barabasz (Chepry) / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0
In one sentence
A steepening curve, a sliding dollar, and record gold and silver are one trade — capital leaving duration and fiat for hard assets — and it's already visible in sector leadership.

There is a story the market is telling this week, and it is being told in three markets at once: the long end of the Treasury curve, the currency market, and the price of gold. None of these are speaking a language of growth. They are speaking a language of erosion — of duration, of the dollar's claim on future purchasing power, of confidence that the people issuing both will keep their promises. Put plainly: capital is leaving paper and walking toward metal, and the move has gone far enough now that it shows up in which stocks are being bought and which are being sold.

Call it what strategists on Wall Street have started calling it themselves: the debasement trade. It is not a new coinage — the phrase circulated through 2025 as gold ran up more than 60% and the dollar posted its worst first half since 1973 — but this week's data confirm it is still the operative thesis, not a stale one.

The curve is steepening for the wrong reason

A steepening yield curve is not, on its own, alarming. Curves steepen when the market expects growth and gradually normalizing policy — that is the textbook, benign version. This is not that. The 3-month bill anchors at 3.71%, a level consistent with a Fed still holding the front end down, while the 30-year has pushed out to 5.28%, a level last visited in 2007. The 3-month/10-year spread has widened to 1.03 percentage points not because the front end is falling — it is barely moving — but because the long end is walking away on its own.

That is a bear steepener, and the distinction matters. When the long end rises while the front end stays anchored, the move is being driven by what bond investors call term premium — the extra yield demanded to hold duration through an uncertain fiscal and inflation future — rather than by anticipated Fed policy. The Congressional Budget Office has lifted its deficit projection to roughly $2.1 trillion, debt-servicing costs are climbing as old low-rate paper rolls into today's higher rates, and buyers of 30-year paper are simply asking for more compensation to hold it. Supply, not growth optimism, is in the driver's seat.

A dollar that keeps finding reasons to fall

The dollar index has slid 2.63% over the past 20 sessions to 98.80, extending a decline that took nearly 10% off the currency in 2025 alone — its worst year since the aftermath of Bretton Woods' collapse. Softer Fed rate-hike expectations and continued Treasury buyback activity get cited as the proximate triggers, but the deeper pattern is that every rally in the dollar this year has been sold. A currency backed by a government running structural deficits north of $2 trillion, serviced increasingly by the printing of more of that currency's own debt, is not an obvious store of value — and traders are behaving accordingly.

This is the second leg of the debasement trade: it is not only that Treasury duration is being repriced, it is that the currency those Treasuries are denominated in is being repriced alongside it. A weaker dollar and a steeper, higher long end are not contradictory signals — they are the same signal, seen from two different markets.

Gold, silver, and the tape that confirms it

Which brings the story to its clearest expression. Gold is up 13.8% over 20 sessions and silver 19.3%, extending a rally that has already delivered gold more than 50 new record highs since the start of 2025 and pushed the metal past $4,500 an ounce, with some intraday prints running further still. Central banks are not spectators in this — they bought roughly 863 tonnes of gold in 2025 and, by some estimates, a record 288.9 tonnes in the second quarter of this year alone, up 62% year-over-year. Surveys now show the overwhelming majority of central banks expect to keep adding to reserves through 2026. When the official sector buys hard assets at this pace, it is because reserve managers are drawing the same conclusion private investors are: dollar-denominated paper carries a risk that gold does not.

Silver's faster percentage gain fits the pattern of a debasement rally broadening beyond the traditional safe-haven metal into anything with a fixed physical supply — a dynamic bullion desks have flagged explicitly as the trade extends into platinum as well.

The sector tape is already voting

What separates this week's read from a purely bond-and-currency story is that equity investors are pricing the same thesis. Utilities, the classic bond-proxy sector prized for stable, rate-sensitive cash flows, are down 7.6% over 20 days — among the worst performers in the market. Real estate, similarly duration-heavy through its dependence on the cost of long-term financing, is off 1.9% over the same stretch. Both sectors are being sold for the same reason 30-year Treasurys are being sold: their value depends on discounting distant cash flows at rates that are supposed to stay low, and that assumption is precisely what the long end is now refusing to validate.

On the other side of the ledger, energy and health care have led. These are sectors with pricing power and real, physical or defensive cash flows less dependent on a benign discount rate — the equity equivalent of gold. That the same rotation shows up simultaneously in rates, currencies, commodities, and sector leadership is what makes this more than a coincidence of three unrelated data points. It is one trade, expressed four ways.

What would prove this wrong

The thesis is falsifiable, and it is worth stating the terms plainly so the week's data can be judged against them. If the bear steepener is really about term premium and fiscal supply rather than growth, the front end should stay anchored even as auctions of long-dated debt come due — a soft 30-year auction with weak indirect bidder participation would confirm it further; a smooth one would call it into question. If the dollar's slide and gold's rise are the same debasement trade, they should keep moving inversely together — a dollar bounce that gold shrugs off would break the pattern, while gold continuing to climb through dollar strength would say something has changed structurally rather than cyclically. And if Utilities and Real Estate are being sold on duration risk specifically, they should stabilize the moment long yields do, and not before. The week's Treasury auction calendar and any fresh CBO or Treasury financing estimates are the near-term tests; how the rate-sensitive sectors trade around them will say whether this is a rotation or a regime.
