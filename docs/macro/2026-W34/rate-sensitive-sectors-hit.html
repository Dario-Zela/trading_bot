SECTORS · DURATION PROXIES · By I. Aresti · 2026-08-23 · 5 min read

Rate-sensitive sectors take the hit as the long end rises

Overhead transmission lines — utilities, the market's purest bond proxy, are absorbing the sharpest losses as long yields rise. — Nixdorf / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Utilities and real estate are being sold hardest as 30-year yields push toward 5.3%, with even tech slipping over five days despite a positive month.

The bond market is no longer a background hum for equity investors — it is setting the tempo directly on the sector tape. As the 30-year Treasury yield pushed to 5.31% last week, its highest close since July 2007, the sectors that behave most like bonds are the ones taking the sharpest losses. Utilities are down hardest, real estate is in the red, and even technology — still comfortably positive for the month — has given back ground over the past five trading days.

This is the clearest kind of confirmation a rates story can get. When the punishment lines up almost exactly with each sector's duration — its sensitivity to long-term interest rates — it stops being a coincidence and starts being the trade.

Utilities absorb the worst of it

The Utilities Select Sector SPDR Fund (XLU) is down 3.48% over the past five sessions and 7.6% over the past twenty, making it the weakest sector on the board by a wide margin. Utilities carry among the highest bond-like characteristics of any equity group: stable, regulated cash flows, heavy debt loads used to finance the grid, and dividend yields that investors have long treated as a proxy for a very long-duration coupon. When the 30-year yield offers more income with none of the equity risk, that proxy trade reverses hard.

The sector's troubles are compounded by a supply problem of its own making. Utility companies have been raising capital aggressively to fund transmission and generation capacity for AI-driven data center demand — NextEra Energy, for instance, has pulled back sharply this year even as investors price in years of load growth, weighed down first by the financing complexity of its Dominion Energy tie-up and now by the rising cost of the debt underwriting that expansion. A utility that must issue more bonds to build more grid capacity is doubly exposed when the cost of that debt climbs: financing gets more expensive at precisely the moment its own valuation multiple is compressing under the same yield pressure.

Real estate slips, but the damage is contained

Real Estate (XLRE) is also negative — down 0.42% over five days and 1.89% over twenty — though the sector has held up notably better than utilities. Part of that resilience comes from composition: XLRE's largest weights now sit in data center and infrastructure REITs riding the same AI capex wave that has punished utilities' balance sheets, giving the sector an offsetting growth narrative that pure income plays like utilities lack. REITs remain classic duration proxies in theory — property valuations are built on discounting a long stream of rental income, so a higher discount rate should mechanically compress asset values — but the AI-infrastructure bid has given real estate a hedge that utilities don't have.

Still, the mechanism is the same one working against utilities: with 30-year yields at a nineteen-year high and mortgage rates stuck up with them, the arithmetic behind commercial property valuations gets less forgiving by the week.

Technology's five-day wobble

The more interesting signal may be in technology. The Technology Select Sector SPDR Fund (XLK) fell 3.53% over the past five days, a sharp reversal that stands out against its still-solidly positive 4.22% return over twenty days. Tech is not a conventional duration proxy in the way utilities and REITs are — its cash flows aren't regulated or contractually stable — but many of its largest constituents trade on long-duration growth assumptions: earnings expected many years out, discounted back at a rate that just got materially higher. When the long end moves this fast, the growth trade absorbs some of the same discount-rate pain as the income trade, just through a different channel.

The five-day tech pullback also lines up with the broader rotation story playing out this month. Energy and health care have led the market since spending on AI infrastructure — the very theme propping up parts of tech and real estate — has coincided with capital spreading out of concentrated mega-cap positions and into cheaper, more cyclical names. Technology's monthly return is still positive, but the direction of travel over the last week points the same way as utilities and real estate: down, and moving with the long bond.

What the tape is confirming

The pattern across all three sectors is consistent with a market pricing rates risk directly into equities rather than waiting for it to show up in earnings. Utilities, the purest income proxy, are down the most. Real estate, cushioned by a partially offsetting AI-infrastructure bid, is down less. Technology, whose duration exposure runs through growth assumptions rather than dividends, is only now starting to feel it over a shorter window.

The next test comes with upcoming long-bond auctions and any fresh inflation data, both of which will show whether 5% on the 30-year is a ceiling or a waypoint. The Treasury's decision to expand long-bond buybacks to at least $4 billion a session, running through November, is a tacit acknowledgment that the department is watching the same yield levels the sector tape is reacting to. Until the long end stabilizes, the sectors most exposed to duration will keep trading like bonds — because, in every way that matters to their valuations, they are being priced like one.
