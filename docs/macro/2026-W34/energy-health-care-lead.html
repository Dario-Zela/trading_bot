Energy and health care lead as money rotates into real assets

Pumpjacks at the Inglewood Oil Field, Los Angeles — energy stocks are tracking crude's sharp weekly gain. — Jengod / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Health Care and Energy top the sector table on both windows, with Energy tracking oil's Hormuz-driven surge and Materials riding the same commodity bid.

The scoreboard for the week has a clear pair of winners, and neither is a surprise once the commodity backdrop is taken into account. Health Care topped the sector table on both the five-day and twenty-day window, up 4.33% and 7.41% respectively. Energy was close behind on the short window and essentially tied on the long one, up 2.79% over five days and 6.74% over twenty. Materials rounded out the podium, up 1.9% and 4.45%.

That two of the three leaders — Energy and Materials — are hard-asset sectors is not a coincidence. It is the equity-market mirror of a week in which oil jumped 6.35%, gold pushed to fresh records and copper kept grinding higher. The rotation into real assets that has been driving currency and rates markets this week (The debasement trade: steeper curve, weaker dollar, gold at records) is showing up just as directly in sector leadership tables.

Energy tracks the barrel

Energy's move is the more mechanical of the two. Crude has been rising for weeks on the back of the Strait of Hormuz standoff, where Iranian attacks on shipping and retaliating U.S. strikes have cut transits through the chokepoint to a fraction of pre-war levels — ship-tracking data from MarineTraffic showed as few as eight to fifteen vessels a day crossing the strait in early August, against roughly 130 before the conflict. WTI has traded as high as the high-$80s and Brent has pushed toward the mid-$90s, and the sector ETF has simply followed. Producers with cash-generative upstream operations catch a direct bid when the commodity they pump is worth more; the correlation between XLE and the crude tape has been close to mechanical all month.

The caveat, familiar to anyone who has watched this trade before, is that some of the barrel's move looks fragile. Analysts have flagged that quiet, undocumented flows — oil shifted ship-to-ship in the Gulf of Oman before reaching buyers — are keeping physical supply closer to normal than the headline disruption suggests, and Iran's foreign minister has signalled Tehran and Oman are close to a deal that could see the strait reopen once U.S. sanctions relief and reparations are agreed. A resolution would take some of the geopolitical premium out of the barrel, and Energy's leadership with it.

Health Care leads for its own reasons

Health Care's rally has nothing to do with commodities and everything to do with company-specific delivery. Eli Lilly's second-quarter revenue rose 47.7% year over year to $22.97 billion, well past estimates, and the company raised its full-year guidance to $85-87 billion. Johnson & Johnson's oncology franchise grew sharply — Darzalex up 23% to $3.96 billion, Tremfya up 68%, Carvykti up 62% — and the cap-weighted and equal-weighted S&P 500 health care indexes both touched fresh all-time highs earlier this month. Pharmaceuticals make up the largest single slice of XLV, at roughly 38% of the fund, so a run of beat-and-raise quarters from the sector's biggest names moves the whole index.

The two leaders are, in other words, running on different engines — one driven by a commodity spike with a geopolitical shelf life, the other by an earnings season that has genuinely outperformed. That they are converging at the top of the table this week is what makes the moment worth noting rather than routine.

What connects them

Materials is the hinge between the two stories. Its 4.45% twenty-day gain rides the same metals complex — gold, silver and copper all near or at records — that is showing up in the currency and rates market as a broader flight from paper assets (Dollar slide accelerates as gold becomes the crowded alternative). Copper alone has been described by traders as an “unsustainable” rally, pushed by tight supply and AI-driven demand for grid and data-centre buildout. Put together, Energy and Materials both draw their strength from the same real-assets bid that has defined the week, while Health Care's advance is an independent, earnings-driven story that happens to be landing at the same time.

The next test for this leadership table comes from two directions that have little to do with each other. On the Hormuz front, any confirmed progress in the Iran-Oman-U.S. talks would be the clearest catalyst for Energy's premium to compress. On the Health Care side, the sector's ability to hold near record highs will depend on whether the rest of pharma and biotech earnings due through September confirm the pattern Lilly and J&J set, or whether — as some smaller XLV holdings have already shown — gains stay concentrated in a handful of mega-cap names.
