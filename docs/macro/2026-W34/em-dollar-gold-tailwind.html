A weaker dollar and record gold are a gift to emerging markets

Central banks, increasingly in emerging markets, have been the marginal buyer behind gold's record run. — Stevebidmead / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
A softer dollar and record gold prices are easing debt burdens for EM borrowers, while silver's outperformance signals real industrial demand behind the rally.

For the better part of a decade, the standard warning to emerging-market finance ministers has been the same: watch the dollar. When it rises, EM currencies fall, dollar debts get more expensive to service, and capital flees for the safety of US Treasuries. This month, for once, the mechanism is running in reverse.

The DXY dollar index has fallen 2.63% over the past 20 trading days, sliding toward a four-month low near 99.4 — a sharp retreat from the 101.80 peak it touched in June. A gauge of emerging-market currencies tracked by Bloomberg touched a record high this week, above 1,906, as traders pare back bets on further Fed tightening and, in some cases, price in the prospect of a more dovish successor to Jerome Powell. The Brazilian real has been the standout, up roughly 10.4% against the dollar this year and the best-performing major currency in the world; the Indonesian rupiah has clawed back a fourth straight session of gains to trade near its strongest levels in months.

Cheaper dollars, cheaper debt

The direct channel is debt service. A large share of EM sovereign and corporate borrowing is still denominated in dollars, so when the greenback weakens, the local-currency cost of servicing that debt falls immediately — no policy action required. Sovereign dollar-bond spreads are now near their lowest since 2007, according to fixed-income strategists tracking the asset class, and corporate spreads remain tight versus their 15-year averages. A softer dollar also tends to import less inflation, which gives central banks in Jakarta, Brasília and elsewhere more room to cut rates without spooking their own currencies — a rare moment where easier money at home and a weaker dollar abroad point the same direction.

Gold's rally, which pushed bullion to a record $4,537 an ounce this week, reinforces the same story from a different angle. Central banks — increasingly emerging-market ones — have been the marginal buyer. They added a net 244 tonnes in the first quarter alone, and a World Gold Council survey found 68% of central banks plan to add to reserves this year, up from 62% in 2025. China's central bank has built its hoard to roughly 2,113 tonnes and India's to about 848, part of a broader shift: emerging-market institutions now hold roughly 32% of global official gold reserves, up from 18% in 2000. Most of that buying is framed as diversification away from dollar assets, a trend that accelerated after the West froze roughly $300 billion of Russian reserves in 2022. Gold in the vault, unlike a Treasury bond, cannot be frozen by sanction.

Silver's tell

If gold buying is mostly a reserve-manager and safe-haven story, silver's move looks more like a bet on growth. The metal is up 19.3% over the same 20-day window, outpacing gold's 13.8% gain and pulling the gold-silver ratio down as industrial buyers compete with investors for a metal that is, unlike gold, mostly consumed rather than hoarded. More than half of silver demand now comes from industrial applications — solar panels, EVs, electronics — and that share is rising as clean-energy buildout continues, particularly in China.

Copper tells a similar story from the base-metals side. Prices have hit fresh records near $11,771 a tonne, and the Invesco DB Base Metals Fund, which tracks copper, aluminum and zinc, is up 3.48% over 20 days. China consumes roughly half the world's refined copper, and the latest leg of the rally followed Beijing's infrastructure and power-grid investment pledges. Taken together, silver's outperformance and the base-metals bid are a cleaner read on EM-linked industrial demand than gold alone — gold can rally purely on fear and reserve diversification, but silver and copper need someone, somewhere, to actually be building things.

What to watch

The risk to this setup is the same one that has burned EM investors before: a reversal in the dollar. Rabobank has flagged Brazil's fiscal trajectory as a reason the real's rally could fade later this year, and any repricing of Fed rate-cut expectations — the next major test comes with the September FOMC decision — could send the dollar higher and unwind the easier financial conditions EM borrowers are currently enjoying. For now, though, the combination of a softer greenback, record gold and industrial metals outperforming bullion adds up to one of the more favorable backdrops emerging markets have had in years — a window that dollar borrowers, and the central banks quietly building gold reserves, appear intent on using while it lasts.
