Dollar catches its breath inside an uptrend that's still intact

The US Dollar Index (DXY), which measures the dollar against a basket of major currencies, has climbed since spring on a hawkish Fed pivot but stalled over the past week. — Wikideas1 / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
The dollar merely paused this week, but gold and silver kept falling hard — evidence the metals unwind is running on positioning, not just a stronger dollar.

The dollar took a breath this week. The DXY, the index that tracks the greenback against a basket of major currencies, eased 0.22% over five days to settle near 100.75. Against the backdrop of the last month, that is a rounding error — the index is still up 0.66% over 20 trading days, and this marks the third straight week that the 'the dollar has bottomed' read from late June has held. Nobody in the FX market is describing this as a reversal. It looks, instead, like a rally catching its breath before deciding what to do next.

What makes the week worth writing about isn't the dollar itself. It's what didn't pause alongside it. Gold fell 2.28% over five days and is down 5.20% over 20. Silver did far worse — down 5.88% on the week and a startling 16.22% over the month, the steepest decline of any asset in this week's cross-market snapshot. If the metals were simply trading as the mirror image of the dollar, a dollar that stalled should have given them room to stabilize. They didn't take it.

A rally built on a hawkish Fed, not a dollar panic

To understand why the dollar even got here, rewind to June 17, when the Federal Reserve held its policy rate at 3.50%–3.75% but delivered a dot plot that flipped the market's assumptions on their head. Nine of eighteen officials projected at least one more hike in 2026, several suggesting more than one, and the median inflation forecast for the year jumped to 3.6% from 2.7% in March. New Fed Chair Kevin Warsh, sworn in that same month, pointedly declined to add his own dot to the plot, saying only that he had "refrained from offering any projections of my own, consistent with my long-held views." The dollar took the hawkish shift as its cue, climbing to a 13-month high near 101.60 in late June.

That climb has since lost some of its edge. June payrolls came in at just 57,000 — weak enough to cool bets on an imminent hike — and the index slipped back toward 100.7-100.9. Warsh's first congressional testimony on July 14 didn't reverse that; he told lawmakers the recent easing in inflation readings was not "mission accomplished" but didn't reach for new hawkish language either. The result is a dollar that's stuck absorbing conflicting signals — a still-hawkish dot plot on one side, softening jobs data on the other — rather than one that's found a new direction.

Metals selling off on their own logic

Gold and silver's declines predate this particular week and go well beyond what a wobbling dollar can explain. Gold peaked near $5,000 an ounce in January and has since given back roughly a fifth of its value, trading around $3,985 to $4,016 as of July 17. Silver's fall has been more brutal: from a record $121.62 in late January to roughly $55-58 now, a decline of more than 50%. The gold-silver ratio — how many ounces of silver it takes to buy one ounce of gold — has stretched toward 70:1 to 72:1, near the top of its 50-year range, a sign that silver has been sold even harder than gold in relative terms.

Some of that is mechanical. Gold's 50-day moving average crossed below its 200-day at the end of June — a so-called death cross — and silver's is forming the same pattern now, feeding systematic and momentum-following sellers on top of the fundamental case. Nicky Shiels at MKS PAMP has argued that gold didn't fail as an inflation hedge so much as respond correctly to a specific kind of shock: energy-driven, supply-side, stagflationary inflation, of the sort that raises the odds of Fed tightening and lifts the opportunity cost of holding a non-yielding asset. That's a different animal from the fiscal-dominance, dollar-debasement trade that drove gold to its January highs — and it means gold has been trading, for now, more like an inverse bet on oil and rates than a hedge against currency erosion.

The irony is that this week's most obvious inflation risk — renewed U.S.-Iran tension and blockade fears around the Strait of Hormuz pushing crude to one-month highs — should, in the old script, have been gold-supportive. Instead bullion stayed pinned below $4,000. That's the clearest evidence that the metals unwind has developed its own momentum: heavy positioning built up during the rally to $5,000 is now being reduced regardless of what the dollar or the daily geopolitical headline does, in what analysts have generally characterized as orderly de-risking rather than panic selling.

What decoupling implies for durability

A sell-off driven mainly by a stronger dollar tends to stop when the dollar stops. A sell-off driven by positioning unwind tends to run until the crowded trade is actually flushed out, dollar or no dollar. This week's numbers point toward the latter. The dollar's pause should have been a moment for gold and silver to catch a bid if currency moves were doing the heavy lifting; instead both kept falling, and silver fell hardest of all. That argues the metals have further to travel on their own schedule, independent of whatever the DXY does next.

The next test for the dollar side of this story arrives July 29, when the Fed meets again — this is widely read as likely to be another hold, with roughly two-thirds odds priced in by markets, but the accompanying language will matter more than the decision itself given how much the June dot plot has already shifted expectations. For the metals, the more relevant catalyst is technical and positioning-driven: whether silver's forming death cross triggers the kind of momentum selling that gold's confirmed cross has already produced, and whether the gold-silver ratio keeps stretching toward the top of its historical range or starts to mean-revert.
