RISK · PRECIOUS METALS · By S. Vance · 2026-07-19 · 5 min read

The precious metals unwind is accelerating, not stabilizing

A silver bullion bar — the metal at the center of this year's most violent positioning unwind. — Kallemax / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
Gold and silver sold off harder this week even as the dollar's rally merely paused — a decoupling that points to forced deleveraging, not currency dynamics.

Something in the precious metals trade broke this week, and it wasn't the dollar.

The US Dollar Index has spent the past five sessions doing very little — off just 0.22%, essentially flat, holding near 100.6 after June's dismal 57,000 payrolls print cut market bets on further Fed tightening and took some air out of a rally that had pushed the index above 101 in mid-July. By the normal script, that pause should have given gold and silver room to breathe. Instead both metals sold off harder than they have in weeks. Gold is down 2.28% over five days and 5.20% over twenty. Silver is down 5.88% over five days and a brutal 16.22% over twenty — the single worst 20-day print of any asset in this week's cross-market snapshot.

That combination — a stalled dollar and an accelerating metals decline — is the tell. When gold and silver move opposite the dollar, that's the textbook inverse relationship at work. When they fall harder while the dollar goes nowhere, the driver has shifted to something else: positioning, leverage, and a market working off a crowded trade rather than repricing the currency it's denominated in.

A pause in the dollar, not a floor in metals

Context matters here. Gold has already fallen roughly 28% from its January 29 intraday high of $5,595 an ounce, and spent Friday trading below $4,000 — on track to lose more than 3% on the week. Silver's round trip has been even more violent: from an all-time high of $121.62 in late January to roughly $55 now, a decline of more than 50%, with Thursday's session marking an eight-month low. These are not small pullbacks in a bull market. They are the unwind of one of the most crowded commodity trades in years, and the past week's action shows that unwind is still running, not resting.

The gold-to-silver ratio makes the divergence explicit. It fell to roughly 50:1 in January, when silver's speculative mania was at its peak — cheap money and momentum chasing pushed silver up nearly 280% over twelve months. It has since blown back out to around 70:1, near the top of its two-year range. A ratio that swings that hard in six months isn't tracking industrial fundamentals; it's tracking which metal had more leverage stacked behind it when the trade turned. Silver did, by a wide margin, and it's paying for that now.

The mechanics of a positioning unwind

Analysts who watched the initial break in late January described it in almost identical terms. Kerstin Hottner, head of commodities at Vontobel, called it "a classic deleveraging shock," in which "extended speculative positions were forced out of a crowded momentum trade as stop-losses and margin calls cascaded through the market." Saxo Bank's Ole Hansen made the same point about self-reinforcing volatility: when prices swing too far, too fast, banks and brokers pull back to manage balance-sheet risk, and that retreat itself becomes the next leg down — more stop-losses, more margin calls, more forced selling.

The silver-specific ETF flows tell the same story in dollar terms. The iShares Silver Trust, the largest silver ETF, has seen billions of dollars in redemptions over the course of this unwind — a single-day outflow event earlier in the drawdown ran to roughly $3.4 billion, more than 6% of the fund's assets in one session. Silver's retail and speculative base, drawn in by a rally that briefly cut the gold-silver ratio to a 14-year low, is now on the other side of the trade: selling into weakness, which is precisely what a deleveraging event looks like from the flow data.

Why the dollar isn't the excuse this time

There's a second, separate force at work, and it explains why this week specifically diverged from the dollar rather than tracking it. Escalating US-Iran hostilities have pushed oil prices higher, and markets have chosen to read that as an inflation event rather than a flight-to-safety trigger — the opposite of gold's usual geopolitical playbook. Under Fed Chair Kevin Warsh, confirmed in a bitterly contested 54-45 Senate vote in May, the central bank has signaled it will answer any inflation resurgence with higher rates rather than accommodate it: nine of eighteen FOMC participants now pencil in at least one 2026 hike, a sharp reversal from the cut-or-hold consensus that prevailed as recently as the spring. By July 17, market pricing had pushed the odds of a Fed hike to roughly 73%, according to one daily precious-metals tracker — and rising real-rate expectations are a direct tax on non-yielding assets like gold and silver, dollar strength or not.

Silver carries an added vulnerability gold doesn't: roughly 58% of its demand is industrial, spanning solar panels, semiconductors and EV components. A market bracing for higher rates is also a market bracing for slower growth, which weighs on that industrial leg even as the speculative unwind hits the investment leg. Gold has one problem right now. Silver has two.

What comes next

The next hard data point is the July 28-29 FOMC meeting, ten days out, where Warsh's Fed will either validate the hike odds already priced in or push back against them — a hold with hawkish language would keep pressure on both metals, while any sign of caution on the Iran-driven inflation spike could let gold, at least, find a base. Until then, the more useful gauge than the dollar is the gold-silver ratio itself: a ratio that keeps widening past 70:1 says the deleveraging trade still has room to run; a stabilization would suggest the forced sellers are close to exhausted. Watching DXY alone, this week, would have told a trader nothing useful at all.
