US consumer signal turns defensive as discretionary lags staples

Staples over discretionary: investors paid up this week for the goods households buy regardless of sentiment. — Massimilianogalardi / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Consumer Staples rose 1.27% on the week while Consumer Discretionary fell 1.54% — a domestic-demand signal pointing the same cautious way as credit and rates, though the same spread flipped hard in May.

There is a low-tech way to read the mood of the American consumer, and it does not require waiting for the Census Bureau or the University of Michigan's next release: watch which shelf Wall Street wants to own. This week, money moved toward the one stocked with toothpaste, soap and canned soup, and away from the one stocked with cars, cruises and living-room furniture.

The Consumer Staples Select Sector SPDR (XLP) rose 1.27% over the past five trading days and is up 1.80% over the past month. The Consumer Discretionary Select Sector SPDR (XLY) fell 1.54% over five days and is now essentially flat on the month, down 0.04%. In sector-rotation terms, that is about as clean a defensive signal as the market produces: investors paying up for the things people buy no matter what, and pulling back from the things people buy only when they feel flush.

What the spread is actually measuring

The XLP/XLY spread works as a consumer-confidence proxy precisely because the two sectors are built to respond to different things. Staples — Procter & Gamble, Coca-Cola, Walmart, Costco — sell goods with inelastic demand; people buy detergent in a recession much as they do in a boom. Discretionary names — Amazon, Tesla, home builders, restaurant chains, cruise operators — sell the goods and experiences that get deferred first when households tighten up. When staples outperform discretionary, it typically means investors expect consumers to keep spending on necessities while pulling back on everything else.

That is broadly consistent with what the demand-side data has been showing. The Conference Board's confidence index inched up to 91.2 in June from a downwardly revised 90.6 in May, but the share of Americans describing the economy in pessimistic terms has climbed toward its highest level since late 2023. Advance retail sales for June came in at $768.6 billion, up a modest 0.2% from May — growth, but not the kind that signals a consumer in expansive mode. Deloitte's consumer pulse work over June and July has flagged the same pattern: discretionary spending intentions have ticked up for three straight months, but remain below pre-pandemic baselines, with households increasingly substituting toward essentials.

The May warning label

This exact trade has misfired before, and recently enough that it deserves an asterisk. In May, a similar defensive tilt — bet on staples over discretionary — was unwound within weeks as oil prices collapsed. Brent crude had spiked past $120 a barrel after Iran's closure of the Strait of Hormuz in March disrupted roughly a fifth of global oil supply; by June it had fallen back toward $85, and by some accounts closer to $70, as supply fears eased and the doomsday scenarios failed to materialize. Cheaper gasoline freed up household budgets fast, and consumer discretionary stocks rallied hard on the relief, with Bank of America's card-spending data showing households still willing to pay for vacations and dining out even as sentiment surveys stayed gloomy. A staples-over-discretionary position built in early May would have been badly wrong-footed within a month.

The lesson is not that the spread is meaningless — it is that it is a fast-moving, headline-sensitive indicator rather than a slow-moving structural one. It flips when a single input, like the price of gasoline, moves sharply enough to change the household budget math overnight. That is worth remembering before reading too much into any single week.

Why this week is different

What makes the current reading more persuasive than a standalone data point is the company it is keeping. Credit markets tilted risk-off for the first time in a month. The bear-steepening move in Treasuries that has dominated the rates conversation for three weeks paused rather than reversing into a rally. The dollar caught its breath inside an uptrend rather than breaking down. None of those, on their own, would be decisive. Together, they describe a market trimming risk in a coordinated way rather than one sector having an idiosyncratic week — and the consumer-sector spread is simply the domestic-demand chapter of that same story.

There is also a specific, near-term reason for caution to show up in cyclical consumer names right now: the labor market has been cooling, and tariff pass-through is still working its way into household costs. The Federal Reserve's own July monetary policy report flagged that a softening jobs picture alongside above-target inflation complicates the policy path, and estimates from trade economists put the annual household cost of sustained tariffs at roughly $2,500 if half the burden lands on consumers. None of that shows up instantly in a five-day sector return, but it is the kind of backdrop that makes fund managers reach for toothpaste stocks over travel stocks when they want to trim risk without leaving the market entirely.

What comes next

The next hard test for this reading arrives October 29, when the Fed announces its rate decision after a two-day meeting; consensus currently expects another hold at 3.50%–3.75%, with no new economic projections due alongside it. Retail sales for July are due August 14, and will show whether June's tepid 0.2% gain was a pause or a trend. Given how quickly this same spread reversed in May, the more useful signal will not be this week's number in isolation but whether staples keep leading discretionary through the next one or two prints. A single week of defensive rotation is a data point. A second and third week, arriving alongside a cooling labor market and a Fed still on hold, would start to look like a pattern.
