Bear-steepening takes its first breather in three weeks

The U.S. Treasury Building in Washington, D.C. — long-end yields have spent 2026 absorbing worries about the volume of debt the department needs to sell. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The 10Y eased and the 3m/10y spread narrowed for the first time in three weeks, but a steady 3M bill says it's consolidation, not a Fed repricing.

For two weeks running, the Treasury curve had one direction: steeper. The 10-year note climbed, the front end stayed put, and the gap between them widened on schedule, like clockwork nobody had asked for. This week the clock skipped a beat. The 10-year yield eased to 4.541%, down from 4.569% seven days earlier, and the 3-month-to-10-year spread narrowed to 83.4 basis points from 87.4bp — the first weekly compression since the steepening trend took hold.

It is tempting, after two weeks of one-way traffic, to read a pullback as the start of something. It probably isn't. The move is small — 2.8 basis points on the 10-year, four basis points on the spread — and it arrives against a backdrop where the thing actually driving the steepening, worry about the volume of debt Washington needs to sell, hasn't gone anywhere.

What actually moved, and what didn't

The more telling number this week sits at the short end. The 3-month bill yield held at roughly 3.707%, essentially unchanged. That steadiness matters more than the 10-year's wobble, because it says the pullback in long yields is not a repricing of what the Federal Reserve is expected to do. If traders had started pricing in a faster path to rate cuts, the front end would have moved with the back end, and it didn't move at all.

Instead, this reads as consolidation after a sharp climb — long-end investors taking profit, or simply pausing, rather than long-end investors reassessing the policy outlook. The distinction matters for anyone trying to trade the curve rather than just watch it: a Fed-driven rally would compress the whole curve. A pause within a term-premium story compresses only the long end, and only a little.

That term-premium story hasn't budged. The 10-year has spent 2026 absorbing a federal deficit running near 6% of GDP — roughly double the pre-pandemic average — with market participants increasingly expecting the Treasury to lean harder on longer-dated issuance later this year to keep the curve liquid. More supply without a matching lift in demand is a textbook recipe for higher term premium, and term premium, not Fed expectations, is what most desks credit for this year's steepening in the first place. A one-week pause doesn't touch that arithmetic.

The scar tissue

Worth being honest about how this desk has fared calling the long end lately: not well. In late June, the call here was that the 10-year would break below 4.25% by mid-July. It did the opposite. Yields pushed through 4.45% in early July and kept going, touching a two-month high near 4.62% on July 13 before this week's retreat. A sluggish July 2 jobs report (57,000 net new payrolls) briefly offered the bond-bull case some cover, but an unexpectedly favorable CPI print on July 14 — headline inflation cooling to 3.5% from 4.2% — did more to validate the Fed's patience than to revive rate-cut bets, and yields kept climbing into mid-month regardless.

That history is the reason to treat this week's pullback skeptically rather than triumphantly. The failed call was a bet that yields would fall meaningfully; they instead rose for three straight weeks before this one. A single week of consolidation, coming after that kind of overshoot, is closer to what markets normally do after a stretched move than it is evidence the pattern has reversed.

What the calendar says next

The next real test lands July 28-29, when the Federal Open Market Committee meets. Futures pricing via CME's FedWatch puts the odds of another hold at roughly 87%, consistent with a Fed still describing inflation as elevated even as it cools. A hold that reads as comfortable — no hawkish surprise on the dot plot, no fresh alarm on inflation — would likely leave the front end exactly where it's been all month, meaning any further movement in the 3m/10y spread would again have to come from the long end alone.

That keeps the relevant question narrow: does the 10-year's pause extend into a second week, suggesting the term-premium climb has genuinely stalled, or was this simply the market catching its breath before Treasury supply concerns reassert themselves into the autumn. Given how this month has gone so far, the higher-probability outcome is the latter.
