Energy is the only sector telling a consistent story this month

A pumpjack in the Permian Basin near Midland, Texas — U.S. producers are among the few able to pump freely while Gulf output stays shut in. — Eric Kounce (TexasRaiser) / Wikimedia Commons, public domain
In one sentence
XLE is the only sector positive on both the 5- and 20-day window, and the entire move traces to one cause: the Strait of Hormuz shutdown, not a broader energy re-rating.

Every other sector's weekly chart reads like a Rorschach test this month — defensives with no bid, health care in an unexplained air pocket, credit spreads that look calm only because duration is doing the work. Energy's chart needs no interpretation. The Energy Select Sector SPDR Fund (XLE) is up 0.80% over five trading days and 6.68% over twenty, and it is the only one of eleven S&P sectors positive on both windows. The next-best performer, technology, is still underwater over the month, down 1.62%. There is no ambiguity in energy's story this week, which is itself unusual enough to be the story.

The explanation sits upstream, in the crude market. United States Oil Fund shares (USO) are up roughly 9% over five days and nearly 24% over twenty, tracking a rally that has pushed Brent crude above $100 a barrel for sustained stretches this year. Unlike most single-sector outperformance, which tends to arrive wrapped in six competing explanations, this one has essentially one: the Strait of Hormuz has been effectively shut to normal tanker traffic since fighting broke out between Iran and a U.S.–Israeli coalition on February 28, 2026, and Gulf producers have had nowhere to put oil they can no longer export.

A textbook supply shock, still running

According to the International Energy Agency's September Oil Market Report, global oil production fell by 1.6 million barrels a day month-on-month in August to 100.1 million barrels a day, with more than 10 million barrels a day of Gulf output shut in amid the security crisis. The agency now expects total supply to fall 5.7 million barrels a day for the year, and it has pushed back any recovery in Gulf output to 2027. OPEC production overall has dropped more than 30% since the war began, according to Seeking Alpha reporting on Saudi Arabia's own curtailments — once onshore storage in Iraq and Kuwait filled up in March, producers simply had to stop pumping.

That is the kind of shock that shows up cleanly in equity prices. ExxonMobil and Chevron together make up roughly 35.5% of XLE's weight — 20.5% and 15% respectively — so the fund is, in practice, a leveraged bet on two companies whose upstream earnings move almost linearly with the price of crude. When crude gaps higher on a geopolitical event rather than grinding up on demand, producer margins expand immediately, with none of the lag that usually separates a commodity move from the equities that depend on it.

The refining wrinkle

The rally isn't confined to the wellhead. Refining margins — the crack spread, in industry shorthand — have hit records this year, with the benchmark 3-2-1 measure pushing past $60 a barrel, according to Forbes reporting from July. That's a second, semi-independent tailwind layered on top of crude's rise: nearly 3 million barrels a day of Gulf refining capacity has been knocked offline by the conflict, and Russian and Chinese refining disruptions have tightened diesel and jet fuel supply further. Retail gasoline prices are up 98% in 2026, according to EIA data, nearly double the 44% rise in WTI crude itself — a gap that has been very good for the refiners sitting inside XLE alongside the majors.

One clean trade, or a re-rating?

The honest answer is that nobody trading XLE this month needs a multi-factor model to explain the position — which is precisely what should make investors cautious about extrapolating it. Energy's outperformance is not evidence that the market has decided the sector deserves a higher multiple, more capital discipline credit, or a structurally better growth outlook. It is evidence that Brent is trading north of $100 because a major shipping chokepoint is closed and a quarter of global seaborne oil trade cannot move normally.

There are early signs the market itself doesn't expect the shock to be permanent. The IEA's own September report also cut its 2026 global demand forecast by 2.5 million barrels a day — the sharpest annual contraction since the pandemic — as high prices work their usual function of destroying the demand that's driving them. And diplomacy is, tentatively, in motion: Gulf Cooperation Council foreign ministers were set to meet their Iranian counterpart this week to discuss a temporary arrangement for managing shipping through the strait, according to trade press coverage cited by Trading Economics, a development that has already helped cap the most recent leg of the rally. Brent slipped back below $105 last Friday even as it held onto most of the week's 9% gain.

None of that unwinds the position XLE holders currently have. But it does mean the sector's forward path is a hostage to a ceasefire negotiation and a shipping lane, not to a re-rating of energy as an asset class. If the Strait of Hormuz reopens to normal traffic — even partially — the same arithmetic that pushed Exxon and Chevron higher on the way up works just as mechanically in reverse. The next data points worth watching are the outcome of this week's GCC-Iran talks and the IEA's October supply update, due in roughly four weeks, which will show whether any of the 10 million barrels a day currently shut in has actually come back.
