RISK · BREADTH · By S. Vance · 2026-09-13 · 3 min read

Ten of eleven sectors are red this week — energy is carrying the tape alone

The New York Stock Exchange trading floor — where this week's sector-by-sector divergence is playing out beneath a flat-looking index. — Eduard Hueber, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Only energy is positive this week; ten of eleven S&P sectors, cyclical and defensive alike, are falling together — a narrow market that breaks more easily.

Look past the headline index levels this week and the market gets a lot less comfortable. Of the eleven S&P 500 sector SPDRs, ten posted negative five-day returns. The only one in the green with any conviction is energy — XLE — riding a crude spike that has WTI above $104 a barrel and Brent near $108, as a widening geopolitical risk premium out of the Persian Gulf works its way through every other line on the tape.

Technology's XLK is the nominal second positive sector, up 0.91% on the week, but that's a rounding error next to its -1.62% slide over the past month — a sector treading water, not leading anything. Everything else, cyclical and defensive alike, is underwater. Health care (XLV) is the week's worst performer, down 4.56%, compounding a run of managed-care margin pressure and reimbursement uncertainty that has made it one of the more idiosyncratic underperformers of the year. But idiosyncratic stories don't explain why utilities, staples, industrials, financials and materials are all red at the same time.

A one-legged rally is not a broad market

That's the part worth sitting with. Breadth — how many components of an index are actually participating in a move — is one of the oldest tells in market technicals, and by any conventional reading, this week's breadth is bad. When a single commodity-shock sector is propping up index-level performance while the other ten sectors, spanning both growth-sensitive cyclicals and defensive havens, all fall together, that is not a market rotating from one theme to another. It is a market with one engine running.

The mechanism is straightforward enough: oil above $100 acts like a tax, pulling spending power out of consumers and margin out of energy-intensive businesses, which is exactly why cyclicals should struggle in an oil shock. What is harder to explain away is the defensive side. Utilities and health care are the sectors investors typically hide in when growth wobbles, and they are getting no bid either — a sign the market isn't rotating toward safety so much as broadly de-risking, with energy exposure treated as the only asset actually correlated with the shock itself.

Narrow markets are structurally more fragile than broad ones. When performance depends on a single sector, a reversal in the one working trade — a de-escalation in the Gulf, a pullback in crude, a rotation of profit-taking out of energy names that are up 40%-plus year-to-date — leaves nothing underneath to catch the index. There is no second engine idling in reserve; cyclicals and defensives are both already negative.

The next tell will be whether breadth stays this narrow if oil prices stabilize. If XLE cools and nothing else picks up the slack, that says less about energy and more about the health of everything it's currently masking.
