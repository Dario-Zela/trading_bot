Oil-importing emerging markets catch the terms-of-trade downside

A crude oil tanker at a refinery jetty — energy-import costs are the fault line splitting emerging markets into winners and losers this month. — Calistemon / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
A firmer dollar plus a 24% oil surge is squeezing oil-importing emerging markets from both sides after a month of currency breathing room.

For most of August, emerging-market currencies had the wind at their backs. The dollar was sliding — the DXY index down roughly 0.84% over twenty trading days — and that alone did a lot of quiet work for finance ministries from New Delhi to Jakarta, cheapening the cost of servicing dollar debt and dollar-priced imports alike. That breathing room is now closing from both directions at once.

Oil has surged 23.9% over the same twenty-day stretch, with Brent crude pushing toward $99 a barrel on the back of an escalating Middle East confrontation between the United States and Iran. At the same time, the dollar has stopped falling: the DXY is up 0.12% over the past five sessions, a modest but telling reversal after a month-long slide. For an oil-importing emerging economy, that combination is close to the worst available — a bigger energy bill priced in a currency that is no longer getting cheaper.

The MSCI emerging-market currency index has already registered the shift, slipping as Brent approached the $100 mark. India and Hungary were among the session's weakest performers on September 8th, and the rupee has since weakened past ₹95 to the dollar, giving back gains built over the summer. India imports roughly 85% of its crude, and its average import price has climbed from $82 a barrel in July to nearly $99 now — a jump that widens the current account deficit and forces the Reserve Bank of India into the now-familiar position of intervening to defend the currency rather than easing policy to support growth.

Not every importer is equally exposed

The pain is not evenly spread. Analysts tracking the shock have sorted emerging economies into rough tiers: a high-risk group running twin fiscal and current-account deficits alongside heavy energy dependence — Egypt, Romania, Sri Lanka, Kenya, Jordan and Bangladesh among them — and a better-cushioned middle tier that includes India, Turkey, Thailand, the Philippines and Hungary, where deeper reserves and more credible central banks blunt some of the impact. Indonesia, a net oil importer since losing its OPEC-exporter status over a decade ago, has seen the rupiah come under similar pressure as Brent climbs.

That the squeeze is concentrated in oil rather than commodities broadly is itself informative. Base metals, tracked via the DBB fund, are roughly flat over the same twenty days — down 1.47% over five days, up just 0.43% over twenty — suggesting industrial demand and broader input costs aren't driving the story. This is an energy-specific shock, not a general inflation wave, which is exactly why it hits oil importers so asymmetrically while leaving exporters like Brazil, Nigeria and Gulf states better off.

The next test comes quickly: central bank meetings in India, Indonesia and Turkey over the coming weeks will show whether policymakers hold rates to defend currencies or look through the shock as temporary. A durable break above $100 a barrel would make that a much harder call.
