Gold and silver just gave back a month of gains in a week

Gold bullion: the classic inflation hedge just had its worst week in months. — Stevebidmead / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
Gold and silver's worst week in months was driven by a dollar bounce and rising long-end yields, not by any change in the oil-driven inflation story.

Gold had spent the summer doing exactly what it is supposed to do. Every worry about sticky inflation, a wobbling dollar, or a central bank cutting into a hot economy sent another few billion into bullion. Then, in the space of five trading sessions, a chunk of that trade came apart. The SPDR Gold Shares ETF (GLD) fell 2.79% on the week to close at $398.77. The iShares Silver Trust (SLV), which usually moves harder in both directions, dropped 4.01% to $58.12.

The timing is the tell. This didn't happen because inflation expectations collapsed — if anything, the week's dominant story was oil ripping through $100 a barrel on Middle East and Russia-Ukraine supply disruptions, the kind of shock that classically helps the gold trade. It happened because the dollar found a bid and the long end of the Treasury curve kept climbing, with the 30-year yield pushing to 5.354%. That combination — a firmer greenback and higher yields, real and nominal — is the one thing that reliably beats gold regardless of what oil is doing.

A hedge with no yield, in a world suddenly full of it

Gold pays no coupon. That is the whole trade-off investors accept when they buy it: no income, but a claim that is supposed to hold its value when currencies or bonds don't. The cost of that trade-off is opportunity cost, and this week the opportunity cost went up sharply. The dollar index (DXY) firmed 0.12% over five days — a modest-looking number, but it came after a run in which traders had been positioned for the dollar to keep weakening, so it was enough to unwind carry. Core CPI data landed a touch hot, at 0.3% month-on-month, and futures markets moved the odds of a Federal Reserve rate hike at the September 15–16 meeting toward roughly 90%, according to CME pricing tracked this week. A Fed under Chair Kevin Warsh that hikes into an oil shock is not a dovish Fed, and gold does not like a hawkish Fed even when the hike is nominally about the same inflation gold is supposed to hedge.

That's the cynical part of this story. The inflation case that justified June's and July's gold rally — an economy running hot, a central bank behind the curve, a currency at risk of debasement — has not gone away. Oil at $100-plus is arguably the strongest confirmation of that case all year, a point made elsewhere in this section's coverage of the bear-steepening curve. But positioning doesn't wait for the fundamental thesis to be disproven. It waits for the price action to turn, and once gold broke down through levels that systematic and momentum accounts had been defending, the unwind fed on itself. Silver, with a smaller, more crowded, and more leveraged retail base, always moves further in both directions — hence the 4% single-week drop against gold's 2.8%.

Silver's leverage cuts both ways

Silver's relationship to gold is usually described as "gold with a turbocharger," and this week showed why that cuts both ways. Silver carries an industrial-demand component — solar panels, electronics — that gold doesn't, which normally gives it an extra leg up during reflation scares. But it also trades on thinner float and with more retail and momentum money than gold, which means when the macro backdrop flips, silver's move is amplified rather than dampened. A 4.01% weekly drop in SLV against a 2.79% drop in GLD is almost exactly the multiple silver typically runs against gold — not a sign that silver's specific story broke, but a sign that whatever hit gold hit silver harder, mechanically.

That mechanical relationship matters because it argues against reading this week as new information about the metals themselves. Nothing changed in the supply of silver for solar cells. Nothing changed in central bank gold buying, which has been the structural bid under the market for two years. What changed is the price of carrying a non-yielding asset when the 30-year Treasury yield is over 5.35% and the dollar has stopped falling — and that price is set in the Treasury and FX markets, not in the metals themselves.

The Fed meeting is the next mile marker

The Federal Reserve's rate decision on September 16, with Chair Warsh's press conference, is the next scheduled point where this can either extend or reverse. A hike, or hawkish language even without one, keeps real yields elevated and gives the dollar another reason to firm — bad for gold on the same mechanical grounds as this week. A hold paired with dovish signaling on the labor market would remove some of the pressure that did the damage, though it would do nothing to resolve the oil-driven inflation risk that arguably should still be gold's friend.

The uncomfortable read for anyone who owned gold as an inflation hedge going into this week is that the hedge just proved it can be unwound by the very forces — rate expectations, dollar strength — that a genuine inflation shock is supposed to override. If oil holds above $100 and the Fed still leans hawkish next week, gold and silver may be about to find out which of those two forces actually wins.
