The dollar's 3% monthly gain is pressure for everyone else

In one sentence
A steady 3.1% dollar climb on high US yields is tightening conditions abroad and coincides with falls of 3.6% in gold and 5.7% in silver; indebted, import-reliant emerging markets look most exposed.

The dollar has been rising slowly and steadily, and the effect is felt well outside the United States. The ICE dollar index (DXY) closed at 102.21 on 9 October. That is up 3.1% over 20 trading days, and the latest week added another 0.27%. No single session defined the move. It is a grind, and a grind is harder for other economies to plan around than a spike would be.

The backdrop is a US bond market paying handsomely for the privilege of lending to Washington. The 10-year Treasury yield sits near 5.25%, and the curve is about 119 basis points steep. Elsewhere in this edition, the steep-curve analysis shows the long end doing most of the work. A dollar that firms while US yields are this high is not a coincidence. Capital earns more at home, and holding anyone else's currency costs more in forgone carry.

How a firm dollar tightens conditions abroad

A stronger dollar acts like a tax on anyone who borrows in it without earning in it. Dollar debt issued by foreign companies and governments gets harder to service when local currency buys fewer dollars. Global trade is invoiced heavily in dollars, so importers elsewhere pay more in domestic terms for the same goods. Central banks outside the US then face a choice. They can let their currencies slide and import inflation, or they can hold rates higher than their own economies would otherwise justify.

A 3% monthly move is not a crisis by itself. The pressure comes from the combination of a rising dollar and high US yields. Each one alone is manageable. Together they tighten financial conditions in places that never changed their own policy.

The commodity channel

The clearest market fingerprint so far is in precious metals. The gold ETF GLD is down 3.56% over 20 days and the silver ETF SLV is down 5.75%. Both are priced in dollars, so a rising dollar makes them dearer for buyers holding other currencies. That tends to damp demand at the margin. Higher real yields also raise the opportunity cost of holding assets that pay nothing.

The pattern fits the story, but the evidence has limits. The metals fell at roughly the pace the dollar rose, and silver fell by more than gold, as it usually does in a risk-off or rates-driven move because it is the more volatile and more industrially exposed metal. Correlation over one month does not prove the dollar caused the slide. Yields, positioning and demand all play a part. The gold-bounce-month-down piece covers why gold rose on the week yet remains lower on the month. The dollar's pause, or lack of one, is part of that picture.

Who is most exposed if the climb continues

Three groups look most vulnerable.

  • Emerging markets with large dollar debt. Their borrowing costs rise with the greenback, and they have the least room to offset it with policy. Commodity exporters among them face a second hit if dollar-priced raw materials keep sliding. Our emerging-markets analysis notes that commodities are mixed rather than uniformly weak, which makes the outcome country-specific.
  • Import-dependent economies with weak currencies. Energy and food are bought in dollars, so a stronger dollar feeds directly into local inflation and limits what central banks can do to support growth.
  • Commodity-linked developed markets. Their currencies and equity indices tend to trade with raw-material prices, so a dollar-driven slide in metals weighs on them even when domestic conditions are sound.

The US sits at the centre of this arrangement. As our regional overview argues, high yields and a firm dollar set the terms for everyone else.

What to watch

The next test is whether 102 holds as support. A drift below it would suggest the rate-differential argument is fading. A move well above it, alongside a 10-year yield staying near 5.25%, would extend the squeeze. Two data points will do most of the work. The first is the next US inflation print, which shapes expectations for the Federal Reserve. The second is any move in the long end of the Treasury curve. The long-bond tail risk is the scenario in which a disorderly rise in yields drags the dollar higher and tightens conditions abroad faster than anyone planned for.
