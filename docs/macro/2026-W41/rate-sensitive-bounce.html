What a 5.25% 10Y does to the equity discount rate

In one sentence
Utilities and real estate bounced hard but remain down on the month; with the 10Y at 5.244%, a close above about 5.40% would end the relief rally.

Utilities and real estate, the two sectors that most resemble bonds, had a good week. They rose even though the 10-year Treasury yield sat at 5.244%, a level at which the usual logic says they should struggle. The question is whether that is a relief bounce inside a downtrend or a sign that the market has accepted the new level of yields.

The numbers favour caution. The utilities sector (XLU) gained 3.97% over five days but is still down 2.31% over 20. Real estate (XLRE) gained 1.96% over the week and is still down 4.17% over the month. Both bounced, and neither has recovered what it lost.

Why these sectors feel the 10-year first

A stock is worth the cash it will pay out, discounted back to today. The 10-year yield is the base of that discount rate. When the base rises, every future dollar is worth less now. The effect is largest where the cash flows are steady, long-dated and fixed in size, which describes regulated utilities and rent-collecting real estate. Their dividends compete directly with Treasuries for the same income-seeking money.

A stylised example shows the scale. Suppose a steady payer is priced at a required return of 8% with 3% growth, a five-point gap. Raise the required return by half a point with growth unchanged, and the fair value falls by roughly 9%. A half-point move in the 10-year is easily enough to cause that. This is an illustration, not a forecast for either sector. It does explain why a month of rising yields can take several percent off these sectors while the broad market moves less.

At 5.244%, the hurdle is also high in absolute terms. A government bond now pays more than many utility dividend yields, so an investor takes on equity risk and gets less income than the risk-free alternative offers. That gap has to be closed by growth, by a lower price, or by lower yields.

Relief bounce or regime change

The pattern in the data looks like a relief bounce. A week of gains of 3.97% and 1.96% against 20-day losses of 2.31% and 4.17% means the recent move is mostly a partial recovery. A regime change would show the sectors holding gains while yields stay high or rise. The market would be saying that 5.25% is simply the new price of money, and these sectors would be rebuilding on it. Nothing here shows that yet.

The bounce also arrived as defensives in general were being bought, which is a different driver from falling yields. That is the argument in our companion piece on a defensive rally on a rising-rate backdrop. Money moving into safe sectors for safety's sake can lift utilities for a few sessions without any change in the discount rate. Buying like that tends to fade when the pressure that prompted it eases.

The shape of the curve points the same way. The yield curve is 119 basis points steep, and the long end is doing the work, as we set out in the piece on the steep curve. A rise driven by longer maturities hits long-duration equities hardest, because it raises the rate at which their most distant cash flows are discounted.

The level that ends the bounce

A bounce needs a line that tells the reader when it is over. A close in the 10-year above roughly 5.40% would be that line: about 15 basis points above the current yield and a fresh high for the move. That figure is our judgment, not a market convention. A yield that makes new highs means the discount rate is still rising, and the monthly losses in utilities and real estate would be likely to resume. A yield that holds below 5.25% and drifts lower would show that the bounce has support in rates and not only in sector rotation.

The tail risk sits beyond that line. A disorderly move in the long bond would reprice every duration-heavy asset at once, a scenario covered in the risk piece. The dollar's gain of about 3% on the month, discussed in the FX piece, adds to the pressure on everyone else.

The next test is the 10-year's behaviour around 5.25% in the coming sessions. If it holds below that level and the sectors keep their gains, the case for a durable bounce improves. A close above 5.40% would end it.
