The US is the story: high yields and a firm dollar set the terms for everyone else

In one sentence
US yields above 5% at the long end and a dollar up 3.12% in 20 days are the main external pressure on the UK, euro area, China and emerging markets.

The United States is where this week's story starts, and the other blocs are mostly receiving it. Long-dated Treasury yields have climbed to levels that make dollar assets hard to ignore, and the dollar has strengthened alongside them. For economies that borrow, trade or price commodities in dollars, those two moves are a change in the weather that arrives whether or not anything has changed at home.

The US sets the terms

The benchmark 10-year Treasury yield stands at 5.244%. The 30-year is at 5.600%. The curve is about 119 basis points steep, and as our companion piece on the long end argues, the longer maturities are doing most of the work. Investors are demanding more to hold duration, and the market is pricing that demand at the far end of the curve rather than in near-term policy expectations.

The dollar has moved with it. The DXY index, which measures the currency against a basket of major peers, is up 3.12% over 20 trading days. A gain of that size in a month is not a rounding error for a reserve currency. It tightens financial conditions abroad without a single foreign central banker voting on anything.

Equity leadership at home has a defensive cast, which is consistent with a market that is wary of the rate backdrop rather than relaxed about it. Defensive sectors have led while technology has paused near the top of its monthly range, and financials, despite a 2.3% bounce on the week, remain down 4.4% over 20 days. A rally led by defensives while yields rise is not a clean all-clear, and the same logic applies to anyone looking to the US for a signal about global risk appetite.

How the pressure travels

The transmission channel is worth spelling out, because this week's data contains no direct readings on regional bonds or equities outside the US. What follows is the mechanism, not a measurement of its effects.

First, yields. When the US risk-free rate is 5%-plus at the long end, every other sovereign borrower competes with it. Gilts and euro-area government bonds do not have to move one-for-one with Treasuries, but they have to offer enough to keep global capital from drifting toward the US. The cost of that competition falls hardest on issuers with weaker fiscal credibility.

Second, the currency. A stronger dollar raises the local-currency cost of dollar debt and of dollar-priced imports such as energy. For the UK and the euro area, it also imports a degree of inflation through weaker currencies, which complicates the job of central banks trying to ease.

Third, commodities. Dollar strength typically weighs on dollar-priced raw materials, but the evidence this week is mixed. Gold rose on the week yet is still down 3.6% on the month, a pattern consistent with a strong dollar and high real yields working against a non-yielding asset. Our emerging-markets piece takes up the split in more detail.

China and emerging markets

The one regional gauge available is indirect. The base-metals fund DBB is down 0.98% over 20 days. That is a read on industrial demand with China as its largest swing factor, but it is not a direct measure of Chinese activity, and a decline under 1% is too small to carry a strong conclusion. It fits a picture of soft rather than collapsing demand, and nothing more.

For emerging markets the exposure is more straightforward. Dollar funding costs rise with US yields, and a dollar that gains 3% in a month squeezes those with large dollar liabilities and little commodity cushion. Commodity exporters have some offset if prices hold; importers have none. That the commodity signals are mixed is precisely why the outcome differs by country rather than by bloc.

What to watch

The credit market offers an early read. Investment grade has outrun high yield this week, a gap we flagged as a small warning; a widening of it would suggest the tighter conditions are starting to bite on weaker borrowers. The next signals are the long end of the Treasury curve, where a disorderly move in the 30-year is the tail risk described in our risk piece, and the DXY itself. If the dollar's 20-day gain extends well beyond 3%, the pressure on the UK, the euro area, China-linked demand and emerging markets stops being background and becomes the main event. Regional bond and equity readings, absent this week, are the data that would show whether it already has.
