Emerging markets face a stronger dollar with commodities mixed

In one sentence
Gas is rising while oil, metals and farm goods slip, and with a firmer dollar, gas exporters look better placed than dollar-indebted peers selling softer commodities.

Natural gas is rising while oil, industrial metals and agricultural goods are all lower over the past month. For emerging markets that sell raw materials, the result is less a single commodity story than a sorting exercise. Some exporters are getting paid more. Others are being paid less, in a currency that is getting more expensive to borrow.

The evidence comes from exchange-traded funds that track each market, which are an indirect read on what exporters actually receive. The United States Natural Gas Fund (UNG) is up 5.2% over five days and 8.3% over 20. The United States Oil Fund (USO) closed at 148.20, down 4.3% over 20 days. The Invesco DB Agriculture fund (DBA) is down 2.1% and its base-metals counterpart (DBB) is down 1.0% over the same stretch.

A split in terms of trade

The spread between those numbers is what matters. A country's terms of trade, the price of what it sells against the price of what it buys, depends on its particular export basket, and the four baskets are pulling in different directions.

Gas exporters have the clearest tailwind. Oil producers face a softer price, though a 4.3% monthly move is a drift rather than a collapse. Copper-, aluminium- and zinc-linked economies are barely worse off, given DBB's 1.0% decline. Grain and soft-commodity sellers sit in between, with agriculture down about 2%.

There are limits to this reading. These funds hold futures, not physical cargoes, and UNG in particular is known to diverge from spot prices because of how it rolls contracts. Many gas exporters also sell under long-term contracts, some priced off oil, so a spot rally may reach their revenues slowly or only in part. The ETF moves show direction, not what any single country is collecting.

The dollar adds the second half

The other pressure is the currency. The dollar has gained roughly 3% over the month, a move examined in more detail elsewhere in this edition. A stronger dollar typically lowers the local-currency value of dollar-priced commodities, and it raises the cost of servicing dollar debt for governments and companies that borrowed abroad.

That is where the split sharpens. An exporter with rising gas revenue and modest external debt can absorb a firmer dollar. An oil or agricultural exporter with softer receipts and large dollar liabilities gets squeezed from both sides. US yields compound the problem: the 10-year Treasury is near 5.25%, and the US-led rate backdrop sets the benchmark that emerging-market borrowers are priced against.

The data here do not include country-level currency, spread or debt figures, so the claim is one of exposure rather than demonstrated stress. The pressure on dollar debtors is the standard transmission from a stronger dollar and higher US rates, not something these numbers confirm directly.

What to watch

The test is whether the gas rally lasts. A move of 8% over 20 days is not yet a trend, and a reversal would remove the one offset in the commodity complex. On the other side, the dollar's path and the long end of the US curve decide how heavy the debt burden becomes. The next readings on the dollar index and the 10-year yield will show whether the pressure on emerging-market borrowers is building or easing.
