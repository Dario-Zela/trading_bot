The curve is 119 basis points steep, and the long end is doing the work

In one sentence
A 119 basis point 3M-10Y spread with most of the steepening in the 10Y-30Y stretch points to term premium and supply, not Fed policy, as the force driving long yields.

The Treasury curve has been upside down for so long that a normal one now looks like news. As of Friday's close, the three-month bill yielded 4.06%, the five-year 5.02%, the ten-year 5.24% and the thirty-year 5.60%. The gap between the three-month and the ten-year is 1.19 percentage points. Lenders can borrow cheaply at the short end and are paid handsomely for lending long. That is the textbook shape of a healthy curve, but how the curve got there matters more than the shape.

Where the steepness sits

The steepening is not evenly spread. From the five-year to the ten-year, the yield rises 22 basis points. From the ten-year to the thirty-year, it rises another 36. The five-year to thirty-year spread is about 58 basis points, and more than 60% of it is earned in the last stretch of the curve.

That progression is the evidence. If the curve were steepening because markets expected the Fed to cut rates faster, the move would show up in the front end and the middle: lower short yields, with the long end roughly anchored. Here the three-month bill sits at 4.06% while the long bond sits well above 5.5%. The spread is wide because the far end is demanding more, and the near end is not offering less.

Policy expectations can explain the left side of this curve. They struggle to explain the right side. A thirty-year bond reflects the average of short rates over three decades, plus whatever compensation investors require for holding it. When that compensation rises, the long end moves on its own, whatever the Fed does next month.

What is pricing the long end

Three candidates compete, and the data on hand cannot separate them cleanly.

Supply. Large and persistent federal deficits mean the Treasury must keep selling duration, and buyers have to be coaxed into taking it. Auction demand at the long end is the place that shows up first.

Term premium. This is the extra yield investors demand for the risk that rates and inflation turn out different from expected. It cannot be observed directly, only estimated from models, and the estimates differ. But a long bond that outpaces the ten-year by 36 basis points while the front end sits still is what a rising term premium looks like in the raw numbers.

Inflation risk. If investors doubt that inflation will settle where policymakers want it, they will ask for more yield to lend for thirty years. The firm dollar, up about 3% over the month, and gold, down 3.6% over the same period, both read more like a story of rising real yields than of inflation panic. That argues the move is about compensation for uncertainty and supply more than about a fresh inflation scare. It is an inference from market pricing, not a measurement.

Whichever mix is right, the practical conclusion is the same. With the three-month at 4.06% and the thirty-year at 5.60%, the term premium story outweighs any near-term policy story. A single Fed meeting moves the left edge of the curve. It does little to the right edge.

What a 10Y above 5.25% does to stocks

The ten-year at 5.24% sits a hair under the level that has become a reference point for equity investors. Equities are long-duration assets: the further out a company's earnings arrive, the more a higher discount rate shrinks their present value. Growth and technology stocks, whose cash flows sit furthest in the future, are the most exposed. That is part of the case in the companion piece on what a 5.25% ten-year does to the equity discount rate.

The market's own behaviour fits that logic. Defensive sectors have led while technology has paused near the top of its monthly range, a pattern covered in the sector report. A defensive rally is not an all-clear when the thing pressing on valuations is still rising.

The financials puzzle

Banks are supposed to like a steep curve. They fund themselves at short rates and lend at long ones, so a 119 basis point spread should widen margins. Yet financials fell 4.4% over 20 days, even after a 2.3% bounce this week.

Two plausible reasons, neither of them proven by the figures here. First, the way the curve steepened matters. A long end that rises because of term premium cuts the market value of the securities banks already hold, which is a balance-sheet cost that offsets the margin benefit. Second, a rising long end often comes with tighter financial conditions, which raises worries over credit quality. Investment grade outran high yield this week, and that gap is a small warning in itself; see the credit note on how IG outran HY.

The steep curve, in other words, is not rewarding the sector that is supposed to profit most from it. That is the clearest sign that the market reads the steepening as a stress signal and not as a growth signal.

What to watch

The nearest tests are the next long-dated Treasury auctions, where weak demand would confirm the supply reading, and the monthly inflation report, which would show whether the compensation being demanded at the long end has a basis in prices. A ten-year close above 5.25% would put the equity discount rate back in focus. The risk the market is trying to price, a disorderly move in the long bond, is taken up in the risk section's piece on the long-bond tail.
