Gold rose on the week but is still down 3.6% on the month

In one sentence
GLD gained 1.17% this week but remains 3.56% lower on the month, with silver weaker still, as a firm dollar and 5%+ yields weigh on non-yielding assets.

Gold has had a good week inside a bad month. The largest gold exchange-traded fund, GLD, closed at 384.58, up 1.17% over five sessions. It is still 3.56% lower than it was 20 sessions ago, and the bounce has done little to change that picture.

A bounce, not yet a turn

The arithmetic is plain. A 3.56% monthly decline puts GLD's level a month ago at roughly 399. This week's gain has recovered about a third of the ground lost since then. The fund would need to climb a further 3.7% or so to get back to where the slide began.

Silver is the weaker witness. SLV closed at 54.78, flat on the week at +0.07%, and is down 5.75% over 20 days. Silver usually behaves like a higher-beta version of gold. It tends to lead when the metals are rallying and lag when conviction is thin, and a week of nothing from it while gold gained more than 1% suggests the buyers were not broad.

Base metals tell a different story. The DBB fund rose 1.08% on the week and is down only 0.98% on the month, a far shallower decline than either precious metal. That points to industrial demand, or at least industrial sentiment, holding up better than the havens. Gold's weakness is therefore not part of a general commodity rout.

Two forces pulling opposite ways

The question is whether a safe-haven bid and a real-yield headwind can coexist. They can, but they are not equals. Gold pays nothing, so its opportunity cost rises with what a investor can earn elsewhere. With the 10-year Treasury at 5.25% and the dollar up about 3% on the month (see the dollar's monthly gain), holding bullion is expensive in two ways: forgone income, and a currency in which gold is priced that is itself appreciating.

The haven argument has not vanished. A defensive tilt in equities and worries about the long end of the bond market are the sort of conditions that usually send some money toward gold (see the tail risk in the long bond). The month's price action implies that, so far, the yield and dollar effects have outweighed it. The 1.17% weekly gain is consistent with a modest haven bid arriving on top of a market still leaning the other way.

One caution applies. A rise in nominal yields is not automatically a rise in real yields, since inflation expectations can lift both. The triage data here does not separate the two, and that distinction matters for how long the pressure persists.

What would confirm a turn

Three things would carry more weight than another good week.

  • Silver joining in. SLV moving meaningfully higher, rather than treading water, would show the bounce is broadening.
  • GLD reclaiming roughly 399. That is the level the 20-day slide started from, and a close back above it would erase the monthly loss.
  • The backdrop easing. A pause in the dollar's advance or a retreat in long-dated yields would remove the headwind that explains the month.

Without those, the more natural reading is a rally within a downtrend. The next test is how GLD behaves if the 10-year pushes further above 5.25% or the dollar extends its run. Gold holding its weekly gains through that would say more than the gains themselves.
