SECTORS · DEFENSIVE LEADERSHIP · By I. Aresti · 2026-11-01 · 4 min read

Health care leads, and the defensives are now the momentum trade

In one sentence
XLV rose 3.1% on the week and 11.2% on the month, with staples and utilities close behind, but industrials' -1.5% 20-day return leaves the breadth of the move unproven.

The stock market's most reliable hiding places are leading again, and they are doing it while borrowing costs climb. Health care, consumer staples and utilities, the sectors investors usually buy for steadiness rather than growth, have been the best performers over both the past week and the past month. Defensive leadership is common in a sell-off. It is less common when the 10-year Treasury yield is 4.93%, because rising yields normally hurt the bond-like, dividend-paying shares that defensives are supposed to resemble.

The leaders

The Health Care Select Sector SPDR (XLV) gained 3.12% over five trading days and 11.25% over 20. Consumer staples (XLP) are close behind on the month at +11.04%, and utilities (XLU) have risen 9.04%. A double-digit monthly gain in staples is large for a group whose appeal is that it rarely moves much. For health care it is a decisive outperformance.

The pattern fits the rest of this edition's picture. Communication services and technology have absorbed the heaviest repricing (see Communication services and technology take the brunt of the repricing), and the wider move out of growth and into cash-generative businesses is covered in the rotation piece. Defensive leadership is the other side of that trade: money leaving long-duration growth has to land somewhere, and it has landed in earnings that look durable and dividends that look dependable.

The rate backdrop makes the move harder to read as simple fear. With the 30-year above 5.3% (see the long-end pressure), utilities and staples face a higher hurdle from risk-free alternatives. That they are rising anyway suggests buyers are paying for earnings stability and relative strength, not only for yield. In other words, defensives have become a momentum trade, and momentum buyers do not need a falling-rate story.

Not only a flight to safety

A pure flight to safety would show cyclicals lagging. Materials and industrials are not lagging. Materials (XLB) rose 2.79% on the week and industrials (XLI) rose 1.7%. Those are sectors that normally trade on growth, capital spending and commodity prices, and their gains cut against a story of investors simply hiding.

Several explanations are plausible, and the data here cannot separate them. Oil's roughly 15% monthly rise (discussed in the emerging-market and Europe piece) points to a commodity-price impulse that could be lifting materials directly. Industrials may be catching a bid as investors rotate away from the largest growth names toward businesses with tangible assets. Or both may be benefiting from the same dispersion running beneath the index (see US equities rotating beneath a steady surface). What the weekly gains do establish is that the market is selecting by quality and cash flow more than by a single risk-on or risk-off switch.

How broad is it?

The industrials number is the caution flag. Despite the 1.7% weekly gain, the sector is still down 1.50% over 20 days. One good week has not undone a poor month. If the week proves the start of a turn, industrials would be expected to keep gaining and pull the monthly figure positive. If it proves a bounce, the monthly deficit would reassert itself.

The breadth test therefore has three parts:

  • Persistence in the defensives. Gains of 9% to 11% in a month leave the three leading groups stretched. Momentum can carry further, but the next leg needs fresh buyers, not the same ones.
  • Confirmation from cyclicals. Industrials moving from -1.50% to positive over 20 days would show participation beyond defensive and rate-insensitive names. Materials holding its weekly gain would do the same.
  • Credit's verdict. High yield has outrun investment grade (see High yield outruns investment grade), which says credit markets are not pricing stress. That is consistent with rotation rather than retreat, and it argues against reading defensive leadership as a warning of recession.

There is also a risk the leadership has not priced. A 5% long end is a tail risk for all equities, and defensives are not immune, since they carry the highest valuation sensitivity among the sectors that are currently winning (see the tail-risk piece). A further jump in long yields would test whether utilities and staples are being bought for stability or for a yield they can no longer offer in competition with Treasuries.

What to watch

The policy path is the swing factor, and the shape of the curve will shape what comes next (see the steep front end). Any move in the 10-year back below 4.93% would help the rate-sensitive leaders, while a push higher would put their momentum under strain. In sector terms, the cleanest signal is industrials: a positive 20-day return would suggest the market is broadening, while another week of lagging would suggest the current leadership is narrow and defensive after all.
