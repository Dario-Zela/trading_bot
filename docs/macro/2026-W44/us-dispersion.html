US equities are rotating beneath a steady index surface

In one sentence
A sector spread of more than eight points, from XLC's -5.3% to XLV's +3.1%, sits beneath a calm US index, with the 10-year at 4.93% setting the terms.

Under a market index that barely moved, the parts of the US market moved a great deal. Over the past week the best-performing sector fund, health care (XLV), gained 3.12%, while the worst, communication services (XLC), lost 5.32%. That is a spread of more than eight percentage points between the two ends. A broad index can look calm when the sectors inside it are pulling in opposite directions and the moves roughly cancel.

What the spread says

The pattern fits the week's wider story of money leaving growth for defensives. Communication services and technology sit at the bottom, and health care leads. Those sectors are the market's most heavily weighted areas, and their weakness is being absorbed by gains elsewhere. A steady headline number therefore says little about conviction. It says that selling in one part of the market was met by buying in another.

The supplied data cover sector funds only. Regional breakdowns within the US, such as small caps against large caps or the performance of individual states' economies, were not available this week. The reading here rests on sector returns alone and should be treated as partial.

The long end sets the terms

The backdrop is a bond market that is charging more for time. The 10-year Treasury yield stands at 4.93%, and the gap between the 3-month bill and the 10-year is 96 basis points. A curve that steep is a notable change from the flat or inverted shapes of recent years. It means investors are demanding a meaningful premium for lending long, with the 30-year above 5.3%.

Higher long-term yields cut the present value of earnings expected far in the future. That is the usual pressure on growth-oriented sectors, and it is consistent with what the sector table shows. Sectors that pay out cash today, or whose earnings are less sensitive to the economic cycle, have held up better. This is an inference from the coincidence of the two moves rather than a demonstrated cause, since the week's data do not isolate what drove each sector.

The dollar adds little to the picture. The dollar index (DXY) is at 101.88, in a range that suggests no sharp swing in foreign demand for US assets. If the rotation were driven by an exodus from US holdings, the currency would likely show it. For now it does not.

What to watch

Two things would test the calm. One is whether the 10-year holds near 5% or pushes through it. A move higher would raise the cost of the sector rotation, because the defensive trade would then have to absorb more selling from growth names. The other is whether index-level stability survives another week of sector losses this size. Offsetting moves can persist, but they depend on the winners continuing to win. The next batch of Treasury auctions and the Federal Reserve's coming communications will show whether the long end steadies or keeps drifting higher.
