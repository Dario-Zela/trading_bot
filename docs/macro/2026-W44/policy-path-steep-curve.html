A steep front end leaves the policy path as the swing factor

In one sentence
With the 3-month at 3.97% and the 10-year near 4.93%, the curve prices easing without recession, and whether long yields follow a data slowdown or defy it decides the path.

The Treasury curve has a shape that is easy to read and hard to trade. The 3-month bill yields 3.97%. The 10-year note sits near 4.93%. The gap, 96 basis points, is positive and wide, and it says the bond market expects the Federal Reserve to lower short-term rates while refusing to lower the price of long-term money along with it.

That combination is not what a recession looks like. In a downturn scare, long yields fall alongside short ones, because investors flee to duration. Here the front end is anchored low by expected easing and the back end is held up by something else. The 30-year yield is above 5.3%, which leaves the whole curve tilted upward from a low starting point.

What the shape is saying

Three readings sit inside the 96 basis points. The first is that policy is expected to ease from here, which explains the low front end. The second is that growth is not breaking: a market braced for a slump would not demand nearly 5% to lend for ten years. The third is that investors want extra compensation for holding long bonds, the so-called term premium, whether because of heavy Treasury issuance, inflation uncertainty, or both.

Oil adds to the third. The US Oil Fund (USO) has gained 14.6% over 20 days. Energy costs feed quickly into headline inflation and slowly into expectations, and a bond investor locked in for three decades is the one most exposed to that drift. The pressure is already visible at the long end, a theme taken up in the 10-year and 30-year duration piece.

Two ways the curve can steepen

The curve is positive, so the question is how it gets steeper, and the two routes imply opposite things for other assets.

In a bull steepener, short yields fall faster than long yields. The trigger would be weaker data: softer hiring, cooling consumption, or both. The market would pull forward its easing bets, the 3-month would drop, and the 10-year would fall by less. Bonds rally overall, and the curve steepens because the front end leads.

In a bear steepener, long yields rise faster than short ones. The trigger would be inflation that refuses to cool, helped by dearer oil, or a bigger supply of long-dated debt. The Fed could still cut at the front while the 10-year and 30-year sell off. Bonds lose money, and the curve steepens because the back end leads. A move toward a long end well above 5% is the tail risk that equity leadership has not priced, as covered in the long-end tail-risk piece.

The observable that separates them

The cleanest tell is what happens to the long end on a day when the front end falls. If a soft labor or activity report pushes the 3-month lower and the 10-year follows it down, the market is trading growth, and the bull path is in play. If the 3-month falls and the 10-year holds or rises, the market is trading inflation and supply, and the bear path is in play.

A second check sits underneath the first: whether moves in the 10-year come from real yields or from breakeven inflation. Breakevens rising with oil point to the bear route. Real yields rising on their own point to term premium and issuance. The same real-yield logic is already showing up in precious metals, as the gold and silver note describes.

What comes next

The next employment report and the next consumer price reading are the data points that decide it. A weak print with a sticky long end would be the clearest sign that the 96 basis points reflects supply and inflation risk rather than a growth cushion. A weak print with falling long yields would confirm easing without a recession premium. Until one of them lands, the front end can say what it likes. The long end is setting the terms.
