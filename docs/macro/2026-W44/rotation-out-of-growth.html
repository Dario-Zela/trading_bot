EDITORIAL · WEEKLY THESIS · By The Editor · 2026-11-01 · 5 min read

The market is rotating out of growth and into things that pay

In one sentence
Tech and communications are down 10% in a month while health care, staples, utilities and energy lead, and a 4.93% 10-year is doing the work.

The market's message this week was not that something broke. It was that something got repriced. Technology and communication services, the two sectors that have carried American equities for most of a decade, lost roughly a tenth of their value over twenty trading days. In the same window, health care, consumer staples, utilities and energy each gained between 9% and 11%. No alarm went off in credit, the dollar barely moved, and the headline indexes stayed comparatively calm. That is what a rotation looks like, as opposed to a panic.

The argument here is that the 10-year Treasury yield, at 4.93%, is doing most of the work, and that capital is leaving long-duration growth for assets that pay now.

The tape

The numbers are lopsided. The technology sector fund XLK fell 2.03% over five days and 10.16% over twenty. The communication services fund XLC dropped 5.32% over five days and 9.45% over twenty. On the other side, XLV (health care) rose 11.25% over twenty days, XLP (staples) 11.04% and XLE (energy) 11.46%. Utilities, by the desk's count, are up by a similar margin.

A spread of roughly twenty percentage points between the weakest and strongest sectors in a month is wide. It is also orderly. Selling was concentrated in the sectors whose value rests on earnings far in the future, and buying went to sectors whose value rests on dividends, regulated returns, commodity cash flows and steady demand. A fear trade would look different, with indiscriminate selling and a scramble for the safest assets. This one has a clear sorting rule.

Credit supports that reading. High-yield bonds, via HYG, returned 0.49% over five days, ahead of the 0.40% from investment-grade LQD. When lenders worry about the economy, the riskier bonds lag. Here they led. The dollar index sits at 101.9, essentially flat. Neither the bond market nor the currency market is describing distress.

The yield is the explanation

The 10-year yield is near 4.93% and the 30-year is above 5.3%. The gap between the 3-month bill and the 10-year is about 96 basis points, a steep curve in which the long end is demanding more compensation than the front end.

The mechanics are the old ones. The present value of a company's earnings falls as the discount rate rises, and it falls most for earnings that sit furthest away. A growth company expected to earn its money in the 2030s is, in effect, a long-dated bond with a variable coupon. When the risk-free yield on a 30-year bond clears 5.3%, that comparison becomes uncomfortable. A utility or a staples maker, with a cash dividend arriving every quarter, has shorter effective duration and a visible income stream. At these yields, owning it looks more defensible than it did at 4%.

The pattern in precious metals points the same way. Gold and silver have fallen while yields have risen. In a genuine flight to safety, both would be expected to hold or climb. Instead they are behaving as assets with no yield competing against Treasuries that now pay a lot. That is the signature of rising real rates, not of fear. The tape is consistent: higher real yields pressure long-duration equities and non-yielding metals alike, and favour assets with cash flows.

Energy fits a slightly different logic. Its strength reflects a rise in oil of about 15% over the month, which makes it a real-asset and inflation-sensitive holding as much as a defensive one. That makes the rotation a mixed one: part defensive, part real-asset. Both halves share the same attribute, which is that they pay for waiting.

What the rotation does not say

It is worth being careful about what the evidence supports. It does not show that the economy is weakening, since credit would be the first place to say so and credit is quiet. It does not show a loss of confidence in the dollar or the Treasury market, since the dollar is flat and the move in yields is a repricing of term premium and policy, not a run. It does not show that technology earnings have deteriorated. The sell-off is a valuation story, and valuation stories can reverse as quickly as they arrive if the discount rate moves.

There is also a risk the rotation itself has not absorbed. Leadership has moved to defensives, but a long end that keeps rising would eventually pressure those sectors as well. Utilities in particular borrow heavily and compete directly with bond yields for income-seeking money. A 5% long end is a tail risk that equity leadership, new or old, has not fully priced.

What would prove this wrong

A thesis is only useful if it can fail. This one fails on either of two conditions within the next two weeks. If XLK recovers its 20-day losses, the sell-off was a pause in an intact trend and not a repricing. If the 10-year yield falls below 4.6%, the pressure on duration has been relieved and the central cause is gone. Either result would send the desk back to the drawing board.

Until then, the weight of evidence favours the plain reading. Yields are high enough to compete with equities for the first time in years, and money is moving toward what pays. The question is no longer whether the market is frightened. It is whether the 10-year stops near 4.93% or keeps going, and what the policy path implies for the front of a curve that is already steep.
