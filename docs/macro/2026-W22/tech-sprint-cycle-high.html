Tech's 20-day sprint is among the fastest sector runs of the cycle

Technology sector performance has diverged sharply from the rest of the market over the past 20 trading days — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
XLK's 19.76% gain over 20 days, while 8 of 11 sectors are negative, means tech isn't leading a broad rally — it is the rally, and the question is whether earnings can keep justifying the price.

Something unusual happened to the technology sector over the past four weeks. XLK, the Technology Select Sector SPDR ETF that tracks the S&P 500's tech cohort, gained 19.76% in twenty trading days. Over the same window, Health Care — the next-best performer — returned 2.38%. The gap between first and second place across eleven sectors is 17.4 percentage points. Eight of those eleven sectors are underwater over the period. This is not a broad market rally with technology in front. It is a technology rally with everything else largely watching.

The move has no shortage of explanations. The harder question is which one does the most work: genuine earnings re-rating, multiple expansion on AI optimism, short-covering into a momentum squeeze, or some combination of all three. The answer shapes how durable this is.

Three beats and a capex arms race

The sprint was lit partly by a concentrated burst of earnings results in late April and May that came in ahead of expectations across the three companies that together account for roughly 39% of XLK's net assets.

Microsoft reported its fiscal Q3 results on April 29. Revenue reached $82.9 billion, up 18% year-on-year, beating consensus by roughly $1.5 billion. Azure cloud growth came in at 40%, clearing the 39% pencilled in by analysts. The figure that turned heads was the AI revenue run rate: Microsoft disclosed it had crossed $37 billion on an annualised basis, up 123% year-on-year. The company simultaneously announced a $190 billion capital expenditure programme for calendar 2026, a figure so large it functions less as guidance and more as a statement of intent about the AI infrastructure buildout.

Apple's fiscal Q2 results, also on April 30, added to the momentum. Revenue was $111.2 billion, up 17%, with iPhone sales jumping 22% to a record $57.99 billion for a March quarter. Services revenue came in at $30.98 billion, slightly above consensus.

Then, on May 20, Nvidia delivered its first-quarter fiscal 2027 numbers. Revenue was $81.6 billion — up 85% year-on-year and well above the $78.8 billion Wall Street had expected. Adjusted earnings per share of $1.87 beat the $1.76 consensus. CEO Jensen Huang stated that demand for Blackwell GPUs was effectively constrained by supply, not buyers. Guidance for the current quarter pointed to approximately $91 billion in revenue, above analyst estimates.

The immediate market reaction to Nvidia was, oddly, a modest decline in the stock — a classic buy-the-rumour dynamic after shares had already gained 13.7% since February's results. But the broader read for the sector was constructive: AI infrastructure spending is accelerating rather than stalling, and the hyperscalers are treating it as durable capex rather than a one-cycle budget item.

Earnings lift or sentiment surge?

The distinction that matters here is whether the 20-day move represents earnings-estimate revision or multiple expansion. A re-rating on fundamentals — analysts raising numbers to reflect better results and stronger guidance — is a different animal from investors simply paying more for the same cash flows because confidence has returned.

The evidence points to some of both, with the fundamental leg arguably more substantial than it looks. Consensus earnings estimates for XLK members are forecast up 43% this year versus last, with another 24% projected in 2027. Those are not trivial revisions; they reflect genuine outperformance from the AI infrastructure cycle compressing into a small number of companies. The forward price-earnings ratio for the S&P 500 Information Technology sector sits at roughly 24.4 times as of late May — elevated by historical standards, but not dramatically so given the earnings growth trajectory. Nvidia's own forward PE has actually compressed as profits have grown faster than the share price.

That said, the pace of the move — nearly 20% in four weeks — is consistent with momentum and short-covering as amplifying forces. Tech entered the period with crowded short books from the broader de-risking that preceded it, and the earnings beats squeezed that positioning. The speed of recovery is characteristic of forced covering rather than purely deliberate accumulation.


A tech-driven market — but one with a narrow base

A rising index on a narrow floor

The breadth picture complicates the bullish read. Goldman Sachs analyst Ben Snider flagged in May that market breadth has reached one of its narrowest levels in decades outside the dot-com bubble. The bank's preferred measure — the distance the S&P 500 index sits from its 52-week high versus the same gauge for the median constituent — shows the index near recent peaks while the median stock remains roughly 13% below its own 52-week high.

Across the eleven GICS sectors, the dispersion is historically extreme. Utilities lost 5.19% over the 20-day window. Financials fell 1.06%. Energy and several consumer-facing sectors are also underwater. The top 10 S&P 500 companies now account for approximately 40% of the index by market capitalisation — a concentration level that has not been seen since the peak of the dot-com era. With AI expected to generate roughly 40% of total S&P 500 earnings growth in 2026, the index is, in effect, a leveraged bet on the monetisation of one technology cycle by a dozen companies.

That dynamic creates a risk profile that diverges sharply from index-level statistics. An investor in the equal-weight S&P 500 ETF saw the index decline in April even as the cap-weight version marched to highs. The technology sector's gains look like a rising tide only if the portfolio is weighted the same way the index is.

The question for the next phase is not whether the earnings were strong — they were — but whether the estimates embedded in current prices require perfect delivery to justify. Nvidia's Q2 guidance of approximately $91 billion in revenue is now the number to watch. If the company hits or beats when it reports in August, the earnings-upgrade thesis stays intact. If supply constraints or demand softness produce a miss, the multiple expansion leg of this rally becomes considerably more exposed. The earnings cycle that powered the sprint is also the one that could end it.
