SECTORS · ENERGY · By I. Aresti · 2026-05-31 · 6 min read

Energy's double-digit loss forces the demand-destruction question

A pumpjack working an oil field — the machinery at the centre of a sector caught between ceasefire euphoria and inventory stress warnings. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
XLE's 5.63% 20-day loss tracks oil's collapse almost point-for-point — the debate is whether a deflating war premium or real demand destruction is doing the work.

The energy sector has handed investors its worst two-week stretch in the S&P 500, and the question it raises goes beyond whether to buy the dip. It is whether oil is telling the truth about the global economy.

XLE, the Energy Select Sector SPDR Fund, fell 4.80% in the past five trading sessions and is now down 5.63% over twenty days — the worst-performing sector on both timeframes. Crude oil, tracked by USO, did considerably worse: off 9.44% this week and 12.24% over the same rolling period. The correlation is tight. The message is anything but clear.

XLE is down 5.63% over 20 days as oil craters — equity markets must now decide between supply-glut benignity and an honest demand signal.

The deflating war premium

The immediate cause of oil's retreat has little to do with collapsing drilling programs or softening refinery runs. Earlier in 2026, American and Israeli military operations against Iran effectively closed the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow passage through which roughly one-fifth of the world's seaborne crude flows. Oil prices surged on the supply shock; XLE, ExxonMobil, and Chevron posted extraordinary year-to-date returns as the market scrambled to price scarcity. Both majors were up more than 37% through April.

What reversed the rally was diplomacy — or at least its possibility. Reports that Iran received an informal draft of a US-Iranian ceasefire understanding punctured the war premium with unusual speed. WTI crude fell more than 16% across May. Energy equity investors took the news more calmly than the commodity itself, and that differential matters: XLE is down roughly half as much as USO in both the five- and twenty-day windows. Equity markets are not fully crediting the decline as permanent.

The executives running these companies are explicit about why. At Bernstein's annual conference this week, Exxon Senior Vice President Neil Chapman described the inventory situation in terms that do not usually appear in polished investor presentations. "We're approaching unheard of inventory levels — I mean really, really low levels," Chapman said, predicting that prices would shoot higher once global storage hits operational minimums. Chevron CEO Mike Wirth concurred: "Over the next few weeks, we're likely to see those pressures flow through more directly to physical prices, and there's more upward pressure that I would expect as we get into June and certainly into July." Goldman Sachs estimates the world is drawing down global stockpiles at a record 8.7 million barrels per day. Cushing, Oklahoma — the WTI pricing hub — has seen inventories fall from 33 million to 24.5 million barrels, uncomfortably close to the 20 million threshold at which physical markets begin to seize.

If that reading is right, XLE's decline is a ceasefire trade on thin physical fundamentals — a premium deflating, not a structural break.

The demand data complicates the story

That would be a cleaner narrative if the IEA were telling the same story. The agency's May oil market report projected global demand to contract by 420 thousand barrels per day year-on-year in 2026, with the deepest hole in the second quarter — a 2.4 mb/d decline versus pre-war forecasts. Petrochemicals and aviation were cited as most affected, but the report noted that higher prices, a weakening economic backdrop, and demand-saving measures were already spreading into broader fuel use.

Macro data offers a consistent undertone. The J.P. Morgan Global Manufacturing PMI fell from 48.8 in April to 47.5 in May, its lowest reading in 31 months. Manufacturing below 50 signals contraction. The Middle East conflict disrupted supply chains, raised freight costs, and compressed industrial margins across the global economy. Energy is not the only sector absorbing that slowdown.

The supply-side bulls counter that the IEA's demand downgrades were constructed against the backdrop of sustained high prices — prices that have since cratered. If oil stabilises at lower levels and Hormuz traffic resumes, demand should recover as the shock absorbers rebuild. Whether that rebound arrives in Q3 or much later is precisely what the market is pricing.

What the rest of the complex is saying

The commodity complex is not speaking with one voice, which is what makes the energy sell-off so difficult to read as a clean demand signal.

Natural gas, tracked by UNG, is up 5.30% this week and 12.55% over twenty days — sharply diverging from oil within the same energy complex. The reasons are supply-specific rather than demand-driven: a drone strike on Qatar's Ras Laffan liquefaction complex removed a significant slice of global LNG export capacity, while US LNG terminals are running near maximum utilisation as European and Asian buyers compete for alternatives. The EIA reported a smaller-than-expected storage build in the week ended May 22. None of this reads as demand destruction; it reads as a supply crunch in a different molecule.

Base metals are also resisting the bearish thesis. The Invesco DB Base Metals Fund, which tracks copper, zinc, and aluminium, is up 4.46% over twenty days and roughly 11% year-to-date. Copper has historically led industrial cycles, and its refusal to confirm oil's downturn is notable. If the global factory floor were shutting down in the way that would make oil's fall genuinely ominous, copper would normally be leading the retreat — not advancing.

Neither signal fully exonerates oil. UNG's rally is geopolitically driven; if Hormuz reopens and LNG flows normalise, the gas premium deflates on its own timetable. DBB's strength may reflect targeted Chinese infrastructure demand rather than a broad global recovery. But together they make a purely demand-destructive reading of crude's decline harder to sustain.

What June 7 will reveal

OPEC+ ministers convene on June 7 to set July production quotas. Several leading producers are expected to agree to a modest output increase — potentially 188 thousand barrels per day — into a market already dislocated by the ceasefire pricing. How that decision lands depends heavily on whether the US-Iran understanding progresses from draft to ratification. If the deal falls apart and Hormuz closes again, any incremental OPEC barrel becomes arithmetically irrelevant against the supply shock. If the deal holds and Gulf flows resume from the third quarter onward — the IEA's working assumption — the market faces a more complex problem: supply recovering faster than demand.

XLE is currently priced between those poles. The wide gap between USO's 12.24% twenty-day loss and XLE's 5.63% suggests a market that believes the inventory tightness Chapman described will eventually reassert itself — that the commodity's signal is noise, not trend. That bet pays off if inventories hit operational stress and prices spike in June. It does not pay off if the ceasefire holds, OPEC hikes, and the IEA's demand downgrades prove more durable than the sector has priced.

The June 7 ministerial decision is the first hard test. Either the supply narrative retakes control, or the demand-destruction argument gains the credibility the commodity market is already implying.
