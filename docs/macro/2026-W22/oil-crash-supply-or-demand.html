The oil crash: benign supply story or honest demand warning?

The Strait of Hormuz, the chokepoint whose effective closure since February 28 sent oil to $138 a barrel before ceasefire hopes reversed the move — NASA MODIS / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Oil's 17% monthly crash is the market pricing in a Hormuz reopening — but the IEA's demand forecasts warn that supply returning won't undo the economic damage already done.

Oil's worst month since the Covid pandemic is over. What's left is a harder question: did prices fall because supply is coming back, or because the global economy quietly stopped wanting so much oil in the first place?

The numbers are stark. West Texas Intermediate closed May at $87.36 a barrel, down 16% on the month and roughly 37% below the cycle peak of $138 it touched on April 7, when traders were still pricing in a years-long closure of the Strait of Hormuz. The United States Oil Fund — the most-watched proxy for broad crude exposure — has shed 9.44% over the past five sessions and 12.24% over twenty, magnitudes that historically force a macro reckoning rather than a shoulder-shrug.

The backdrop is the war with Iran. When US-Israeli airstrikes began on February 28, Iran's Revolutionary Guard effectively shut the Strait of Hormuz — the narrow passage through which roughly 15.8 million barrels per day normally flow, representing nearly a fifth of global seaborne oil supply. The IEA called it the largest supply disruption in the history of the world oil market. Brent crude recorded its steepest-ever monthly rise in March, up roughly 65% to reach that April peak.

What has happened since is equally dramatic in the other direction. Reports in late May that Washington and Tehran have "mostly agreed" on a 60-day memorandum of understanding to extend an existing ceasefire — and critically, ease restrictions on Hormuz transit — sent prices into retreat. The deal has not been signed. President Trump has not approved it. Iranian state media said it is not finalised. That hasn't stopped traders from pricing the reopening as if it were a matter of weeks, not months.

The supply story, and why equities believe it

The benign reading of the price decline is straightforward: what went up on supply fear is coming down on supply hope. If the strait reopens, some 10-11 million barrels per day of stranded Gulf production re-enters the market, prices normalise, and the inflation shock that has been hammering Asian importers and Middle Eastern fiscal accounts begins to unwind. On this reading, cheaper oil is a tax cut for every economy that runs on it — most of them.

Equity markets are voting heavily for this interpretation. The S&P 500 has recorded nine straight weeks of gains, a streak not seen since 2023, and is up roughly 20% from its March lows. Technology stocks — measured by XLK — have put in one of the fastest 20-day sector runs of the current cycle, gaining 19.76%. Consumer discretionary is also higher. The growth-sensitive corners of the market are, implicitly, reading cheaper oil as good news.

OPEC+ has added a measure of its own nuance. In May, seven cartel members agreed to lift output by 188,000 barrels per day from June — a number described by Al Jazeera as "symbolic" given the scale of the Hormuz disruption — but its message was notable: the group is not circling the wagons to defend a price floor. With Gulf producers already losing an estimated $1.1 billion per day in oil revenues while the strait remains closed, the political incentive to get ships moving again is overwhelming.

The demand reading is harder to dismiss

Set against this, there is a less comfortable set of facts.

The IEA's May Oil Market Report forecast global oil demand contracting 420,000 barrels per day year-on-year across 2026, with the second quarter alone showing a demand decline of 2.45 million barrels per day — the sharpest since the pandemic. The petrochemical and aviation sectors are most directly hit. But the IEA specifically warned that demand destruction tends to compound when energy scarcity persists: higher prices, weaker economic conditions, and conservation measures feed on each other.

The IMF reduced its global growth forecast to 3.1% for 2026 and lifted headline inflation to 4.4%, both moves attributable in significant part to the energy shock. Economists at several Asian central banks have noted what history suggests — that a major oil disruption has been followed, without exception, by a recession in at least one major economy. The question is whether the ceasefire, if it holds, cuts the cycle short before that dynamic takes hold.

The demand interpretation of the oil decline runs like this: much of the Q2 contraction in oil consumption reflects real, structural damage to manufacturing activity, supply chains, and consumer confidence that does not simply reverse when the price of crude falls. Airlines have cut routes. Factories in India, Singapore, and South Korea have rationed energy. Container shipping has been rerouted around the Persian Gulf at significant cost. Some of that activity does not snap back.

What the cross-assets say — and what they don't

The most instructive puzzle is what is not falling alongside oil.

Base metals, tracked by DBB, are up 4.46% over the past twenty sessions. Copper futures have held above $6.25 a pound even as oil has broken down. That divergence matters: copper is a reliable barometer of industrial activity, and its resilience argues against a simple global demand-destruction story. If manufacturing were genuinely rolling over, copper would be leading oil lower, not lagging it.

Natural gas, proxied by UNG, has risen 12.55% over the same twenty-day window. That move is harder to read cleanly — it may reflect gas-to-oil substitution as buyers scramble for alternatives to Hormuz-stranded crude, or it may reflect separate LNG dynamics in a world where Asian buyers are diversifying energy supply with unusual speed. Either way, it complicates a straightforward demand-collapse thesis.

Gold, historically a barometer of fear and inflation anxiety, is essentially flat. That is an interesting non-signal: gold hasn't spiked on recession fears, nor has it collapsed on "crisis resolved" optimism. The precious metal appears to be in a wait-and-see posture that the equity market has clearly not adopted.

The risk desk's read: the cross-asset picture is consistent with a supply-driven price decline in crude, not a demand-driven one — for now. The manufacturing PMI surprised to the upside in May at 55.3, a three-year high, though much of that order strength was driven by precautionary stockpiling ahead of expected supply normalisation, not organic end-demand. Stockpiling and genuine demand growth look identical in the PMI until they don't.

What to watch

The supply narrative needs the ceasefire to hold and the Hormuz transit numbers to actually recover. Trump's sign-off and Iran's formal agreement are both still outstanding — a single hardliner move in Tehran or Washington could push oil back through $100 in hours. The strait carries not just crude but LNG, petrochemicals, and refined products, and the logistics of restarting 2,000 stranded ships are not trivial.

The demand narrative needs corroboration from the June PMI releases and the updated global freight cost indices, due in the first week of the month. If Asian manufacturing prints confirm the weakness that the IEA's demand revisions implied, the supply story loses its floor quickly — because falling prices no longer signal a normalising supply shock but an economy that has taken genuine damage. At that point, the equity market's nine-week rally faces a different conversation.

Energy HY issuers, who have already been warned that oil at these levels creates a fresh refinancing test, are unlikely to wait for the June PMI to make their case to creditors. For them, the supply narrative isn't academic — it is the difference between rolling a credit facility and restructuring one.
