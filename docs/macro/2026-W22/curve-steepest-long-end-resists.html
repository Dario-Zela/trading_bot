RATES · YIELD CURVE · By G. Mehlman · 2026-05-31 · 5 min read

Steepest normal curve of the cycle — but the long end is the real story

The US Treasury Building, Washington, D.C. — the institution managing nearly $10 trillion in borrowing over the next twelve months — AgnosticPreachersKid / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The 3M-10Y spread hit +86.5bps as the front end prices Fed cuts, but a 30-year yield anchored near 5% signals rising term premium that rate optimists are underweighting.

The US yield curve, after nearly two years of inversion, has not merely normalised — it has steepened into territory that markets have not seen at this stage of a cycle. The 3-month bill yields 3.59%, the 10-year note 4.45%, the 30-year bond sits just under 5.0%. At +86.5 basis points on the 3M-10Y spread, the curve is telling a clear story. The question is which part to believe.

The front end's dovish thesis

The 3-month yield anchoring at 3.59% reflects a market that believes the Federal Reserve has meaningfully more cuts to deliver. The fed funds target sits at 3.50–3.75%, and futures pricing embeds multiple additional 25-basis-point reductions over the next twelve months. That bet received fresh support this week as oil collapsed: Brent crude shed nearly 19% in May — its worst calendar month since March 2020 — as US-Iran ceasefire talks raised expectations of Strait of Hormuz normalisation and eased supply-chain pressure.

Lower energy is unambiguously disinflationary for goods costs, and it makes it harder for the Fed to hold. The central bank's own March projections still flagged one or two cuts in 2026; the front end has simply run ahead of that, pricing normalisation toward something closer to 3.0% by mid-2027. The internal logic is coherent: if oil is cheap on supply rather than demand, the growth backdrop holds, inflation pressures ease further, and the Fed can act. That is the soft-landing corollary fully embedded in the bill complex.

What the long end is pricing instead

The 30-year bond peaked at 5.19% on May 19 — its highest level in 19 years — before settling back to just under 5.0%. Over the same stretch that oil was collapsing and the 3-month bill was rallying, the 30-year barely moved. The 5Y-30Y spread stands at 84.4 basis points, meaningfully wider than the already-wide 3M-10Y spread. The steepest part of the curve is the ultra-long end, not the belly — a geometry that does not resemble classic rate-expectation repricing.

If this were about rate expectations, the 30-year would have followed the 10-year lower as disinflationary signals accumulated. It has not. What it resembles instead is a term premium regime shift: investors demanding structural compensation for holding duration deep into a fiscal cycle they distrust.

The evidence is accumulating on several fronts. In mid-May, Moody's stripped the US of its last top-tier sovereign rating, cutting from Aaa to Aa1 — citing a budget deficit expected to approach 9% of GDP. The 30-year briefly crossed 5.0% on the announcement and has since returned there anyway, which is either confirmation of the signal or evidence that the market had already priced it. Either interpretation is uncomfortable for rate optimists.

The fiscal arithmetic is blunt. The federal government needs to borrow approximately $10 trillion over the next twelve months — roughly $7.5 trillion refinancing maturing debt plus $2 trillion covering the annual deficit. The "One Big Beautiful Bill," which passed the House and is before the Senate, will add an estimated $4.7 trillion to the national debt through 2034 on a dynamic score, according to the Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget. Against that supply backdrop, the New York Fed's Adrian-Crump-Moench term premium model turned positive this year for the first time since 2023. That shift is not a sentiment blip. It reflects the rational calculation that issuance volumes, deficit trajectories, and sovereign credit uncertainty together set a floor under long-end yields that rate-cut bets cannot dislodge.

The divergence as a diagnostic

The resulting curve shape is a diagnostic, not a forecast. Bull steepening — the benign variety that historically precedes recoveries — typically shows up when the long end rallies alongside the front end. This cycle, the front end is moving lower while the long end is largely stationary. That is a different configuration entirely: the front end trusts the Fed, the long end trusts the calendar.

The market is implicitly pricing a world in which Fed cuts are real but insufficient to produce the long-end rally that would complete the soft-landing narrative. The 5.0% handle on the 30-year is not just a psychological level; it is the market's estimate of a floor built from fiscal supply, not monetary policy. At current CBO projections, even the baseline deficit path — before the reconciliation bill — keeps net new Treasury issuance well above pre-pandemic run rates through the decade.

Three data points will test whether that floor holds. The Senate is still amending the reconciliation bill; any material scaling back of the fiscal package would remove a headline supply risk from the long end and could trigger a modest rally in the 30-year. The Treasury's August refunding announcement will show how aggressively the department leans on ultra-long issuance; a shift back toward bills reduces duration pressure. And the June CPI print, landing July 15, will determine whether oil's disinflation has penetrated services — the one component the Fed has consistently flagged as sticky and the variable most capable of accelerating the front end's cuts-pricing into something the long end has to acknowledge.
