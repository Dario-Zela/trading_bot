RISK · DURATION · By S. Vance · 2026-09-27 · 5 min read

Someone is sitting on the mark-to-market losses from this move

The former Silicon Valley Bank headquarters entrance in Santa Clara, California — the 2023 failure that showed how unmarked duration losses become forced-sale losses. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A 30-year yield at 5.53% and a 1.42% weekly drop in LQD are repricing duration everywhere at once — the risk is that no forced seller has shown up in the data yet.

The number that matters this week is not the yield itself. It is the question of who is holding the bonds that yield now prices against.

The 30-year Treasury climbed to 5.53% on Thursday, its highest level since 2004, capping what Bloomberg called a "bruising week" for long-dated debt — the worst stretch for the 30-year bond since 2006, by some measures, after a run that has taken it from below 5% in early July to a two-decade high in under three months. The 10-year followed, breaking above 5.22%. Investment-grade corporate bonds, which carry duration risk of their own on top of credit spread, moved with it: LQD, the largest investment-grade corporate bond ETF, is down 1.42% for the week and now sits at a year-to-date loss of roughly 3.5%.

A bond that falls 1.4% in a week is not, on its own, a crisis. Bond prices move. What makes this particular move worth pausing on is where losses like it end up sitting once they're marked — and where they sit when they aren't marked at all.

The accounting trick that isn't a trick

Banks, insurers and pension funds hold enormous quantities of long-duration debt, and under US accounting rules, a meaningful share of it never has to be marked to market. Securities classified as "held-to-maturity" sit on the balance sheet at cost, with unrealized losses disclosed in a footnote rather than run through earnings or capital ratios — right up until the institution is forced to sell.

That footnote has gotten large. FDIC data for the second quarter of 2026 show unrealized losses on held-to-maturity securities at US banks totaling $216.9 billion, or 10.5% of amortized cost — up from $214.5 billion in the first quarter as long-end yields kept climbing. Add available-for-sale securities and total unrealized losses across the banking system reach $326.7 billion. Those numbers have actually improved from their 2023 peak, when the Federal Reserve's rate-hiking cycle first exposed the mismatch. But they were also improving steadily until this year's climb in long yields interrupted the recovery — and the second-quarter figures predate the past month's fresh leg higher, which pushed the 30-year through levels not seen since George W. Bush's first term.

Two precedents, one mechanism

The mechanism connecting a duration repricing to an actual financial event has played out twice in recent memory, and both episodes are worth having in view now, precisely because neither looked dangerous until it suddenly was.

In September 2022, the UK's "mini-budget" sent gilt yields up sharply in a matter of days. Defined-benefit pension schemes running leveraged liability-driven investment strategies — designed to hedge long-dated liabilities using gilts and derivatives — faced margin and collateral calls that the Bank of England later estimated at more than £70 billion in aggregate. Funds began selling gilts to raise cash, pushing yields higher still and forcing more selling. The Bank of England intervened directly, buying £19.3 billion of long-dated gilts over 13 days to arrest what it later said had brought a number of pension schemes to within hours of collapse. The proximate cause was a budget; the transmission mechanism was duration and leverage.

Silicon Valley Bank's failure in March 2023 ran the same mechanism through a different institution. SVB had built up a long-dated securities book — average asset duration of roughly six years — funded by deposits that could leave overnight. As the Fed raised rates through 2022, the bank's held-to-maturity portfolio racked up an unrealized loss north of $15 billion, and its available-for-sale book was underwater too. Neither loss touched the income statement until SVB was forced to actually sell $21 billion of available-for-sale securities to meet deposit outflows, crystallizing a $1.8 billion realized loss that triggered a capital raise, a bank run and, within 48 hours, a Friday-morning seizure by regulators. Again: a Fed hiking cycle was the proximate cause, but a mismatched, unmarked duration book was what turned rate moves into an insolvency event.

Where the exposure sits now

The useful exercise is not predicting a repeat of either episode — it's asking which of today's long-duration holders resemble the vulnerable party from each one, and which don't.

US corporate defined-benefit pensions look less exposed than their UK counterparts did in 2022. Average funded ratios have climbed to roughly 108%, up from about 87% in 2018, and the growth in leveraged LDI allocations has slowed since 2019 as plans that are now overfunded have less incentive to run leveraged hedges. That is a genuine structural difference from the UK book that blew up.

Regional banks and life insurers look more analogous to the 2023 setup, at least in shape. Bond-heavy insurers — MetLife, Brighthouse Financial, CNO Financial Group among them — carry portfolios where a jump in the risk-free rate cuts both ways: better reinvestment yields on new premiums, but further mark-to-market pressure on legacy holdings and, for some, statutory capital strain. On the bank side, analysts have flagged that losses concentrated in mortgage-backed securities with long effective duration and negative convexity respond less to any eventual rate relief than plain Treasuries would, meaning the drag persists even if yields plateau.

What's missing from this week's data, notably, is any sign of forced selling. No fund has announced a margin call; no bank has announced a deposit run; no insurer has announced a downgrade tied to investment losses. That absence is worth taking seriously rather than dismissing — both 2022 and 2023 had weeks that looked exactly like this one, where the number moving was a yield rather than a bank's stock price, before liquidity pressure turned an accounting footnote into a headline.

What would change the picture

s
The channel to watch is deposit or redemption behavior, not the yield print itself: a regional bank reporting deposit outflows, an insurer facing a ratings-agency capital review, or a pension scheme disclosing a collateral call would be the first concrete sign that mark-to-market losses have started to force actual transactions rather than sit quietly on a balance sheet. The next FDIC Quarterly Banking Profile, covering the third quarter and due in early December, will be the first official read on how far this month's move in the 30-year has pushed the industry's unrealized-loss figure — and it will capture the period in which the 30-year actually crossed 5.5%, something the second-quarter data did not.
