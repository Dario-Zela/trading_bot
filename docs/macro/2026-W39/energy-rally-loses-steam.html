Energy's month-long rally is losing steam

A pumpjack in the Permian Basin near Midland, Texas. — Eric Kounce (TexasRaiser) / Wikimedia Commons, public domain
In one sentence
Crude's 14% monthly rally reversed 3.6% this week on Iran-US truce talk, and energy stocks fell even harder than oil while natural gas kept climbing.

Crude oil spent most of September doing what it does when a war disrupts a shipping lane: it went up, steadily and a lot. The United States Oil Fund (USO) gained more than 14% over the past month as the naval blockade around the Strait of Hormuz choked off Iranian exports and traders priced in a genuine risk to Gulf supply. This week, that trade started coming apart. USO fell 3.6% over five sessions, WTI crude gave back nearly $8 a barrel, and energy stocks — which are supposed to move with the commodity they're built on — fell even harder.

The Energy Select Sector SPDR (XLE) dropped 3.53% over the same five days, the weakest five-day showing of any of the eleven S&P sectors this week. That's the detail worth sitting with. Oil pulled back on real news — a senior Iranian official told Reuters that Tehran was weighing a phased reopening of Hormuz in exchange for the US lifting its naval blockade, and CNBC reported oil prices pulling off their highs on Thursday as those talks emerged. Energy stocks, meanwhile, fell by more than the commodity that drives their earnings. That gap is the story.

A rally built on a blockade

The run-up made sense on its own terms. Since July, when a ceasefire brokered in June collapsed and fighting resumed, the US has maintained a naval blockade that has kept new Iranian crude from reaching the market, forcing Tehran to draw down floating stockpiles built up before the blockade was reinstated. Brent has held above $100 a barrel through most of that stretch, and WTI's climb toward the low-$90s over the past month tracked the growing sense that a wider Gulf conflict, and a wider supply shock, wasn't going away.

That's precisely the kind of move — fast, geopolitical, one-directional — that invites profit-taking the moment the catalyst wobbles. WTI's 7.9% single-week drop, its first weekly decline since late August, came directly off reports that Washington and Tehran might be inching toward a deal to reopen the strait. Nothing has been signed. But options-adjusted risk premium built on blockade fear is exactly the kind of premium that evaporates on rumor alone, before any barrel actually moves.

Why energy stocks fell further than oil

A sector ETF underperforming its own commodity on the way down is a specific and useful signal. XLE's holdings aren't a pure play on the spot price — refiners like Valero and Marathon earn off the crack spread, and midstream names like Williams and Kinder Morgan collect fee-based income tied to throughput rather than price. In theory, that mix should cushion a pullback, not amplify it.

That it didn't points to positioning rather than fundamentals. Energy had been the best-performing S&P sector for much of 2026, pulled up by the same blockade premium sitting in the crude price, and investors who piled into producers and services names on the way up appear to be exiting faster than the commodity itself is falling — trimming winners, not reassessing the war. Whether that's simple portfolio rebalancing after a strong month or the early edge of a broader re-rating is the open question the next few sessions should start to answer.

Gas isn't listening

While oil and energy equities gave back ground, natural gas kept climbing — the United States Natural Gas Fund (UNG) is up 6.92% over five days and 6.71% over the month, a divergence that undercuts any simple "energy complex rolling over" reading. Gas has its own drivers: a force majeure declaration on TC Energy's Columbia Gas Transmission pipeline in Appalachia triggered the biggest single-day price jump since January, forecasts pointed to one of the hottest Septembers on record, and LNG feedgas demand to major export terminals rose to roughly 17.8 billion cubic feet a day from 17.2 bcfd in August. None of that has anything to do with Hormuz.

That split matters for how to read this week. If oil and energy stocks were falling because global demand was weakening, gas would likely be falling too — it isn't. The more consistent explanation is that crude's move was a geopolitical-risk trade unwinding on truce headlines, not a signal about growth or energy demand broadly.

The next test is whether Iran and the US actually produce something concrete on Hormuz. A confirmed phased reopening would justify more of this week's selloff; a return to stalemate, or renewed attacks on shipping, would put the blockade premium — and energy's month-long lead — right back in play.
