A stronger dollar and cheaper gold squeeze emerging markets again

A stronger dollar raises the real cost of emerging-market debt denominated in it. — Milad Mosapoor / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Twenty days of dollar strength and a near-7% drop in gold and silver are squeezing emerging markets unevenly, with oil's own pullback splitting importers from exporters.

The dollar has spent twenty straight sessions doing what it always does when it firms: making life harder for anyone who owes money in it and doesn't earn in it. The DXY is up 1.83% over that stretch, gold has fallen 6.91% and silver 7.38% — and emerging markets, which sit exposed on both fronts at once, are absorbing the squeeze unevenly depending on which side of the commodity ledger they're on.

The mechanism is old and reliable. A stronger dollar raises the local-currency cost of servicing dollar-denominated sovereign and corporate debt across the emerging world, from Brazilian eurobonds to Turkish bank loans. It was on full display last week: MSCI's local-currency EM debt index fell 0.88%, with currency losses — not rate moves — doing almost all the damage, and 18 of 20 tracked EM currencies losing ground. The Brazilian real slid to R$5.14 per dollar by September 23. Turkey, Argentina, Egypt, Indonesia and South Africa are all sitting with currency weakness in the 15-40% range this year, according to tourism-industry data tracking the knock-on effects on outbound spending — a slightly odd lens, but a real one: when a currency falls that far, ordinary households feel it before bondholders do.

Gold's retreat hits reserves and revenue at once

The metals side compounds the problem rather than offsetting it. Gold has been the preferred reserve-diversification asset for emerging-market central banks all year — the World Gold Council still pencils in roughly 60 tonnes a month of official-sector buying, and China's September plan to act as custodian for foreign sovereign gold reserves was meant to accelerate exactly that trend. But the metal itself has round-tripped hard: from a January record near $5,600 to a June low near $4,000, back up past $4,390 by early September, then down again to roughly $4,289 by September 23 as the Federal Reserve's September 16 rate decision pushed real yields higher. A stronger dollar and richer real yields raise the opportunity cost of holding a non-yielding asset — precisely the mechanism now working against both gold and silver, the latter down to around $63-64 an ounce from its own recent peak.

That drop lands hardest on countries that mine the stuff rather than merely hold it. Mexico is the world's largest silver producer, Peru is close behind, and Chile's copper output is already down 7% year-on-year on separate supply problems — so the silver slide arrives on top of an already-strained Andean mining sector, not into a neutral backdrop. For central banks holding gold as a reserve buffer, the paper loss is a balance-sheet problem; for economies that export the metal, it is a current-account problem too.

Oil cuts the other way — for some

Crude is the one leg of this story that doesn't sort neatly along the same fault lines. USO is still up 14.09% over twenty days despite a sharp Friday pullback — WTI fell 2.33% to $92.41 and Brent 2.14% to $104.32 on September 25, as profit-taking and easing Middle East supply-risk premiums took hold. That reversal was a relief for importers: Hungary and India, both net oil buyers, were among the worst-performing EM currencies just as oil hit its earlier highs, and the retreat has taken some pressure off. It was less welcome for exporters riding the rally — Petrobras shares fell 2.26% and Argentina's YPF dropped 2.82% on the pullback, both instances of dollar-denominated equities catching the downside of a move that had been paying out for months. Colombia's Ecopetrol was the exception, closing up 1.28%.

The net effect is a split rather than a shared verdict. Commodity-importing EMs get a dollar problem and metals-price relief from a mixed bag, but a gold-and-silver hit if they hold reserves; commodity-exporting EMs get some of that oil cushion fading just as their gold and silver export revenues shrink, on top of the same dollar-debt burden everyone shares. Argentina, Peru and South Africa arguably sit worst-positioned — debt-service costs rising, mining and metals export income falling, and in Argentina's case an oil rally now losing steam too.

The next test is whether the Fed's September 16 move marks the top of this tightening turn or merely a pause in it. Emerging-market currency traders will be watching US real yields and the DXY's next few sessions for the answer — and mining shares in Mexico and Peru for signs the silver slide has further to run.
