This is a duration selloff, not a credit selloff — for now

In one sentence
HYG is beating LQD by 57bp this week and HY spreads are near cycle tights — the tell of a rate move, not credit stress.

Junk bonds are supposed to be the canary. When credit markets get nervous — really nervous, the kind that precedes defaults — high yield sells off harder than investment grade, because a BB-rated telecom is more exposed to a downturn than a AA-rated pharma giant with a fortress balance sheet. That is not what has happened this week.

Over the past five trading days, the iShares iBoxx High Yield Corporate Bond ETF (HYG) is down 0.85%. The iShares iBoxx Investment Grade Corporate Bond ETF (LQD) — the supposedly safer instrument — is down nearly twice as much, off 1.42%. High yield is outperforming investment grade by 57 basis points. That is the opposite of a credit event. It is the signature of a duration event.

Same trigger, different exposure

The proximate cause is not mysterious. The 30-year Treasury yield topped 5.5% on September 25th, its highest level since 2004, after a stronger-than-expected consumer sentiment reading gave the long end one more reason to sell off in what Bloomberg described as a market operating in a "vacuum" of fresh information — traders extrapolating from a single data point because there was little else to trade against. That move rippled straight into corporate bonds, because both HYG and LQD are priced off the same Treasury curve. But they are not priced off the same part of that curve, and that distinction is doing all the work here.

LQD's effective duration is 7.63 years, more than double HYG's 3.18 years, according to iShares' own fund data as of September 24th. LQD also carries a much longer weighted average maturity — 12.7 years against HYG's 4.2 — because investment-grade issuers, with cheaper access to long-dated funding, load up on 20- and 30-year paper that high-yield issuers structurally can't get away with. When the long end sells off and the front end doesn't move nearly as much, a long-duration IG portfolio simply loses more principal value than a short-duration HY portfolio, independent of anything happening to either issuer's creditworthiness. This week's mathematics is not subtle: rates went up, longer bonds fell more, and LQD is a longer bond.

What the spreads say, not just the prices

The old scar tissue on this beat is simple: don't just watch bond prices, watch the spread — the extra yield investors demand over Treasuries for taking credit risk. Prices can fall in lockstep with Treasuries for entirely benign, rate-driven reasons. Spreads widening is the tell that something has actually changed about default risk.

By that measure, credit markets are about as relaxed as they have been all year. The ICE BofA US High Yield Index option-adjusted spread sits at roughly 2.80%, not far off the cycle's tightest levels — compare that to the 4.61% peak reached in April 2025, when tariff shock genuinely did blow out both investment-grade spreads (to 120bp) and high-yield spreads (to 461bp) simultaneously. That April episode is the template for what real credit stress transmission looks like: a Treasury shock that widens spreads across the board and hits HY hardest. Nothing like that is visible now. One tracker of the HYG/LQD ratio puts the current relationship at more than two standard deviations tight versus its one-year history — a "risk-on" credit reading, not a risk-off one.

Why the distinction matters

It matters because the two stories call for opposite responses. A duration selloff is a rates call — it says something about growth, inflation expectations, term premium, and fiscal supply at the long end of the curve, all of which are live issues with 30-year yields at multi-decade highs. A credit selloff says something about the corporate sector's ability to service its debt, which would be a far more urgent problem given how much leveraged credit has been issued in the tight-spread years since 2023.

Conflating the two is an easy mistake to make from the price action alone, because falling bond prices look the same on a screen whether the cause is a rate shock or a credit shock. It is also a mistake with a history: investors who read 2007's early subprime wobbles as a rates story, rather than watching spreads quietly widen underneath calm headline prices, paid for it later. The current setup argues for the opposite kind of complacency risk — reading a rates story as a credit story and getting needlessly defensive on corporate exposure that isn't actually deteriorating.

What would change the read

The falsifier is specific and worth tracking directly rather than inferring from price alone: a genuine credit deterioration would show HY option-adjusted spreads widening relative to IG, not just HY and IG bond prices falling together. So far the spread compression that has defined 2026 is intact. The next real test is less about a single data point and more about duration: if the long end keeps grinding higher into October — with 30-year yields already flirting with 2004 levels — LQD's underperformance should persist for the same mechanical reason it's happening now. Watch the spread indices, not the ETF price charts, for the moment that would actually change this story.
