Europe heads into autumn with gas prices climbing again

Domed roof of an underground LNG storage tank at the Canvey Island terminal in the UK. — Alnitak3 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Natural gas is up nearly 7% this week just as winter demand season begins, with a strengthening dollar adding to import costs.

In one sentence: Natural gas is up nearly 7% this week just as winter demand season begins, with a strengthening dollar adding to the cost of importing it.

Natural gas futures climbed to their highest level since July this week, up 6.92% over five trading days and 6.71% over the past month — the sharpest move of any commodity the desk is tracking. October contracts touched $3.35 per million British thermal units on the New York Mercantile Exchange, with the front month on pace for one of its strongest weekly gains of the year. The proximate cause is mundane: a run of unseasonably warm weather across the South-Central United States pushed cooling demand higher through late September, then a pivot toward seasonally cool forecasts added early heating load on top of it, all while a drop in daily output and rising flows to LNG export terminals tightened the balance further. The storage surplus over the five-year average narrowed from 118 billion cubic feet to 95 in a single week.

That is a weather-and-output story, and on its own it wouldn't be more than a footnote. What makes it worth watching is that it's arriving in the same week the dollar index pushed to roughly its strongest level in two months, up 1.83% over 20 days as traders price in the chance of further Federal Reserve tightening. A stronger dollar raises the effective cost of every dollar-denominated energy import, and Europe imports essentially all its gas.

Europe's tightness isn't a weather story

Across the Atlantic, the picture is less about a hot spell and more about a supply gap that has been building since March. Iranian missile strikes on Qatar's Ras Laffan complex damaged two LNG trains and knocked out roughly 17% of the country's export capacity — repairs that QatarEnergy and outside estimates now put at three to five years. Force majeure on cargoes bound for European and Asian buyers has been extended repeatedly since, most recently through the end of September, cutting into the very cargoes Europe needed to refill storage over the summer injection season.

The result shows up plainly in the numbers. EU gas storage sits at roughly 68% of capacity, about 16 percentage points below the five-year seasonal average and the lowest absolute level since 2013. Dutch TTF, the European benchmark, touched an annual high near €84 per megawatt-hour in mid-September — its highest since December 2022 — before easing slightly to around €81. Goldman Sachs has flagged the possibility of TTF breaking above €100/MWh at the peak of winter if Gulf LNG exports don't recover; Morgan Stanley's base case already sits at €85 for the season, with the same €100 threshold as the risk case if a cold winter coincides with further Qatari disruption.

Two tightenings, one direction

The US move and the European move are not, strictly, the same story — one is a short-run weather and output wobble, the other a structural supply loss from a damaged export complex that won't be fixed this winter. But they're pointing the same way at the same time, and that convergence is what the desk should not wave off as coincidence. US gas strength is a reasonable leading indicator for global tightness precisely because American LNG cargoes are one of the few remaining swing sources Europe can lean on to make up for the Qatari shortfall; when the domestic US market tightens, the marginal cargo that would have gone to Rotterdam or Zeebrugge gets more expensive too.

Layer the dollar on top and the arithmetic gets worse before it gets better. Gas is priced and shipped in dollars; a currency that is 1.83% stronger over the month means European and UK buyers are paying more in euros and sterling for the same molecule, on top of a TTF price that is already flirting with three-year highs. It is a double tax on import-dependent economies at exactly the point in the calendar — the start of the northern-hemisphere heating season — when their leverage to walk away from the market is lowest.

What to watch

The next real data points are the weekly EIA storage report, due Thursday, and the pace of Qatari force-majeure extensions, which have been renewed roughly monthly since March and are next due for a decision around the end of September. If Doha extends again into October, expect TTF to test the €90 mark rather than drift back toward €80, storage deficit or not. The desk's working question — US weather quirk versus genuine global tightening — will be answered less by the American data than by whether European storage manages to claw back any ground before the first real cold snap arrives.
