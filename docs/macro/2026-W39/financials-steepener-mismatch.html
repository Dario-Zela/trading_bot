A steeper curve was supposed to help financials — it isn't

The New York Stock Exchange sign on Broad Street — financials have decoupled from the steepening trade that's supposed to help them. — Billie Grace Ward / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
Textbook says banks win when the curve steepens; XLF is down 5.25% on the month and LQD's duration losses point to securities-book pain, not credit stress.

The textbook trade this autumn should have been simple: the Treasury curve is steepening sharply, long rates are climbing faster than short ones, and banks — which borrow short and lend long — are supposed to be the beneficiaries. The 2s10s spread has pushed out to roughly 0.26 percentage points, the 30-year yield touched 5.50% on September 25, and every strategist note on financials this year has repeated some version of the same line: a steeper curve widens net interest margin.

XLF, the sector's benchmark ETF, did not get the memo. It is down 1.83% over the past week and 5.25% over the past month, even as the curve did exactly what the playbook wanted.

Where the margin story breaks down

The mechanism itself isn't wrong. Banks that fund themselves with short-term deposits and hold longer-dated loans or securities do see margins improve as the curve steepens — several smaller lenders have reported exactly that in recent quarterly filings, citing higher yields on securities and a lower cost of deposits. But that benefit shows up with a lag, booked quarter by quarter as maturing low-yield assets roll into higher-yielding ones. What a steepening curve delivers immediately, before any of that accrues, is a mark-to-market hit on the long-duration bonds banks already hold.

That immediate hit looks to be what's showing up in the price action, and the bond market is pointing more clearly at it than the stock market is. LQD, the investment-grade corporate bond ETF that skews long-duration, fell 1.42% over the past week — worse than the 0.85% drop in HYG, the high-yield ETF that carries shorter duration and more credit risk. If this were a credit scare, junk bonds should have led the selloff. Instead the high-quality, long-duration paper cracked harder, which is a duration story, not a credit one. High-yield spreads, at roughly 266 basis points, remain historically tight — nowhere near levels associated with genuine credit stress.

An echo, not a repeat, of 2023

That combination — long-duration bonds losing value while credit stays calm — is the same mechanism that sat underneath the securities-book losses at Silicon Valley Bank and other regional lenders in 2023, when rapid rate moves left banks sitting on unrealized losses in held-to-maturity and available-for-sale portfolios. Nothing here suggests a repeat of that episode; deposit bases are more stable now and the move has been gradual rather than a shock. But the direction of the mechanism is the same, and it helps explain why XLF has decoupled from a steepening trade it should theoretically be cheering. Notably, financials have also been getting squeezed by unrelated forces this month — a stretch of Mag 7 and AI-driven strength has pulled money toward technology names and away from banks — but the LQD-versus-HYG gap suggests the securities-book effect is real on top of that rotation, not just a rotation story dressed up as a rates story.

The next real test comes with third-quarter bank earnings in mid-October, when lenders disclose accumulated other comprehensive income and any changes in unrealized losses on their available-for-sale books. If those disclosures show securities losses widening in step with the long end's climb, it will confirm that the curve's near-term bite is outweighing its long-term promise — and that XLF's slide is about balance sheets, not sentiment.
