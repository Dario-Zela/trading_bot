The 30-year closes in on 5% as the long end does the work

In one sentence
The 30-year Treasury yield is pressing 5% while short rates barely move — this week's selloff is a term-premium and fiscal-supply story, not a Fed repricing.

The number to watch this week isn't the Federal Reserve's rate — it hasn't moved. It's the 30-year Treasury yield, which closed at 4.985%, a hair's breadth from the 5% line that has repeatedly marked the edge of investor tolerance for long-dated US government debt over the past three years.

That's the whole story in one sentence: this is a long-end move, not a Fed move. Three-month bills, the purest read on where traders think overnight policy sits, were at 3.668% — barely budged. The five-year note, still short enough to be anchored by Fed expectations, sat at 4.23%. It's only once the curve stretches past the belly, into the ten-year and thirty-year sectors, that the selloff shows up. The ten-year, implied by the roughly 82-basis-point gap between bills and that maturity, is trading near 4.49%. The thirty-year alone has done the heavy lifting.

Not a policy repricing

The Federal Reserve held its target range at 3.5%–3.75% at the June 16–17 meeting, and futures markets aren't pricing a near-term move in either direction — the next decision doesn't land until the July 28–29 meeting. If anything, the committee's own dot plot has drifted hawkish, with nine of 18 officials now penciling in at least one hike by year-end and the median year-end projection creeping up to 3.8% from 3.4% in March. That shift happened weeks ago and is already in the price of front-end paper. It doesn't explain why the long bond has moved again this week while five-year yields stayed put.

What's left is the classic long-end suspect: term premium, the extra compensation investors demand to hold duration through an uncertain fiscal and inflation path, rather than rolling over a string of short bills. The thirty-year already touched roughly 5.1%–5.2% in mid-May, its highest level in about a year, before drifting back below 5%. Demand at that auction was unremarkable — a bid-to-cover ratio of 2.30, below the six-month average, and a slight tail versus the when-issued yield — consistent with a market absorbing large, recurring supply rather than one reacting to fresh Fed signals.

The 5% line

That threshold has mattered before. Each time the thirty-year has pressed against 5% since late 2023, it has coincided with tighter financial conditions and equity drawdowns, making it as much a psychological marker as a mechanical one. Whether this approach holds or breaks through again likely hinges less on anything Jerome Powell says at the end of the month and more on how the next round of long-bond auctions clears, and on whether the deficit trajectory driving fiscal-supply concerns shows any sign of narrowing. Watch auction tails and bid-to-cover ratios in the coming weeks — they will say more about the 5% line's fate than the FOMC statement will.
