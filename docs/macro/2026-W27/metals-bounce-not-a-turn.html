Gold and silver's best week in a month doesn't erase a brutal one

400-ounce gold bars — the metal's weekly bounce still leaves it down for the month. — Andrzej Barabasz (Chepry) / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0
In one sentence
Gold and silver jumped on soft jobs data and rate-cut hopes, but a five-day bounce doesn't offset a 20-day rout, and base metals didn't join the move.

Gold and silver just had their best week in a month. Spot gold rose roughly 2.3% to around $4,182 an ounce, and silver did better still, up about 6.7% to near $62.77 — both on track for their first weekly gains in five weeks. After the stretch they've just been through, that qualifies as relief. It does not qualify as a turn.

The rally had a clean trigger: Friday's US jobs report showed the economy added just 57,000 jobs in June, the weakest reading in four months and well short of the roughly 110,000 forecast. Traders read it as confirmation that the Federal Reserve has less room to keep leaning hawkish, and fed funds futures pulled the odds of a September rate hike below 50%, down from around 67% before the report. Cheaper money, or at least less-expensive money, is the oldest tailwind precious metals have. Gold also caught a bid from easing Strait of Hormuz shipping risk and signs of progress in indirect US-Iran talks, which took some of the geopolitical premium out of oil and, with it, some of the inflation anxiety that had been keeping the Fed cautious.

A bounce, measured against the hole it's climbing out of

Set the five-day numbers next to the twenty-day ones and the picture changes. Gold is still down about 7.29% over the past month even after this week's gain. Silver is down roughly 16.9% over the same stretch — the widest gap between its five-day and twenty-day return of any asset in this week's cross-asset snapshot. A metal that fell nearly a fifth of its value in a month and then rallied 5% in five trading days hasn't recovered; it has partially retraced. The gold-to-silver ratio tells the same story from a different angle: it blew out toward 85-to-1 during the selloff, compressed back toward the mid-60s as silver led the bounce, and remains above the roughly 65-to-1 long-run average — nowhere near the 40-to-50 range that typically marks an actual bull phase in the metals complex.

The cleanest test of whether this is a genuine inflection or a short-covering pop is whether the rally is showing up anywhere else in the commodity complex. It isn't, not yet. Base metals, tracked here via DBB, were still down 0.83% for the week and 8.58% over the month — a sector that has nothing to do with rate-cut hopes and everything to do with industrial demand, and it simply didn't participate. Copper told a similar story: it climbed roughly 1% on the same weak jobs data, enough to snap a two-week losing streak, but that's a dead-cat bounce off a low base, not a demand story. If softer Fed expectations were driving a broad reflation trade, base metals and industrial commodities would be leading it, not lagging a full percentage point behind even as gold and silver ran.

That divergence matters because gold and silver's rally isn't really a growth story — it's a rates-and-safety story. Softer jobs data cuts both ways: it lowers the odds of a hike, which precious metals like, but it's also a signal of a slowing economy, which is exactly why industrial metals aren't following. A metals complex that's genuinely turning higher should see the safe-haven trade and the industrial-demand trade move together. Right now they're moving in opposite directions, and that's a tell about what's actually priced in.

The next test comes fast. If the rate-cut repricing holds and Treasury yields keep drifting down, gold and silver should show follow-through into next week rather than fading back toward their monthly lows. If instead the bond move reverses — and long-end yields have been the more volatile side of this market lately — the metals bounce likely reverts with it, and the 20-day trend reasserts itself as the more reliable read.
