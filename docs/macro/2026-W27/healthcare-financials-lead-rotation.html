Health care and financials lead a rotation that has nothing to do with tech's earnings

The trading floor of the New York Stock Exchange, where this week's sector dispersion has been the widest of the year. — Scott Beale / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Health care and financials are running away from tech and energy by the widest sector spread on record, and it's a defensive story, not an earnings one.

The scoreboard for this week's stock-market sectors has a shape that doesn't map onto the story most investors have been telling themselves for the past six months. That story was about artificial intelligence, chip demand, and a handful of mega-cap technology names pulling the rest of the market along behind them. This week, technology was the market's second-worst performer, and the two sectors doing the pulling were health care and banks — neither of which has anything to do with a GPU.

The Health Care Select Sector SPDR Fund (XLV) is up 5.21% over five trading days and 10.97% over twenty, the best showing on both horizons of any sector in the market. The Financial Select Sector SPDR (XLF) is close behind, up 4.06% over the week and 9.34% over the month. At the other end of the table, the Technology Select Sector SPDR (XLK) is down 2.16% over five days and 7.97% over twenty, while the Energy Select Sector SPDR (XLE) has shed 1.61% and 9.35% over the same windows. The spread between the best and worst sector — roughly 20 percentage points on the 20-day measure — is the widest gap in the table, wider than at any point in recent memory. That kind of dispersion usually means something is genuinely changing under the surface, not just noisy week-to-week rotation.

Banks are getting an honest tailwind

Of the two leaders, financials' rally is the easier one to explain. The move coincides with a Treasury curve that has been steepening as long-dated yields march higher — the 10-year breached 4.48% this week, blowing through levels that would have counted as a dovish reversal just a few weeks ago (a move detailed in this edition's coverage of the 30-year's approach to 5%). For lenders, a steeper curve is close to free money: banks borrow short, through deposits, and lend long, through mortgages and commercial loans, so the wider that gap gets, the fatter the net interest margin. That's the mechanical story, and it lines up with what banks themselves have been signaling — dividend increases and buyback announcements followed this year's Federal Reserve stress tests, and dealmaking activity has picked up enough that some desks are calling it a resurgence in bank M&A. On July 1, XLF gained 2.18% on a single session even as XLK fell 2.57%, a pairing that shows up again and again in the daily tape this week.

That the curve move is also unsettling other corners of the market — the dollar, credit spreads, emerging-market currencies — makes financials' strength a genuine idiosyncratic story rather than a pure risk-on signal: banks are benefiting from the same steepening that is complicating everyone else's month, which is precisely why the sector's outperformance reads as fundamental rather than sentiment-driven.

Health care looks less like conviction, more like retreat

The health care rally is a different animal. There are real catalysts underneath it — the FDA's advisory committee voted unanimously on July 2 to back Moderna's mRNA-based flu vaccine, and roughly 20 million Medicare patients became newly eligible for obesity-drug coverage under a July 1 expansion that Eli Lilly has been citing as a tailwind. Those are genuine, sector-specific developments. But the breadth of the move looks bigger than any single announcement can explain, and the timing lines up too neatly with the technology sector's stumble to be coincidence. Healthcare, alongside utilities and consumer staples, held up better than semiconductors through the worst of this week's tech drawdown — a pattern that reads as capital rotating out of richly valued, high-beta growth names and into a sector with durable earnings and, over the trailing twelve months, a record of outperforming the S&P 500 on 85% of the days when the index fell more than 1%. That is a defensive profile, not a growth story, and it's worth noting because a rally built on genuine catalysts sustains itself if the catalysts keep coming; a rally built on fear of everything else evaporates the moment the everything-else stops being scary.

The tell in the middle of the table

The more interesting number in the sector table this week isn't at either extreme — it's Communication Services (XLC), which gained 3.81% over five days but is still down 2.21% over twenty. That combination describes an early-stage reversal: enough buying pressure this week to produce the second sharpest move in the table, not nearly enough to erase a month of prior damage. The sector's largest holdings — Meta, Alphabet, Netflix — sit close enough to the technology complex that XLC's behavior often serves as a leading indicator for whether a tech selloff is stabilizing or just pausing. If the reversal holds and broadens over the next two weeks, it would suggest the growth-to-defensive rotation is closer to spent than fresh. If it stalls, XLC's bounce will look like exactly what a one-week snapback inside a downtrend usually is.

The backdrop that ties all of this together is a Federal Reserve under Chair Kevin Warsh that has turned notably less dovish than markets expected earlier this year — nine of eighteen rate-setting committee members now favor higher rates in 2026, a reversal from a March consensus that penciled in a cut. That repricing is what's driving the curve steepening benefiting banks, and it's also what has technology and other long-duration growth trades under pressure, since their valuations depend more heavily on the path of future rates than a bank's net interest margin does.

The next test for this rotation comes on two fronts: whether the 10-year yield keeps climbing toward the psychologically loaded 4.5%-plus territory discussed elsewhere in this edition, and whether Communication Services' bounce this week turns into a second consecutive positive week or reverts to the month's prevailing trend. Either would say more about whether this rotation has legs than another day of headlines about chip demand.
