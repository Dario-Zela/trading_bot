The US market is pricing higher-for-longer without the growth confidence to match

The trading floor at the New York Stock Exchange, where this week's rotation out of technology and into defensives played out. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Rising yields, a defensive equity rotation, and a collapsing oil price are pointing at three different economic stories at once — and credit isn't buying any of them.

Look at any single chart from this week and you get a clean story. Look at all of them together and the story falls apart.

The 10-year Treasury yield climbed to 4.485%, up from 4.372% a week earlier, with the curve steepening further — the spread between 3-month bills and the 10-year note now sits at 81.7 basis points, the widest in months. Rising long yields are usually a growth-and-inflation signal: the economy is running hot, or the Fed is stuck higher for longer. Except the week's other data point cuts against that. On July 3, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that the U.S. economy added just 57,000 jobs in June, roughly half the 115,000 economists expected, with May's initially strong 172,000 print revised down to 129,000. The unemployment rate ticked down to 4.2%, but the hiring trend is unmistakably cooling.

Meanwhile, equity investors spent the week rotating out of technology and into healthcare, financials, staples and utilities — a classic defensive posture that usually accompanies growth fear. And oil fell hard, down more than 26% over 20 trading days, a move that in isolation reads like a demand-destruction warning. Yet credit markets, which are typically the first to flinch when a recession gets priced, have barely moved. Investment-grade spreads remain near 80 basis points and high-yield around 285 — both close to multi-decade tights.

Four signals, four different regimes. That incoherence is itself the domestic story this week.

A steepening curve with no growth story attached

Normally, a curve that steepens because long yields are rising tells you the market has gotten more optimistic about growth, or more worried about inflation running away. Neither fits cleanly here. The weak June payrolls report trimmed the odds of a Federal Reserve rate hike by September from roughly 65% to about 50%, according to CME FedWatch pricing — but it didn't flip the market into pricing cuts. Instead, the long end kept climbing, pulled by a separate and more structural force: term premium. The 30-year Treasury yield touched 5.197% in May, its highest since 2007, as investors have demanded more compensation to hold long-dated government debt amid persistent fiscal deficits and heavy auction supply. That is a bond market pricing higher-for-longer not because the economy is strong, but because Washington's borrowing needs aren't going away — a fiscal story wearing a monetary costume.

Equities are hedging against a slowdown the bond market isn't fully pricing

If rates were rising purely on term premium, you might expect stocks to shrug it off. They haven't. On July 5, the Technology Select Sector SPDR (XLK) fell 2.7%, dragged down by Tesla, Intel and Meta, while Health Care Select Sector SPDR (XLV) gained 2.63% and Financials Select Sector SPDR (XLF) climbed 1.53%. Both defensive sectors have now posted gains on both a 5-day and 20-day basis, while growth-sensitive tech and energy are negative on both windows. Some of this is sector-specific mean reversion — XLK had run up roughly 33% year-to-date on the AI infrastructure trade before June's pullback, and investors are simply taking profits after a run that outpaced fundamentals. But financials rallying on the same day the jobs report softened suggests investors are leaning into the idea that a slowing labor market eventually forces the Fed's hand on cuts, whatever term-premium pressure is doing to the long end in the meantime. It is a rotation that hedges against a growth wobble the bond market, for its own structural reasons, isn't fully reflecting.

Oil's plunge is a supply story wearing a demand costume

The most misleading signal this week may be oil. A 26% drop over a month is the kind of move that historically accompanies a demand-destruction scare — the market pricing in a genuine slowdown in consumption. But the more direct cause here is supply, not demand. A mid-June U.S.-Iran ceasefire framework reopened the Strait of Hormuz, and the United Arab Emirates has restored exports to more than 3.9 million barrels a day, pushing total daily flows through the strait past 10 million barrels. Crude had spiked above $100 a barrel during the conflict; it has since given back essentially the entire war premium, trading back near $67-70 for WTI. That is a geopolitical risk premium unwinding, not a fresh recession signal — though the EIA's own outlook does flag softening global demand growth into the second half of the year, muddying even this read. Energy stocks, which had been 2026's best-performing sector on the back of that same war premium, are now among its worst, a mirror image of the same unwind.

What doesn't add up, and why it matters

Put the four signals side by side and no single macro regime explains all of them. Rising yields say fiscal worry, not growth optimism. Defensive equity rotation says growth caution, but only in stocks. Oil says supply glut more than demand collapse. And credit — the market with arguably the best track record of sniffing out real distress before it shows up elsewhere — says nothing is wrong at all. Desks are not disagreeing about the data; they are trading different parts of the same week's data through entirely different lenses, and none of those lenses currently commands enough conviction to pull the others into line.

The next test comes fast. The June CPI print lands in the coming weeks and will show whether the labor market's cooling is starting to bleed into prices, which would validate the bond market's fiscal-premium read or undercut it entirely. Fed officials speak repeatedly over the following fortnight, and any hint of near-term cut timing would collide directly with a 30-year yield still hovering near two-decade highs. Until one of those data points forces a resolution, the fairest description of this market is not confidence in any direction, but four desks pricing four different worlds off the same numbers.
