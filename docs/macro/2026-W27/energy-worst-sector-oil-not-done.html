Energy is now the market's worst-performing sector, and oil says it isn't over

A pumpjack in West Texas — U.S. crude output remains a swing factor as oil unwinds its 2026 war premium. — Eric Kounce (TexasRaiser), Wikimedia Commons, public domain
In one sentence
XLE's 20-day slide is tracking oil's 26% collapse almost tick for tick, and the gap argues for more pain ahead.

Energy has flipped from 2026's best-performing sector to its worst in the space of a single quarter, and the reason isn't a mystery — it's sitting in the crude oil market, where prices have fallen off a cliff.

The Energy Select Sector SPDR Fund (XLE), the standard proxy for U.S. oil and gas equities, is down 1.61% over the past five trading days and 9.35% over the past twenty. That's a rough stretch for a sector that was up 21% at the halfway point of the year. But the equity move looks almost restrained next to what's happened in the underlying commodity: the United States Oil Fund (USO) has dropped 4.88% in five days and a brutal 26.18% over twenty — one of the steepest drawdowns of any asset in the market right now.

In one sentence: XLE's 20-day slide is tracking oil's 26% collapse almost tick for tick, and the gap between them argues the sector still has catching down to do.

A war premium unwinding fast

The scale of the move only makes sense against what came before it. Oil spent the spring pricing a shooting war: after the U.S. and Israel struck Iranian targets and Iran retaliated by threatening tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz, Brent crude spiked toward $107 a barrel in May as roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil trade backed up behind a chokepoint. That premium is now coming out just as fast as it went in. A ceasefire took hold in April, a memorandum of understanding followed in mid-June, and shipping through Hormuz has been rebuilding — the UAE alone has restored exports above 3.9 million barrels a day. WTI, which was trading near $71.59 in mid-June, tumbled to around $67 a barrel by the start of July, essentially back to pre-war levels.

Layered on top of the geopolitical unwind is a supply story that was bearish long before Iran entered the picture. The EIA and most large forecasters had been calling for a global surplus approaching 3-4 million barrels a day in 2026 on the back of OPEC+ production increases, with several banks projecting WTI under $60 by year-end even before the war premium existed. That structural overhang hasn't gone away — it was simply obscured for two months by a conflict that has now mostly resolved.

Why equities haven't caught down yet

XLE's 9.35% slide over twenty days is real pain, but it's less than half the size of oil's 26.18% collapse over the same window — a gap that matters given how the fund is built. Exxon Mobil and Chevron alone make up nearly 40% of XLE's weight, and integrated supermajors with refining and chemicals arms don't move dollar-for-dollar with the front-month crude price the way smaller exploration-and-production names do. That concentration has cushioned the fund so far. It hasn't insulated it.

The contrast within the energy complex itself makes the point. Natural gas, measured through the United States Natural Gas Fund (UNG), has barely moved — down just 1.45% over five days and 1.11% over twenty, with Henry Hub holding near $3.19 per MMBtu on strong storage injections and steady LNG export demand. The sector's pain is concentrated almost entirely in crude, not gas, which is exactly what a war-premium unwind should look like: a shock localized to the commodity that was actually threatened by the Strait of Hormuz risk.

What's next

The open question is whether $67 WTI is a floor or a waypoint. If Hormuz traffic keeps normalizing and the OPEC+ surplus reasserts itself as the dominant story, the pre-war bearish case — WTI drifting toward $60 or below — comes back into view, and XLE's laggard status would have further to run before the equity move matches the commodity move already priced into crude. The next real test comes with the formal 60-day deadline on the Iran memorandum of understanding, and with OPEC+'s upcoming production decision, both of which will determine whether the barrels currently flowing back through the Gulf keep flowing at this pace.
