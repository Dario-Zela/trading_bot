Credit shrugs off the rate spike — for now

Both high-yield and investment-grade credit ETFs posted losses this week, even as high yield outperformed on a relative basis. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
High yield is outperforming investment grade even as both ETFs fell this week, a complacency signal this desk isn't ready to trust.

Junk bonds had a better week than investment-grade paper. That sentence, read on its own, sounds like a market cheering itself hoarse over risk appetite. The reality underneath it is less flattering: both slices of the credit market lost money this week, and the one that supposedly signals confidence in economic growth simply lost less.

In one sentence: High yield is outperforming investment grade even as both ETFs fell this week, a complacency signal this desk isn't ready to trust.

The scoreboard, and its trick

The iShares iBoxx High Yield Corporate Bond ETF (HYG) fell 0.21% over the past five sessions. The iShares iBoxx Investment Grade Corporate Bond ETF (LQD) fell 0.79% over the same stretch — a gap of 57 basis points in high yield's favor. Framed as a horse race, junk won comfortably.

But a horse race implies someone crossed the finish line ahead. Here, both horses stumbled. LQD's total return was negative. HYG's total return was negative too. The interesting number isn't which one lost less — it's that credit, in aggregate, lost money in a week when the setup should have made investors nervous, not merely mildly annoyed.

The reason IG usually underperforms HY in a rate spike is duration: investment-grade portfolios carry longer average maturities, so they take a bigger hit when yields move. The 10-year Treasury yield pushed to roughly 4.485% this week, a level last threatened in the spring, and that alone explains most of LQD's underperformance — it's a rate story more than a credit story. High yield's shorter duration and slightly better carry cushioned it. That's mechanics, not conviction. It says nothing about whether junk bond buyers are pricing default risk correctly; it says their bonds mature sooner.

What the credit tape isn't pricing

Strip away the HY/IG comparison and look at what else happened this week. Oil fell sharply — West Texas Intermediate is down roughly 26% over the past 20 trading days, a collapse that has pushed crude back toward levels last seen before this year's Middle East flare-up, as shipping through the Strait of Hormuz normalized and supply from the Gulf states came back online faster than expected. Energy stocks took the hit directly: the Energy Select Sector SPDR (XLE) is down 9.35% over 20 days, the worst-performing sector in the market. Technology hasn't been spared either — the Tech Select Sector SPDR (XLK) is down 7.97% over the same window, as money rotated out of growth names and into more defensive corners like health care and financials.

That is not a quiet backdrop. A 26%-in-a-month oil collapse squeezes energy-sector issuers directly — many of them mid-tier exploration and production names that sit disproportionately in the high-yield universe. A rotation this sharp out of two major sectors at once is the kind of dislocation that has, in past cycles, shown up in credit spreads within weeks, not months. Energy paper alone makes up a meaningful slice of the ICE BofA high-yield index, and its option-adjusted spread — sitting in the mid-2% range as of late June — has room to widen before it reflects anything resembling stress.

So far, it hasn't. Spreads have crept modestly wider in recent weeks alongside the broader wobble in equities, but nothing in the price action of either ETF suggests credit desks are treating oil's slide or the growth-to-value rotation as a solvency question. That's either evidence the moves are being absorbed in stride — energy issuers hedge output, balance sheets are healthier than in past cycles, and Hormuz-driven supply gluts read as temporary — or it's evidence that credit is simply slower to reprice than equities and commodities, and is still catching up to information the rest of the market has already traded on.

The lag, not the all-clear

Credit markets have a well-documented tendency to move after equities, not alongside them — spread indices are less liquid, rebalance less frequently, and are dominated by buy-and-hold institutional accounts that don't chase daily headlines the way equity funds do. That lag is exactly why a week like this one is more ambiguous than it looks on the surface. HYG's outperformance over LQD reads, at a glance, like a market that has decided the yield spike is a rates problem, not a credit problem. But both ETFs closing the week in the red, against a backdrop of an oil crash and a two-sector rotation, is not the same as credit giving growth risk a clean bill of health. It looks more like credit hasn't gotten the memo yet.

The next tell will come from the primary market and from spread levels specifically, not total-return comparisons between ETFs. Watch whether energy-sector high-yield issuance gets repriced wider at the next new-deal pricing, and whether the ICE BofA high-yield OAS — still in the low-3% area — breaks out of the range it has held since spring. A move through 3.25-3.50% would be the first real sign that credit is starting to price in what oil and the sector rotation have already been saying for a month.
