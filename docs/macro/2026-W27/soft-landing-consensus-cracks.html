EDITORIAL · WEEKLY THESIS · By The Editor · 2026-07-05 · 7 min read

The soft-landing consensus cracks as yields snap back

The advanced trading floor at the New York Stock Exchange — the venue where this week's rotation out of tech and into defensives played out. — Eduard Hueber / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A bond selloff, a collapsing oil price, and a rotation into defensives all broke the same week — markets no longer trust the dovish, soft-landing script.

For most of June, the market's working theory was simple: inflation was cooling, the labor market was softening just enough to let the Federal Reserve ease off, and the 10-year Treasury yield — the anchor rate for mortgages, corporate borrowing, and stock valuations alike — was heading lower. Last week this publication's rates desk went further, calling for the 10-year to break below 4.25% by mid-July. Instead, it broke the other way. On July 2 the 10-year closed at 4.485%, up from 4.372% a week earlier, blowing through the 4.45% line that would have falsified the dovish call. It didn't clear that line quietly; it printed 4.485% on a day when the jobs report came in weak enough that, on the old script, yields should have fallen.

That single data point would be a footnote on its own. It isn't on its own. The same week that yields refused to cooperate, oil fell another 4.88%, extending a 20-day slide to 26.18% — one of the sharper commodity moves of the year. Money rotated out of technology stocks and into healthcare and financials with a force not seen since the spring. And gold and silver, which usually sit out weeks when bonds are selling off, put in their best week in a month. Individually, each of these is explicable. Together, they describe a market that has stopped believing the clean version of the soft-landing story and started pricing something messier.

The bond market's inconvenient steepening

The mechanics of the Treasury move matter more than the headline number. June nonfarm payrolls rose by just 57,000, badly missing the 115,000 consensus and well below May's revised 129,000, with the unemployment rate ticking down to 4.2% only because the labor-force participation rate fell to its lowest level since 2021 — a soft print by any conventional reading. Short-dated yields did what the textbook says they should: the 2-year fell roughly 2 basis points as traders trimmed the odds of a Fed rate hike at the July 28–29 meeting to under 20%, from nearly 29% a day earlier. But the 10-year rose anyway, and the 30-year — now at 4.985%, within striking distance of the 5% threshold it last crossed convincingly before the financial crisis — kept climbing with it. The 3-month/10-year spread has widened to +81.7 basis points, and the steepening is concentrated from the belly of the curve out to the long end.

That combination — short rates easing on labor weakness, long rates rising anyway — is a bear steepener, and it is not the shape a soft landing is supposed to take. A soft landing looks like a bull flattener: yields fall across the curve as inflation cools and cuts arrive on schedule, credit stays calm, and risk assets keep climbing because the path ahead is orderly. What the market delivered instead looks like a term-premium story: investors are demanding more compensation to hold long-dated government debt even as the economy shows signs of slowing, which is a symptom of doubt about the fiscal and inflation outlook, not confidence in it. New Fed chair Kevin Warsh, who took the oath of office on May 22 and has made clear in his first public remarks that bringing inflation back to the 2% target takes precedence over supporting a wobbling labor market, has done nothing to discourage that read. The Fed's own June projections shifted from a single anticipated cut in March to eight of the committee's members penciling in no move at all this year, an acknowledgment that inflation is running at its highest in three years even as hiring slows.

Oil's collapse complicates, rather than confirms, the story

A falling oil price is usually read as the market's cleanest vote on demand — and a 26% collapse over four weeks would ordinarily be treated as a flashing warning about growth. This one deserves a caveat. A meaningful share of the decline traces to the resolution of the Iran-Israel conflict rather than to weakening consumption: the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz, a widened shipping route certified by the U.S. Navy's Joint Maritime Information Center in late June, and Saudi Arabia's crude exports rebounding to roughly 90% of pre-war levels have all added supply back into a market that had priced in a war premium. Brent, which spiked above $126 a barrel at the height of the crisis, has now given back more than 38% of that move.

Supply-driven or not, the effect on markets is the same: oil's plunge is disinflationary at the headline level, which should, in isolation, support the case for the Fed to ease. That it is coinciding with rising long-term yields rather than falling ones is precisely what makes the current moment hard to file under either the optimistic or the pessimistic script. A demand collapse would argue for lower yields and a rush to safety across the board. A supply glut with resilient demand would argue for calmer bonds and steady risk appetite. What's showing up instead is a hybrid: cheaper energy, a bond market pricing higher term risk, and equity investors moving money as if they don't fully believe either story.

Equities are already voting for caution

That equity signal is not subtle. The Technology Select Sector SPDR (XLK) is down 2.16% over five days and 7.97% over twenty, while the Health Care Select Sector SPDR (XLV) has gained 5.21% and 10.97% over the same windows — a swing of roughly 20 percentage points between the two sectors in a month. Financials have moved with healthcare rather than with tech. Individual names underline the rotation's texture: Tesla shares fell more than 7% even after reporting second-quarter deliveries of 480,126 vehicles, comfortably ahead of the roughly 406,000 analysts expected, while Meta dropped nearly 5% on analyst warnings that 2027 capital spending could approach $200 billion. Growth stocks are being punished even when they beat, which is a tell that the market is repricing the multiple investors are willing to pay for future earnings, not just the earnings themselves.

Meanwhile gold has climbed toward roughly $4,182 an ounce, on pace for its best week in five, and silver has outpaced it — both moves consistent with investors buying insurance against inflation and policy uncertainty rather than betting on renewed economic strength. That gold and long-dated Treasuries are not moving in the same direction — one up on inflation-hedge demand, the other selling off on term-premium concerns — is itself a sign that the market has not settled on a single narrative. It is hedging several at once.

What the parts add up to

No one of these moves is decisive by itself. A bear-steepening bond market, a geopolitically-driven oil slump, a defensive equity rotation, and a precious-metals bid could each be explained away individually. Laid side by side, they describe a market that has abandoned the tidy version of the soft landing — falling yields, falling inflation, resilient growth, all arriving together — without yet committing to a recession call either. What's left is a stagflation-adjacent wobble: growth signals from oil and technology keep softening, while the instrument that is supposed to reward that softening with lower borrowing costs is instead getting more expensive. Warsh's Fed, newly hawkish and explicitly prioritizing its inflation mandate, is one reason long yields aren't cooperating. A widening fiscal deficit and heavy Treasury issuance are almost certainly compounding it, adding term premium regardless of what the labor market does next.

The next real test arrives July 28–29, when the FOMC meets for the first time since the June jobs miss and the bond market's about-face. A hold with dovish language would go some way toward validating the original soft-landing case; a hold that reads as unmoved by softening data would confirm that this is a Fed, and a bond market, no longer taking its cues primarily from the labor market. Either way, the 10-year's failure to fall below 4.25% as forecast last week is now the data point every other call in this section has to be measured against.
