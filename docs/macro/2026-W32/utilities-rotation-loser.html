Utilities are the rotation's biggest loser

Overhead power transmission lines — utilities' bond-like dividend profile has made the sector the biggest casualty of the long end's climb. — Nixdorf / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
XLU's 20-day slide deepens to -3.96% as the 30-year yield near 5.21% makes bond-proxy utilities lose out to a steepening curve and a cyclicals bid.

Every sector table has a bottom row, and this week utilities own it outright. The Utilities Select Sector SPDR Fund (XLU) is down 1.67% over five trading days and 3.96% over twenty — the worst 20-day showing of any S&P 500 sector — as the long end of the Treasury curve keeps grinding higher and takes the market's classic bond substitutes down with it.

The mechanism is not complicated. Utility stocks pay out most of their earnings as dividends, which makes them behave, in investors' heads, less like growth equities and more like long-duration bonds. When a genuinely safe asset — a 30-year Treasury bond — starts yielding 5.21%, against a 3-month bill anchored near 3.71%, the math on a regulated power company yielding roughly 3% gets harder to defend. Why hold a utility for income when the government will pay more for less risk?

Real estate, the other classic rate-sensitive sleeve of the market, is showing the same symptom in a milder form: the XLRE real estate SPDR is off 0.20% over five days. Both sectors carry heavy debt loads and long-dated cash flows, which is precisely the combination that gets marked down when the discount rate on the far end of the curve rises. What's different this time is that the curve isn't just rising — it's steepening. The 30-year climbing while the front end stays pinned near the Fed's policy rate is a signature of a bear steepener: the market pricing near-term rate cuts even as it demands more term premium for locking up money for three decades, on worries about long-run deficits and Treasury issuance.

A story rates are writing, not fundamentals

The irony is that utilities have had a genuinely strong operating story this year. Data-center power demand tied to AI buildout has utilities on pace to spend roughly $240 billion on grid capacity in 2026, according to sector analysts tracking the capex cycle, and regulated returns on that spending are about as close to a sure thing as the stock market offers. None of that has mattered this month. A sector can have the best rate-base growth story in a decade and still get sold if the discount rate used to value it keeps rising faster than the growth story can offset.

Meanwhile the capital utilities are shedding is finding a home elsewhere. Cyclicals — technology, industrials, consumer discretionary — are picking up the bid that used to sit in bond proxies, consistent with a market that is treating the steepening curve as a signal about growth and inflation rather than recession risk. That is the connective tissue between XLU's slide and the broader rotation showing up across the sector tables this week: it is the same long-end move showing up as a loser in utilities and a tailwind in cyclicals.

The next test comes with Treasury's long-bond auction calendar and any fresh data on inflation or federal borrowing needs — supply news that could push the 30-year toward 5.3% or give it a reason to pause. Until the long end stops climbing, utilities are unlikely to find a bid, no matter how strong the AI-driven demand backdrop looks on paper.
