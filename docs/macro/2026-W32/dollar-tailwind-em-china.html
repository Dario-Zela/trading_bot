A softer dollar is the quiet tailwind for China and EM this week

In one sentence
A fourth straight weekly dollar decline and a firmer base-metals tape point to easier conditions for China and EM assets, even with no China data point in this week's set.

The dollar has now fallen for four straight weeks on the measures tracked in this week's dataset, down 1.36% over twenty trading days. Nothing dramatic happened to cause it — a fifth consecutive Federal Reserve hold, a July payrolls report that missed badly (nonfarm payrolls fell by 23,000, with sizeable downward revisions to the two prior months), and a bout of yen intervention that rattled the index's largest single weighting. But the cumulative effect is the kind of grinding dollar weakness that tends to matter more for the rest of the world than for Wall Street.

Base metals noticed. The Invesco DB Base Metals Fund, a proxy dominated by copper, aluminum and zinc, is up 0.83% over five days and 3.76% over twenty. Copper itself has been the standout: futures in London pushed past $14,000 a ton this week, within reach of the record set in a bout of speculative buying at the start of the year. The immediate triggers are supply-side — a Congolese export ban on copper concentrate, a squeeze on sulphur supplies that feeds smelting — but the demand backdrop is a fresh round of Chinese infrastructure and power-grid investment pledges, plus more than 200,000 tons of copper landing at US ports in July, the largest monthly inflow in over a decade.

The gold connection, and the honest gap

Gold's own rally this year tells a related but distinct story. Central banks now hold roughly a third of global official gold reserves in emerging-market hands, up from 18% at the turn of the century, and the World Gold Council's latest survey found 89% of reserve managers expect global central bank holdings to rise further over the next year. China's central bank alone has reported gold reserves near 2,113 tonnes as of the end of 2025, with monthly purchases still running at 10-15 tonnes during bouts of geopolitical tension. That is a slower-moving, structural theme — reserve diversification away from dollar assets that accelerated after Russia's central bank assets were frozen in 2022 — rather than a reaction to this week's data specifically.

What this week's dataset does not contain is a direct China data point: no fresh PMI, trade balance or yuan fixing in the numbers driving this piece. The read here is inferential — a softer dollar and firmer industrial metals are the kind of financial-conditions easing that historically helps China-exposed and EM assets, via cheaper dollar funding costs, more room for local central banks to cut rates without currency risk, and better terms of trade for commodity exporters. Emerging-market currencies rode exactly this dynamic earlier in 2026 before erasing those gains in July on speculation about firmer US rates. The dollar's renewed slide over the past month reopens that door, even without a headline out of Beijing this week to point to directly.

The next real test arrives with China's July trade and credit data and the PBOC's next reserves disclosure, both due in the coming weeks — either would convert this week's circumstantial case into something more concrete.
