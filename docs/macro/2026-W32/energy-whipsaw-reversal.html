Energy whipsaws from best month to worst week

In one sentence
XLE is giving back most of a month's gains in a week as OPEC+ supply hikes and a fading Hormuz risk premium hit oil and gas together.

Energy stocks spent July looking like the market's best trade. This week they look like a warning sign.

The Energy Select Sector SPDR Fund (XLE), the standard proxy for big oil and gas names, has fallen 3.44% over the past five trading sessions — erasing most of the 4.39% gain it built up over the prior twenty. The reversal tracks crude almost tick for tick: the United States Oil Fund (USO) is down 8.66% on the week after rallying 8.54% over the previous month, and natural gas, via the United States Natural Gas Fund (UNG), has given up 3.18% on top of an already-brutal 8.11% monthly slide.

In one sentence: Energy stocks are giving back a month of gains in a week, as OPEC+ supply hikes and a fading Middle East risk premium hit oil and gas together.

What actually broke

The proximate cause is supply, not demand. OPEC+ approved its fifth consecutive monthly production increase for August, adding roughly 548,000 barrels a day to the group's target as it continues unwinding the voluntary cuts it imposed back in 2023. Brent crude slid to just under $72 a barrel and West Texas Intermediate settled near $68.55 — both close to five-month lows — before a small Friday bounce to about $78 left crude still down more than 7% on the week.

Layered on top of the OPEC+ decision is a geopolitical unwind. The U.S. and Iran signed a memorandum of understanding in June aimed at de-escalating tensions and reopening the Strait of Hormuz to fuller shipping traffic. That agreement has been doing exactly what it was designed to do: strip out the risk premium that had propped up prices through the spring. Every barrel that flows more freely through Hormuz is a barrel the market no longer needs to price for disruption.

Natural gas is falling for a more mundane reason — abundance. The EIA's storage report for the week ended July 31 showed a 33 billion cubic foot injection, on the heavier side of forecasts, with Lower 48 dry gas production running near 110.6 billion cubic feet a day even through peak summer heat. Storage levels are sitting almost 7% above their five-year average. There is, simply, too much gas.

Profit-taking or early signal

The question worth asking is whether this week is energy investors banking gains after a strong run, or the first crack in a demand story that hasn't been tested yet. The honest answer is that the evidence so far points more to supply than to demand. OPEC+'s output decision and the Hormuz reopening are both engineered, deliberate additions to the barrel count — not a symptom of economies buying less oil. Analysts tracking the 2026 balance widely expect a meaningful global surplus this year as supply continues to outpace consumption, which argues this leg down has room to keep running even without a demand shock.

That distinction matters for how the reversal should be read elsewhere in markets. A supply-driven oil slide is disinflationary and, all else equal, a mild tailwind for consumers and for sectors that use energy as an input. A demand-driven one would be the more worrying version — it would say something about growth expectations that equity markets, still near highs, are not yet pricing.

What to watch

The next real test comes from OPEC+ itself: the group meets again in early September to set October output, and a pause or reversal in the hike pace would be the clearest signal yet that producers see the glut as real rather than transitory. Weekly EIA storage and inventory data between now and then will show whether the supply overhang is deepening or stabilizing. Credit markets are also worth watching — energy issuers have so far shown little stress despite the price move, but a sustained slide below $65 WTI would start to test that calm.
