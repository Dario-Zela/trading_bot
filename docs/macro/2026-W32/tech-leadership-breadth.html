Tech runs away from the rest of the market

A modern trading floor — the kind of venue where this week's sector rotation into tech and cyclicals is playing out. — Eduard Hueber, courtesy of Asymptote Architecture / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
XLK's 7.2% five-day surge looks less like a mega-cap trade and more like a capex-driven rotation, given Materials, Discretionary and Industrials are all up 3%+ while Utilities lag.

The tape this week has a simple story sitting on top of it: technology stocks are running away from everything else. The Technology Select Sector SPDR Fund (XLK) — the standard proxy for the sector, anchored by Nvidia, Apple and Microsoft — is up 7.2% over five trading days, more than double the next-best sector and enough on its own to explain most of the week's move in the S&P 500.

That kind of gap invites an obvious question: is this a real, broad-based advance, or is it three or four mega-cap names doing the work while everyone else in the fund goes along for the ride? A look under the hood, and across the rest of the market, suggests it's closer to genuine breadth than a single crowded trade — though not without caveats.

The number that stands out

Start with the scale of the move. A 7.2% five-day gain in a sector ETF is a large jump by any measure — the kind of move that normally accompanies a specific catalyst, not a drift. Zoom out and the picture gets more interesting: XLK's 20-day return is just 1.18%, meaning almost the entire month's gain arrived in the last week alone. That points to a catalyst-driven repricing rather than a slow grind higher, and the most obvious candidate is the ongoing AI infrastructure buildout. Hyperscalers — Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet, Meta and Oracle — are on pace to spend somewhere in the region of $775-800 billion combined on AI infrastructure in 2026, and that capex flows directly into the chip and equipment names that dominate XLK's weighting. Nvidia alone is roughly 14% of the fund; add Apple, Microsoft, Broadcom and AMD and the top five holdings already account for close to half the index.

That concentration is precisely what raises the narrow-trade concern. If Nvidia and Microsoft are moving on capex headlines or earnings beats, a fund weighted so heavily toward them can post a 7% week even if half its 76 constituents are flat. The way to check that isn't to stare harder at XLK — it's to look sideways, at what the rest of the market is doing while tech runs.

Cyclicals aren't just watching

That's where the case for breadth firms up. Materials (XLB) is up 4.82% over the same five days. Consumer Discretionary (XLY) is up 3.25%. Industrials (XLI) is up 2.97%. None of those sectors carry anything like tech's concentration risk — Materials and Industrials in particular are baskets of chemicals, machinery, capital-goods and construction names with no single stock exceeding a mid-single-digit weight. When three sectors that different from each other, and that different from software and semiconductors, all post gains north of 3% in the same week, it's a signal the move isn't confined to one crowded trade.

The read-through connecting them is the same AI capex story wearing different clothes. Industrials names like Caterpillar and Eaton sell the turbines, generators and cooling infrastructure that data centers need. Materials suppliers sit further upstream — copper, specialty chemicals, industrial gases — feeding the physical buildout that AI compute depends on. Discretionary's participation is a touch more conventional, tracking a consumer that so far isn't cracking under the weight of tighter-for-longer policy rates. Put together, what looks like a tech story on the headline number reads more like a capex and reflation story once the other cyclical sectors are laid alongside it.

What's being left behind

The cleanest evidence that this is a rotation, not a broad melt-up, is what's on the other side of the ledger. Utilities (XLU) is down 1.67% over five days and down 3.96% over 20 — the worst performer in either window. That's the mirror image of the cyclical story: money moving out of the bond-proxy, rate-sensitive corner of the market and into names that benefit from growth, capex and risk appetite. Utilities tend to draw a bid when investors want yield and safety; their underperformance here is consistent with a market that, for now, wants neither.

That matters for how the tech rally should be read. A genuine growth-and-capex rotation should produce exactly this pattern — cyclicals and the AI-adjacent supply chain up together, defensives left behind — rather than tech alone gapping higher while everything else, cyclical or defensive, sits still. The data lines up with the former.

None of this rules out the concentration risk resurfacing. XLK's 20-day return lagging so far behind its five-day pop means the sector was essentially flat for three weeks before this move, and a fund this top-heavy can reverse as fast as it rallies if Nvidia or Microsoft earnings disappoint. The next test is straightforward: watch whether Materials, Industrials and Discretionary keep pace with tech through the next data cycle, or fall back once the current capex headlines fade. If the cyclicals keep tracking tech rather than lagging it, that's the strongest evidence yet that this is a market-wide repricing of the AI buildout rather than a handful of mega-caps carrying the index on their own.
