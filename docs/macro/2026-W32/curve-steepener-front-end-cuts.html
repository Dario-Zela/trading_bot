Curve steepens as front end prices cuts the long end won't confirm

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C., where Kevin Warsh now chairs a committee split over whether to cut or hike. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The 3M-10Y Treasury spread has widened to 95bp as front-end yields slide on Fed cut bets, while the 30-year, pinned above 5.2%, keeps steepening the curve on its own terms.

The bond market is arguing with itself. Short-dated Treasury yields have fallen hard on bets that the Federal Reserve is about to start cutting interest rates. The 30-year bond has not gotten the memo — it is still trading above 5.2%, near the highest levels in nearly two decades. The gap between them, the spread between 3-month bills and 10-year notes, has widened to 95 basis points, while a separate stretch of the curve, from 5-year notes out to 30-year bonds, has steepened by roughly 85 basis points on its own.

That is two steepening moves happening for two different reasons, layered on top of each other. One is the kind of steepening that shows up when a central bank starts easing. The other is the kind that shows up when bond investors get nervous about how much debt they are being asked to hold. Untangling which one dominates from here says a great deal about whether the next six months bring an orderly glide toward lower rates or something messier.

A jobs report reshuffles the front end

The proximate trigger was Friday's employment report. Nonfarm payrolls fell by 23,000 in July, a shock against forecasts for an 83,000 gain, and revisions to prior months added to the sense that hiring has stalled. Two-year yields dropped more than 5 basis points to 4.19%, their lowest since mid-July, and CME's FedWatch tool showed the odds of a 50-basis-point cut at the Fed's September meeting jumping to roughly 45%, up from near zero a week earlier.

That repricing lands at an awkward moment for the Fed itself. Kevin Warsh, sworn in as chair in May after succeeding Jerome Powell, held rates at 3.50%–3.75% at the July 29 meeting over the objections of three regional bank presidents — Cleveland's Beth Hammack, Minneapolis's Neel Kashkari and Dallas's Lorie Logan — who wanted to raise rates on stubborn inflation. Warsh's own remarks that day leaned hawkish, emphasizing the Fed's 2% target. The jobs number nine days later scrambled that posture, and markets have moved to pricing a cut at the September 15–16 meeting almost regardless of what August's CPI, due September 11, shows.

The 3-month bill, at 3.71%, still sits close to the top of the Fed's target band — a sign the front end has room to fall further if the committee actually delivers. The 5-year note, more sensitive to the path of policy over several years, has already dropped to 4.36%, pricing a cutting cycle that extends well past September.

The long end won't come along

None of that dovish repricing has reached the back of the curve. The 10-year sits at 4.66% and the 30-year at 5.21%, not far off the 5.198% intraday peak the bond touched in May — the highest level since 2007 — after a soft auction and a hot inflation print rattled buyers. A 30-year sale that same month tailed the when-issued yield and drew below-average bid-to-cover, a textbook sign of indigestion.

The New York Fed's term-premium model has been climbing in step: the compensation investors demand for the risk of holding long-dated debt has swung roughly 118 basis points since its 2020 trough, and readings above 100 basis points are generally read as a market-supply warning rather than a growth or inflation call. The mechanics are straightforward. Washington is running deficits above $2 trillion a year, and someone has to absorb the paper. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has tried to manage the optics by leaning on short-term bill issuance rather than adding to note and bond supply — guidance he has held to for several quarters now, much as Janet Yellen did before him — but the long bond keeps drifting higher anyway. Part of the explanation is a shrinking price-insensitive buyer base: foreign reserve managers have been trimming Treasuries as a share of their holdings since 2022, and that retreat falls hardest on the 20- and 30-year points, where their bid used to matter most.

Two steepeners, one curve

Splitting the curve into its front and back halves clarifies what is actually going on. The 3-month/10-year spread is behaving the way curves normally do early in a cutting cycle — short rates falling faster than long ones as the Fed moves and the market front-runs further moves. That is a bull steepener, and history is not entirely reassuring about it: in the postwar record, recessions have tended to arrive not while the curve is inverted but in the 6 to 18 months after it re-steepens, as cuts arrive in response to a labor market that is already cracking. The July payrolls miss fits that pattern uncomfortably well.

The 5-year/30-year spread is a different animal — a bear steepener, where the back end is rising in yield even as the front end falls, driven by term premium and supply concerns rather than growth expectations. That combination is usually associated with inflation risk or fiscal stress, not recession. Having both signatures present in the same curve at the same time is the unusual part. It means the bond market is simultaneously pricing a Fed that needs to rescue a weakening labor market and a Treasury that cannot borrow as cheaply as it once did, and neither dynamic is offsetting the other.

The risk is that this stops being orderly. If the Fed cuts in September and the 30-year keeps climbing regardless — because auctions keep tailing, because the deficit path doesn't improve, because inflation prints stay sticky — long rates end up doing the opposite of what rate cuts are supposed to do: tightening financial conditions for mortgages, corporate borrowing and the government's own interest bill even as the policy rate falls. That would be a steepener the Fed cannot control from the front end alone, and it is the scenario term-premium watchers are increasingly flagging.

The next test comes fast. August's CPI print on September 11 will shape how much cover Warsh has to cut five days later, and the 30-year's next scheduled auction will show whether May's weak demand was a one-off or the new normal for long-duration paper.
