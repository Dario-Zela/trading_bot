CREDIT · IG VS HY · By T. Larch · 2026-07-26 · 6 min read

Investment-grade bonds are the ones getting hurt, not junk

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C. — the FOMC's July 29–30 decision is the next test of this week's yield backup. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
IG bonds are underperforming junk this week not because credit quality slipped, but because LQD's ~8-year duration is absorbing a sharp Treasury selloff that HYG's ~3-year duration mostly avoids.

Turn on a bond terminal this week and the screen reads backwards. Investment-grade corporate debt — the stuff issued by the likes of Johnson & Johnson and Apple, the paper that's supposed to be boring — has lost 1.24% over the past five sessions, measured by the iShares iBoxx Investment Grade Corporate Bond ETF (LQD). High-yield debt, the junk-rated bonds of weaker borrowers that are supposed to get hit first when markets turn skittish, is down a comparatively mild 0.53%, tracked by the iShares iBoxx High Yield Corporate Bond ETF (HYG). That's a 71-basis-point gap in IG's favor — for the worse.

Normally it runs the other way. When something spooks a market — a growth scare, a liquidity squeeze, a credit event — junk bonds sell off harder than investment-grade, because that's what compensates holders for taking on default risk in the first place. This week inverted that pattern, and the explanation has less to do with credit quality than with a number that has nothing to do with either ETF's balance sheet: duration.

The mechanical explanation

LQD carries an effective duration of roughly 8 years. HYG's is closer to 2.9. That difference of nearly three times means LQD's price is close to three times as sensitive to a given move in interest rates as HYG's — a mechanical fact of bond math, not a judgment about which issuers are healthier.

And rates moved hard this week. The 30-year Treasury yield touched 5.162%, according to Treasury market data, its highest level since May and part of what CNBC and Bloomberg both described as a two-month high across the long end, driven by a stretch of oil-fuelled inflation anxiety. The 10-year climbed alongside it — up to 4.679% in the week's data, from 4.541% previously — while the front end firmed too, with 3-month bills rising to 3.805%. That's a steepening move with the whole curve under pressure, not just the long end drifting on its own, and it's landing three to four days before the Federal Reserve's July 29–30 meeting, which will be read as a referendum on how much of this the Fed is willing to validate.

Investment-grade corporate debt, with its longer average maturities — LQD's weighted average maturity runs close to 13 years, versus a much shorter ladder for the average junk bond — simply has more coupon-years of exposure baked into every basis point of yield move. When the 30-year backs up, LQD absorbs more of the hit almost by construction. HYG, built from shorter-dated paper, shrugs off the same yield move more easily. None of that requires a single downgrade or a single missed coupon payment.

What the spread market is (and isn't) saying

The credit-specific evidence backs this reading. The ICE BofA US High Yield Index option-adjusted spread — the part of a junk bond's yield that compensates for default risk, stripped of the Treasury component — sat around 277 basis points as of July 23, up only modestly from roughly 269bp two weeks earlier. That's a tight spread by historical standards; readings north of 500bp are the traditional marker of genuine credit stress, and this week's print isn't within hailing distance of that. If investors were actually repricing default risk in junk debt, the OAS would be the place it would show up first and hardest — and it isn't moving.

So the two datasets tell a coherent story: yields backed up sharply across the curve, LQD's longer duration made it absorb more of that move in price terms, and HY spreads stayed calm, meaning the market isn't (yet) demanding extra compensation for the risk that weaker borrowers default. Duration pain, not credit repricing — for now.

The second-order risk sitting underneath

The caveat is what's driving the yield backup in the first place. This isn't a generic growth scare or a Fed communication slip — it's an oil shock. Brent crude has surged roughly 25% on renewed Middle East tension, including reports of attacks near the Red Sea and the risk of a wider confrontation with Iran, and that move is what's pushing inflation breakevens and term premium higher across the Treasury curve. Prediction markets have shifted meaningfully toward pricing a 2026 rate hike rather than a cut, a repricing that would have been unthinkable in most 2026 forecasts written before the oil move.

That matters for high-yield specifically because energy issuers are not a footnote in the junk market — they make up something in the neighborhood of 14% of the HY index by weight, concentrated among exploration-and-production and oilfield-services names whose creditworthiness is itself sensitive to the price of oil, just pointed the other way. A supply-driven oil spike is, in the near term, a cash-flow tailwind for those issuers, which is part of why HY spreads haven't budged. But if the same oil shock forces the Fed into a genuinely more hawkish stance — not just a hold, but the kind of tightening repricing prediction markets are now flirting with — the transmission into weaker consumer- and industrial-facing borrowers within HY could turn a duration story on the IG side into an actual credit story on the junk side. Energy issuers benefit from higher oil; borrowers on the other side of the energy cost curve, which make up a much larger share of HY by number of names, do not.

That's the distinction worth holding onto this week: IG's underperformance is a clean, explainable function of duration mismatch against a sharp curve move, not a signal that investment-grade credit quality has deteriorated. But the same oil shock that produced this week's yield backup is also the variable that could, on a longer horizon, turn into the kind of hawkish Fed repricing that stops being kind to energy-adjacent junk issuers and starts squeezing everyone else in the HY index. The duration story and the credit story are not the same story yet — but they share a common trigger.

The next real test lands with the FOMC decision on July 29–30. A hold that leans hawkish, or any signal that the committee is taking the oil-driven inflation impulse seriously, would likely extend the long-end selloff — and keep pressuring IG's duration-heavy book — while giving an early read on whether HY spreads stay this calm once the market has to price a Fed that's genuinely reconsidering its cutting path.
