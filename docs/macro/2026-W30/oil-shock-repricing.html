Oil's 25% surge is quietly repricing the rest of the tape

A crude oil tanker at sea — shipping through the Strait of Hormuz has been disrupted by the US-Iran conflict, driving the month's oil surge. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A month-long, Hormuz-crisis-driven oil surge is now bear-steepening the yield curve, splitting equity sectors, hurting IG credit, and lifting the dollar all at once.

The tape has a new pacemaker, and it isn't the Federal Reserve. It's crude oil.

Over the past month, the United States Oil Fund — the most widely watched proxy for West Texas Intermediate — has risen 25%, with 10 of those points arriving in just the last five trading sessions. That is the sharpest, fastest commodity move of the summer, and it is no longer content to stay in the energy section. It is bleeding into Treasury yields, into the dollar, into the relative pricing of credit risk, and into which sectors of the stock market get bid up versus sold down. A rally that started as a geopolitical shock is starting to look like something with its own momentum — a genuine, if young, inflation impulse working its way through every desk in the building.

The proximate cause is not mysterious. Fighting between the United States and Iran has escalated for a twelfth straight night, with U.S. strikes targeting Iranian command and missile sites and Iran hitting back at American bases in the Gulf. Tehran has effectively closed the Strait of Hormuz to U.S.-linked shipping, and Saudi and Qatari tankers have already been hit near the strait — a waterway that normally carries roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil. Brent has traded above $100 a barrel; U.S. gasoline has pushed back above $4 a gallon nationally, up from $3.87 a week earlier and $3.14 a year ago. This is not a supply scare priced in a futures curve somewhere — it is showing up at the pump.

The curve is doing the talking

Bond markets read energy shocks differently than equity markets do, and this week the difference is stark. The 3-month/10-year Treasury spread has moved out to roughly +87 basis points, and the 30-year yield has pushed up to about 5.16% — a full bear-steepener, where long rates rise faster than short ones. That is the market's way of saying this isn't a growth story. A growth scare flattens or inverts the curve as investors flee to long-duration safety. A bear-steepener says the opposite: investors want compensation for holding money over a longer horizon because they now think inflation, not recession, is the more likely destination.

That suspicion has company. Inflation is already running near 3.7%, well above the Fed's 2% target, and traders have pushed the odds of a rate hike later this year from roughly 12% to about 38% in a single week, according to fixed-income pricing. The Federal Reserve is still expected to hold its benchmark rate steady at its July 29 meeting — its fifth straight hold — but Vice Chair Philip Jefferson and Governor Christopher Waller have both signaled the committee will reconsider that stance if energy-driven inflation doesn't cool. The front end of the curve isn't pricing a pivot. The long end is pricing the opposite.

Winners, losers, and a very clean split

Equity sector performance this week reads almost like a textbook illustration of an oil shock, which is itself notable — real markets rarely cooperate this tidily. Energy (XLE) leads the S&P 500 sectors, up 3.36% on the week. Consumer Discretionary (XLY) is the worst performer, down 5.22%. That is not a rotation between similarly-situated cyclical sectors; it is a direct transmission mechanism. Every extra dollar spent filling a gas tank is a dollar not spent at a restaurant, a car dealership, or an online retailer. Goldman Sachs has already trimmed its 2026 consumption growth forecast to roughly 1.2% year-over-year, down from just over 2% before the latest escalation, citing exactly this squeeze on discretionary budgets.

Communications Services stocks are catching a similar downdraft, another sector whose earnings lean on discretionary attention and ad spending rather than anything energy-adjacent. Utilities, meanwhile, are trading with Energy rather than against it — a sector normally prized for defensiveness is instead benefiting from its correlation to power and fuel costs. The market is not treating this as noise to be arbitraged away; it is treating it as a structural repricing of which businesses face higher input costs and which face higher revenue.

Credit and currency confirm the same story

Two more corners of the market are echoing the same signal, and both would look strange without the oil context. Investment-grade credit, tracked by LQD, has fallen 1.24% over five days — nearly triple the 0.53% decline in high-yield, tracked by HYG. That gap is unusual, because high-yield is supposed to be the riskier asset. What's happening instead is a duration story, not a credit-quality one: LQD carries roughly eight to nine years of duration against HYG's three to four, so when long rates back up on inflation fear, investment-grade bonds absorb the price damage first even though their underlying credit quality hasn't budged. It is the bond-market equivalent of Consumer Discretionary underperforming Energy — the instrument with the longer horizon takes the hit.

The dollar tells the same tale from a third angle. After roughly a month of unusual calm, the dollar index has broken higher, touching around 101.3 — its best level in about three weeks and within striking distance of a 15-month high set in late June. Traditionally, an oil shock centered on Gulf supply would be expected to weigh on the currency of the world's largest oil importer. Instead the dollar is rallying alongside crude, which suggests traders are treating this less as a terms-of-trade shock and more as a global risk-off, inflation-hedge event — the same instinct that is lifting long-end yields and hurting long-duration credit simultaneously.

What would confirm it — or break it

The honest caveat is that one month of a single commodity's price action, however sharp, is not yet a regime. Every piece of evidence here — the steepening curve, the sector split, the credit gap, the dollar bid — is consistent with a single underlying cause, which is unusually clean for markets that normally have multiple things happening at once. But clean correlation during an active war is also exactly what you'd expect from a shock that fades the moment the shooting stops. J.P. Morgan's own year-end oil forecast, penciled in before the latest escalation, still sits near $78 a barrel, well below where crude trades today, on the view that a de-escalation would send cyclicals and consumer names right back up.

The next real test arrives in two stages. The Fed's July 29 decision will show whether policymakers treat this as transitory or something requiring a rate response — a hold with hawkish language would validate the bear-steepener; a hold that shrugs off oil would undercut it. And any credible sign of a ceasefire or a reopened Strait of Hormuz would be the fastest way to find out whether this was a regime change or just a very loud four weeks.
