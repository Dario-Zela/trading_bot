The front end isn't buying a pivot anytime soon

Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh, whose FOMC holds its July policy meeting this week — Federal Reserve (official portrait, 2026) / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The 3-month bill at 3.805% shows the front end still pricing a patient Fed, even as the long end blows out on oil-driven inflation fear.

The long end of the Treasury market has been in open revolt for a week. The short end has barely shrugged.

The 3-month bill sat at 3.805% on Friday — a hair above the top of the Federal Reserve's 3.50%-3.75% target range, and almost exactly where it has traded for weeks. Compare that with the rest of the curve: the 5-year at 4.426%, the 30-year at 5.162%, and a 3-month-to-10-year spread that has blown out to +87.4 basis points. Every bit of that steepening is happening beyond the front end. The market that prices what the Fed does next has not moved. The market that prices what inflation does for the next three decades has moved a great deal.

That split is itself the signal. Bills, unlike long bonds, are a direct read on near-term policy — money-market funds and short-duration buyers price them off where the Fed actually is and where it's about to be, not off term premium or growth stories. A 3-month bill bought this week matures in late October, spanning both this week's FOMC meeting and the September one, and it is still pricing a Fed that holds. That's a market telling Chair Kevin Warsh it doesn't expect a surprise.

A hold that isn't really in question

The Fed's committee meets Tuesday and Wednesday, with a decision due July 29 at 2 p.m. and Warsh's press conference to follow. Futures markets have priced a hold at close to certainty for months, and nothing in the bill market this week suggests that's changed, even as oil's surge toward $100 a barrel — Brent gained roughly 7% in a single session last week on Red Sea tanker attacks and renewed Iran tension — has been read elsewhere in the curve as an inflation problem. TD Securities' Gennadiy Goldberg has described the broader Treasury selloff as a rapid repricing of Fed expectations tied to oil; his colleague Prashant Newnaha has gone further, calling a sustained high oil price potentially "the nail in the coffin for bonds." None of that has shown up yet in a 3-month bill.

Further out the curve, fed funds futures tied to September have started drifting toward pricing some hike risk rather than cut risk — a notable shift from a week earlier. But that's a thin, futures-market signal next to the flat conviction sitting in cash bills, and the gap between the two is worth watching rather than trusting outright.

The question this sets up for next week is which side gives first: does the front end eventually catch up to the long end's inflation fright, or does the long end retrace once the oil spike cools and the term-premium story loses its urgency. Warsh's press conference Wednesday, and any language he uses — or pointedly avoids — about the long end, is the first place to look for an answer.
