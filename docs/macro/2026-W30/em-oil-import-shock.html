Emerging markets face a fresh oil-import bill

In one sentence
A firmer dollar and a 25% crude rally are squeezing oil-importing emerging markets from both sides at once, while exporters split into clear winners and surprising losers.

For economies that buy their oil rather than pump it, the last two weeks have delivered a shock from both directions at once. The dollar, quiet for most of July, jumped 0.71% this week after a month of near-total calm — a 20-day move of just 0.04% gave way to a sharp break higher. At the same time, crude has climbed roughly 25% over 20 days, with Brent pushing back above $100 a barrel. For an oil producer, a stronger dollar and dearer crude can offset each other. For an oil importer, they compound.

A bill paid twice

The mechanism is simple enough to explain without jargon. Oil is priced and settled in dollars, so when crude rises, importing countries need more dollars to cover the same barrels. When the dollar also strengthens against local currencies, each of those dollars costs more in rupees, lira or rand. India, the world's third-largest oil importer, has felt both legs directly: the rupee has been trading near record lows around 96-97 to the dollar this month, a level it first touched in May, as Brent's move back above $100 widened the trade deficit and left traders watching how much intervention the Reserve Bank of India is willing to spend defending the currency.

S&P Global and Allianz Trade have both flagged a similar list of exposed economies — countries running fiscal deficits, current-account gaps and heavy energy-import dependence simultaneously. Egypt, Pakistan, Sri Lanka, Jordan and Morocco sit in the most exposed tier; Turkey, India, the Philippines, Thailand and Chile carry moderate but rising risk. The Egyptian pound has already depreciated more than 10% as one of the sharpest currency casualties, according to Allianz Trade's tracking of the current shock. Base metals, up 2.19% over 20 days, and agricultural commodities, up 4.90%, are layering a second round of imported inflation on top of energy — fertiliser and food costs move with the same dollar-denominated logic as crude.

Not every exporter is spared

The flip side of the trade looks cleaner than it is. Gulf producers are the straightforward winners: at Brent near $108, Saudi Arabia's budget would swing to a surplus in the $42-48 billion range, its largest since 2014, easing pressure on the sovereign wealth funds that have been drawing down reserves. But Latin America's oil exporters don't uniformly benefit. Mexico pumps crude but lacks refining capacity, and ran an oil trade deficit of roughly $25 billion in 2025 because it imports the refined products — gasoline, diesel — that its own refineries can't supply. Ecuador faces a similar mismatch. Crude export revenue and refined-product import bills move on different tracks, and a country can be a net oil exporter while still bleeding dollars at the pump.

For central banks in the exposed tier, the dilemma is now familiar: growth argues for rate cuts, energy-driven inflation and currency fragility argue against them. The next test comes with July trade-balance and inflation prints across South and Southeast Asia, alongside any further RBI intervention data — the numbers that will show whether this is a two-week spike or the start of a sustained squeeze on external accounts.
