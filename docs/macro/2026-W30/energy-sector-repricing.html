SECTORS · ENERGY · By I. Aresti · 2026-07-26 · 4 min read

Energy's quiet month is now impossible to ignore

A pumpjack in the oil fields near Midland, Texas — energy is the only S&P sector leading on both the weekly and monthly horizon. — Eric Kounce (TexasRaiser) / Wikimedia Commons, public domain
In one sentence
XLE is the only sector leading on both the 5-day and 20-day windows, and utilities' parallel strength suggests markets are hedging the same crude shock two ways.

There is a version of this rally that reads as noise — a geopolitical scare, a few weeks of headline risk, the kind of thing that shows up in commodity markets every year or two and fades before the next earnings season. Energy stocks have had plenty of those false starts since 2022.

This is not shaping up to be one of them. The Energy Select Sector SPDR Fund (XLE) is up 3.36% over the past five trading days and 10.22% over the past twenty — the only S&P 500 sector posting gains on both the short and medium window at once. Everything else in the market is either fading on one horizon or the other. Energy is doing it on both, which is the statistical signature of a trend, not a bounce.

In one sentence: XLE's 10% one-month gain, compounding with a 3.4% weekly pop, looks less like a rotation trade and more like the equity market catching up to a genuine crude repricing.

The crude math underneath the equity move

The fundamental driver is not subtle. The United States Oil Fund (USO) is up 25.05% over the same twenty-day stretch, and the price action behind it has been a straight line up since a Middle East ceasefire collapsed on July 8. WTI crude, trading in the high $70s and low $80s as recently as late June, briefly touched $100 a barrel this week for the first time in nearly two months before settling back near $92. Brent sat close to $94. Diesel markets have moved in sympathy — ICE Gasoil has held above $1,100 a tonne — which matters because it means refiners are not just riding a crude tailwind, they are capturing wider cracking margins on top of it.

That combination — a geopolitical risk premium in crude plus genuine tightness in refined products — is why the energy trade has legs that a pure headline spike would not have. It also arrives against a backdrop that should, in theory, be capping prices rather than igniting them: OPEC+ has spent months unwinding its 2023 production restraint, adding roughly 800,000 barrels a day of quota since April in a stated bid to reclaim market share, and forecasters as recently as this spring were penciling in a supply glut and Brent in the low $60s for 2026. The rally is overriding that oversupply narrative, not confirming it, which is precisely why it reads as a repricing of risk rather than a rotation into cheap energy names.

Venezuela adds a smaller but real wrinkle. Independent producers have been moving faster than the majors to strike deals for access to the country's reserves following Nicolás Maduro's fall from power earlier this year, but the realistic upside — analysts peg incremental Venezuelan output at 250,000–300,000 barrels a day — is modest against global demand growth running at 750,000 barrels to 1.25 million barrels a day. It is a storyline that keeps traders' attention on the region without materially changing the supply math.

The majors are the clearest expression of the move. ExxonMobil is up 31% year-to-date, Chevron close behind at 29%, and both report second-quarter earnings on July 31 — consensus has Exxon's EPS up 136.6% year-on-year and Chevron's up more than 227%, numbers that would have looked implausible in a $60 crude world.

Utilities are quietly telling the same story

The part of this that elevates it from a single-sector story to a market signal is what is happening one column over. The Utilities Select Sector SPDR Fund (XLU) gained 2.48% over the past five days — not energy's pace, but a clear second-place finish, and utilities do not typically move alongside oil and gas stocks unless something broader is going on.

Utilities carry a beta near 0.5 and have outpaced both inflation and the 10-year Treasury in 13 of the past 20 years — the closest thing equities offer to a bond proxy with an inflation kicker. Money moving into that sector at the same time energy is running higher on a crude shock looks like investors positioning for the same outcome from two different angles: energy for direct exposure to higher oil and gas prices, utilities as a hedge against what a sustained crude shock does to the broader inflation picture. AI-driven power-demand optimism is doing some of the work in utilities too, but the timing of this week's move — arriving in lockstep with the oil breakout rather than on its own schedule — points to the inflation-hedge explanation carrying real weight.

What to watch

Exxon and Chevron's earnings on July 31 will be the first hard test of whether the crude move is translating into the cash-flow numbers the rally already assumes. Beyond that, the OPEC+ supply overhang has not gone away — it has simply been outrun by geopolitical risk — so the next monthly production decision, and whether the Middle East truce shows any sign of reforming, will determine whether energy's two-horizon lead extends into a third, or whether this settles back into the pattern of false starts the sector has produced since 2022.
