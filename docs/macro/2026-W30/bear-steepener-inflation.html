The long end is pricing an inflation problem, not a growth one

The U.S. Treasury Building in Washington, D.C. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A bear steepener — 30Y at 5.16%, front-end bills flat near 3.8% — shows bond investors pricing an oil-driven inflation risk, not a growth scare.

The gap between short-term and long-term borrowing costs in the U.S. Treasury market has widened to its most since early last year — but the way it happened matters more than the number itself. The 3-month bill sits at 3.805%, barely different from where it was a month ago. The 10-year note has climbed to 4.679%. The 30-year bond, the part of the curve investors use to bet on the economy's distant future, is at 5.162% — the highest point on the whole curve and the highest it has been since before the 2025 easing cycle began.

That spread — 3-month bills to 10-year notes — now stands at roughly 87 basis points. A steepening curve is often read as good news: it can mean the market expects the Federal Reserve to cut rates and growth to reaccelerate. This is not that. When the front end refuses to move and the back end does all the climbing, bond traders call it a bear steepener, and it typically means something less comfortable — the market is demanding more compensation to hold long-duration debt, not because it fears recession, but because it fears inflation won't behave.

The front end isn't going anywhere

Start with what hasn't moved. The 3-month bill yield tracks where investors expect the Fed's policy rate to sit over the next quarter, and it has been anchored near 3.8%–3.9% for weeks. The Federal Open Market Committee, under Chair Kevin Warsh, meets again on July 29 and is widely expected to hold its benchmark rate at 3.5%–3.75% for a fifth consecutive meeting. June's FOMC statement described inflation as "elevated relative to the Committee's 2 percent goal, in part reflecting supply shocks that have driven price increases in certain sectors, including energy" — language that reads, in hindsight, like a preview of the month that followed.

The labor market gives Warsh little cover to move early in either direction. Jobless claims for the week ended July 18 came in at 187,000, well below the 212,000 economists had penciled in — a number that argues against the kind of growth scare that normally drags the whole curve lower. Options markets have pushed the odds of a rate hike later this year to roughly 38%, up from 12% just a week earlier, which is itself a tell: rising hike odds are not what a market pricing recession looks like.

What's dragging the long end up

The proximate cause sits in the Gulf. Iran's effective closure of the Strait of Hormuz since early March, followed by a collapsed ceasefire and renewed U.S. strikes in mid-July, has choked a waterway that normally carries close to a fifth of the world's seaborne oil. Brent crude has traded above $100 a barrel this month, and USO — the most widely held oil-tracking fund — posted a roughly 25% gain over the trailing month, one of its sharpest moves since the pandemic-era price collapse and rebound.

"The economy may be heating up today, but the path ahead for the employment markets could still be rockier with the escalation of the war in the Middle East causing a u-turn in energy prices virtually overnight this week," Chris Rupkey, chief economist at FWDBONDS, said as the 10-year yield pushed above 4.7% in the days after the strikes resumed. The 10-year yield touched its highest level since January 2025 in the same session, with Treasury desks pointing squarely at the oil move as the trigger.

What's notable is what hasn't moved much alongside it. The 10-year breakeven inflation rate — the gap between nominal Treasurys and inflation-protected TIPS, and the market's cleanest read on expected inflation — sits at 2.28%, only modestly above where it has spent most of the year. That is a meaningful distinction. If investors genuinely expected a new inflationary regime, breakevens would be racing higher alongside yields. Instead, the move looks concentrated in what bond desks call term premium — the extra yield investors demand simply for the uncertainty of holding 10- or 30-year paper through a period when an energy shock could still cascade into stickier prices, a delayed Fed response, or both. Underlying inflation expectations have barely budged; the compensation for the risk of being wrong about them has gone up sharply.

Why the shape matters more than the level

A bull steepener — front-end yields falling on rate-cut bets while the long end holds — is the curve of a market getting comfortable. A bear steepener with the front end pinned and the long end pushing to multi-year highs is the curve of a market getting nervous about a different thing entirely: not whether the economy slows, but whether the Fed can bring inflation back to target without the energy shock forcing its hand later than currently priced. The 30-year, as the maturity least tied to near-term Fed policy and most exposed to long-run inflation and fiscal risk, is doing almost all of the work — which is exactly what a term-premium repricing looks like on a chart.

The next data points worth watching arrive close together: the July 29 FOMC decision, where any hawkish shift in the Committee's language on energy-driven inflation would validate the long end's move, and whatever comes out of the Oman-brokered talks over managing traffic through the Strait of Hormuz, which is the one lever that could take the oil premium — and with it, some of the term premium — back out of the curve almost as quickly as it went in.
