The dollar's oil-shock bid

US dollar banknotes — the greenback posted its sharpest weekly gain since February as oil-driven inflation fears lifted Treasury yields. — US Federal Reserve / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
A month of dead-calm trading in the dollar broke this week as crude's 25% surge lifted both US real yields and demand for the dollar as a safe haven against oil-importer currencies.

For twenty straight trading days, the dollar did almost nothing. The ICE Dollar Index — a basket measure of the greenback against the euro, yen, pound and four other major currencies — drifted in a band so narrow that FX desks had taken to calling it dead money, up a grand total of 0.04% since late June. Then, in the space of five sessions, it moved more than it had in the entire month prior. The index closed the week at 101.47, up 0.71%, its sharpest weekly gain since February.

The timing is not a coincidence. Crude oil has surged roughly 25% over the past month, Brent trading near $97 a barrel after briefly topping $102, as a US-Iran military confrontation has put the Strait of Hormuz — the transit point for roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil — under the kind of stress traders have not priced since the tanker-war scares of the 1980s. The question the dollar's sudden pop raises is whether this is a durable regime shift in the currency or a one-week reaction to a geopolitical shock that could unwind as quickly as it arrived.

Two stories, one chart

There are two competing explanations for why a barrel of oil trading in Hormuz should move a currency index, and both have shown up in the data this week.

The first is a rates story. Oil at $95-plus has revived inflation fears that had been dormant for months, and the long end of the Treasury curve has done the repricing: the 10-year yield pushed to around 4.68-4.7%, its highest level since January 2025, while the 2-year has lagged, steepening the curve. When long-dated real yields — the return investors demand above expected inflation — rise faster than they do abroad, the dollar tends to benefit even if the Federal Reserve's policy rate hasn't moved an inch. That is the mechanism cross-linked in this edition's rates coverage: a bear steepener driven by an inflation scare, not a growth scare, with the front end of the curve still refusing to price a near-term Fed pivot.

The second explanation is broader and more geopolitical: a risk and terms-of-trade bid. The dollar is one of the few genuine safe havens left when a Gulf shipping lane is under fire, and it draws flows on that basis alone, independent of what Treasury yields are doing. Layered onto that is a terms-of-trade effect that hits oil-importing economies asymmetrically. Currencies such as the Indian rupee, the Thai baht and the Turkish lira have all been pushed toward record or multi-year lows this month, not because their central banks are behind the curve on inflation, but because higher oil import bills widen current-account deficits and force local corporates to buy dollars to pay energy suppliers. Thailand imports something like 85% of its crude; India imports the large majority of its own. That dynamic doesn't need a single basis point of Fed repricing to push the dollar index up — it only needs the rest of the world's currencies to weaken against it.

In practice the two stories are reinforcing rather than competing. Higher oil pushes up US breakevens and term premium, which lifts real yields and pulls in dollar demand; the same oil spike simultaneously drags down the currencies of countries that have to pay for it. Both mechanisms point in the same direction, which is part of why the move showed up so cleanly across a full week rather than in a single session's noise.

What a flat month was hiding

The more interesting fact may be the twenty days of calm that preceded this. A dollar index sitting still for a month usually means opposing forces are in balance — in this case, likely a market that had priced in a soft landing, a patient Fed, and stable energy prices all at once. The oil shock broke exactly one leg of that stool. It did so abruptly, via an escalation that markets had treated as a tail risk rather than a base case; oil's month-long climb, chronicled elsewhere in this edition as a stealth repricing of the broader tape, only became undeniable to FX markets once Hormuz itself was in the headlines.

That abruptness cuts against reading too much directional conviction into a single week. Currency moves that track a geopolitical risk premium tend to be as reversible as the risk itself — if a diplomatic off-ramp materializes and tanker traffic through Hormuz normalizes, both the oil bid and the dollar bid it has fed could unwind together, and possibly just as fast as they appeared, an outcome one skeptical read of this rally has already flagged directly.

What to watch

The cleanest test of which story dominates is whether the dollar's strength survives an oil pullback. If crude gives back a meaningful share of its 25% gain and the DXY holds most of its 0.71% weekly advance, that would point to the real-yield channel — a Fed-and-Treasury-market story with more staying power — having taken over as the primary driver. If the dollar retraces in lockstep with oil, this week looks more like a geopolitical risk premium than a genuine turn.

The near-term catalysts are concrete: any further escalation or de-escalation around the Strait of Hormuz, the next Treasury refunding data on foreign demand at the long end, and incoming US CPI, which will show how much of the oil move has already fed into headline inflation. Until one of those breaks decisively, both explanations for the dollar's oil-shock bid remain live at once.
