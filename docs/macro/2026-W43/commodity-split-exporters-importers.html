Oil up, metals and gas down: what the commodity split means for exporters and importers

In one sentence
Oil rose while gas and precious metals fell, favouring crude exporters over gas and metals producers, though this is inferred from commodity prices with no regional market data.

Commodities pulled in opposite directions this week, and the split sorts economies by what they sell. Oil rose while natural gas and precious metals fell, which helps crude exporters and strains importers. Producers of gas and metals are on the wrong side of the move.

One caveat first. This piece infers from commodity prices. There is no regional market data this week: no emerging-market equity, currency or sovereign-spread readings. What follows is a read-through from the price of the raw materials, not a report of how regional markets traded.

The price moves

The oil fund USO rose 4.8% on the week. The natural gas fund UNG fell 10.1%. The broad agriculture fund DBA slipped 1.3%, a small move beside the other two. Over 20 days, the silver fund SLV is down 16.2% and the gold fund GLD is down 6.8%.

These are exchange-traded proxies, not spot prices, and they can drift from the underlying commodities, particularly in futures-based funds like UNG and USO. The direction is clear enough for a first read.

Who gains, who pays

The oil exporter's case is the simple one. A higher crude price lifts export receipts and, for economies where oil supplies much of the government budget, fiscal revenue. Importers face the mirror image: a bigger fuel bill, pressure on current-account balances and, where fuel is subsidised, pressure on public finances. How much of this week's 4.8% passes through depends on whether it lasts, which one week cannot show.

The weaker side is wider than it first looks. A 10.1% weekly drop in natural gas cuts into gas-exporting economies and gas-linked producers. UNG tracks US natural gas futures, so the cleanest read is for US-priced gas. Other regions' gas contracts may be priced differently, and some are tied to oil, which would soften the effect for them.

The metals decline is a precious-metals decline, so it is not a verdict on copper or the other industrial metals that drive many mining economies. Gold and silver sellers are the more direct losers. A 16.2% fall in silver over 20 days is large by any standard.

The slight fall in agriculture matters mainly at the margin. Food importers get small relief, and grain and soft-commodity exporters see slightly weaker receipts.

The dollar complicates it

The dollar index stands at 101.81, up 0.83% over 20 days. It is a modest rise, but it matters for emerging-market borrowers with dollar-denominated debt, because their servicing costs rise when local currencies weaken against it. Commodity exporters whose revenues are in dollars are partly insulated. Importers with dollar debt face the squeeze twice: dearer fuel and a stronger currency to repay in.

The pattern is therefore not a clean win for every oil seller or loss for every importer. An exporter that also sells gas or precious metals nets the moves against each other. An importer with little dollar debt is better placed than one carrying a lot.

What to watch

The test is persistence. If crude holds its gain through the next few weeks while the dollar index keeps firming, the divide between energy exporters and everyone else will widen. Regional equity, currency and spread data, absent this week, would show whether markets are already pricing it.
