Falling gold and rising yields: reading the metals sell-off through real rates

In one sentence
Gold fell 4% and silver 5.4% in a week as long yields held near cycle highs and the dollar stayed flat, pointing to rates, though no TIPS or breakeven data confirm it.

Gold and silver have had a bad week, and the usual explanations are not doing much of the work. The SPDR Gold Shares ETF (GLD) closed at 366.61, down 4.03% over five days and 6.81% over 20. The iShares Silver Trust (SLV) closed at 48.72, down 5.42% on the week and 16.20% on the month. Over the same stretch long-term Treasury yields stayed close to cycle highs, with the 10-year at 4.99%.

What the data show is a coincidence in time: metals fell while yields held high. The rest is inference.

What the dollar does not explain

The obvious suspect for a metals sell-off is a stronger dollar, since gold and silver are priced in it. The ICE Dollar Index (DXY) closed at 101.81, a change of -0.03% on the week. Over 20 days it is up 0.83%.

That is a firming trend, but a modest one. A dollar that was flat this week cannot account for gold losing four percent in the same days. Over the month, a 0.83% gain in the dollar sits against a 6.81% fall in gold and a 16.20% fall in silver. The currency may have contributed at the margin, but it does not carry the move. (The dollar's own flat-week, firm-month pattern is covered separately in this edition.)

The tape also does not look like a flight from fear. Credit has stayed calm, and a fear-driven episode would more typically pull money into gold, not out of it.

The real-rate reading

Gold pays no coupon. When the return on safe, inflation-adjusted bonds rises, the cost of holding it rises with it, and the metal tends to lose ground. Silver is more volatile than gold and also has an industrial side, so it often moves further in the same direction. A 16.2% monthly decline against gold's 6.8% fits that pattern, though the gap is not proof of anything on its own.

The argument, then, is that the metals are reacting to the price of money. A 10-year yield at 4.99% and a curve sitting 96 basis points above bills describe a bond market demanding a substantial return for lending long. Against that backdrop, an asset with no yield is harder to defend.

What this snapshot cannot show

The attribution to real rates is an inference, not an observation. This snapshot contains no data on Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS) yields or on breakeven inflation rates, which are the measures that separate real yields from the inflation compensation inside nominal ones.

That matters. If nominal yields held high because breakevens rose, real yields may not have moved much, and the real-rate explanation would weaken. If breakevens were steady or falling, the case strengthens. Positioning and fund flows are also absent. A 16% silver decline in 20 days could reflect forced selling as much as a repricing of rates.

The evidence that does exist points one way: the dollar is not the main driver, and credit shows no stress. By elimination, rates are the most plausible source of pressure. Elimination is weaker than measurement.

What to watch

The cleanest test is the next move in the long end. If 10-year yields ease from 4.99% and the metals do not recover, the rates story loses force. If yields climb again and gold and silver extend their decline, it gains. TIPS yields and breakevens would settle the question directly, and adding them to the data set is the first fix.

The joint risk that oil and long yields rise together, discussed elsewhere in this edition, would put further pressure on the same inputs.
