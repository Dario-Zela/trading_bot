CREDIT · IG VS HY · By T. Larch · 2026-10-25 · 4 min read

Credit stays calm while rates and equities rotate

In one sentence
High yield (+0.25%) and investment grade (+0.44%) both gained on the week, a small tilt toward quality rather than a flight, though without spread data the signal is limited.

Both halves of the corporate bond market rose over the past week. High yield rose a little less than investment grade, and that is all the tape shows. It is a modest tilt toward quality. It is not a flight from risk, and it is not the start of a credit scare.

The iShares iBoxx High Yield Corporate Bond ETF (HYG) returned +0.25% over five trading days. The investment-grade counterpart, LQD, returned +0.44%. Subtract one from the other and high yield trails by 0.19 percentage points, or about 19 basis points. In a week when the equity market rotated into energy and out of long-duration tech, and the 10-year Treasury yield sat at 4.99%, that is a small gap.

What the numbers do and do not say

The figures above are ETF total returns. They are not credit spreads. This desk has no option-adjusted spread (OAS) data for either market, so it cannot say whether the premium over Treasuries widened, narrowed or stayed put. That matters because the two series can diverge. Investment-grade funds carry long duration, so when Treasury yields fall they gain more than their spread alone would suggest. High-yield funds are shorter and more tied to equity-like risk appetite. A 19bp return gap could come from spreads, from rates, or from both.

What the returns can support is narrower. Neither fund fell, so nobody was selling credit indiscriminately. The higher-quality fund did better, so the marginal buyer leaned a little toward safety. If high yield had been sold hard while investment grade held steady, that would look like stress. A gap this small, with both funds positive, looks like ordinary rebalancing.

The rates backdrop

The 10-year at 4.99% is the benchmark for what investment-grade issuers pay to borrow, and it sits at a level that was once treated as a ceiling. The curve is steep, with the 10-year 96bp above bills. The same move in long yields has weighed on duration-heavy equities, and the sector rotation this week reflected that. Credit has so far absorbed it. Part of the explanation is that corporate bonds already pay a high absolute yield, and income buyers have a reason to stay in them. A fund yielding more than a 5% Treasury plus a spread cushion is not easy to dislodge from portfolios on a single week of mild rotation.

The tape is calm, but that is not a verdict on the next move.

The sceptic's case

The argument for caution is about timing, not this week. A 5% long end raises the cost of refinancing. Companies that issued cheaply in earlier years will eventually have to roll maturing debt at much higher coupons. That pressure builds slowly through interest expense and thinner coverage ratios, and it falls hardest on the weakest balance sheets, which is to say the high-yield universe. Markets can ignore that arithmetic for a long time, then price it all at once.

Nothing in this week's data shows that process under way. High yield rose. The investment-grade lead is a few hundredths of a percent, well inside what a single week of noise can produce. The sceptic is describing a risk, not an observation, and the evidence presented here cannot confirm or dismiss it.

The energy-cost angle is related. A joint move in oil and long yields, rather than either alone, is the combination most likely to squeeze borrowers who are both rate-sensitive and cost-exposed. Credit has not yet registered that, either.

What would change the read

The falsifier is specific. If high yield begins to underperform investment grade by a wide margin while yields stay at or above current levels, the calm reading fails. That would mean credit investors are charging more for default risk at the same time as the risk-free rate holds high, which is the pattern a refinancing squeeze would produce. A 19bp weekly gap is not that. A gap several times larger and persisting for more than a week or two would be.

The cleaner test needs data this desk does not yet have. Actual OAS levels for both indices would show whether spreads are moving independently of Treasury yields, and would separate a credit signal from a rates signal. Until those are in hand, the honest summary is that the return evidence leans slightly toward quality and gives no sign of distress. The next prompts for a re-read are another week of HYG-versus-LQD returns with the 10-year still near 5%, and any move in the long end that pushes the 10-year decisively past it.
