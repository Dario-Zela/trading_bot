Communications and tech slide as long yields weigh on duration

In one sentence
XLC fell 4.4% on the week, far more than any other sector, while XLK was nearly flat; rates explain the monthly slide in both, but not communications' latest drop.

Communications services was the weakest part of the US equity market this week by a wide margin. The sector fund XLC fell 4.36% over five days, more than twice the next-worst sector. The technology fund XLK, usually the other half of the rates-sensitive growth trade, slipped only 0.13%. That gap is the most useful clue in a week when long-dated Treasury yields, with the 10-year near 5%, tempted everyone to blame duration for everything.

A weekly drop that outruns the rate story

The straightforward reading is that higher long yields cut the present value of distant earnings, and growth sectors pay for it. That explanation fits the 20-day figures. XLC is down 6.60% over that stretch and XLK is down 6.64%, nearly identical, which suggests both have been marked down by the same force for a month.

It does not fit the week. If rates had driven the latest leg, XLK would have fallen with XLC. It did not. XLK's -0.13% means technology mostly held its ground over five days while carrying a large monthly loss. The rate-driven damage in tech looks like it came earlier and has since stabilised.

So the roughly 4.4% fall in communications cannot be mostly about rates. One way to size it: if XLK's flat week is the rates-only baseline for long-duration growth, then nearly all of XLC's weekly loss is something else. That is a rough calculation, not a clean decomposition. The two sectors differ in composition and in how much their earnings depend on advertising and subscriptions rather than on capital spending.

The likely source of the extra weakness is stock-specific news. Communications is a concentrated sector, with a few very large index members carrying most of the weight, and the weekly reporting calendar matters. A sharp move in one or two heavyweights after results or guidance can swing a sector fund by several percentage points without any change in the rate backdrop. The data here cannot name the stock responsible. It shows only that the pattern, a large sector-specific drop alongside a flat sister sector, is what such an event typically looks like. Confirming it would mean looking at the largest holdings' individual moves.

Industrials and the defensive bid

Industrials were the other soft spot. XLI lost 1.87% on the week, a sizeable move but well short of communications. A sector that depends on capital spending and economic activity is a natural casualty when long borrowing costs sit near 5% and an energy-cost shock is also in the picture. It is the sort of loss rates and input costs can explain without any company-level story.

On the other side of the ledger, the defensive sectors gained. Health care (XLV) rose 1.45%, utilities (XLU) 0.83% and consumer staples (XLP) 0.73%. The combination is a recognisable rotation: money leaving growth and cyclicals for steadier earnings. Utilities are the awkward member of the group, since they are also bond proxies that normally suffer when yields climb. Their gain suggests investors were reaching for stability rather than trading strictly off the rate signal. That is more consistent with caution about growth than with a pure duration trade.

What the split tells and what it doesn't

Taken together, the week reads as two overlapping stories. The first is a month-long, rate-driven de-rating of long-duration equities, visible in the near-identical 20-day losses in XLC and XLK. The second is a one-week event concentrated in communications, plus a more ordinary cyclical weakness in industrials, with defensives absorbing the flows.

The caution is that a single week of sector returns is thin evidence for either story. A sector fund's weekly move can be dominated by a handful of names, and the 20-day figures overlap the five-day ones, so the two windows are not independent.

The next checks are straightforward. The remaining earnings from large communications and technology companies will show whether the communications drop was a one-company episode or a broader reassessment of the sector. The next moves in the 10-year yield will show whether technology's steadiness holds if rates push higher, or whether the flat week was a pause in a longer slide.
