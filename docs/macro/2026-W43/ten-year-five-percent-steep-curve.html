The 10-year sits at 4.99% as the curve holds a steep 96bp over bills

In one sentence
With the 3M at 4.03% and the 10Y at 4.99%, the curve's 96bp steepness sits mostly in the front-to-belly; a close above 5% would test duration-heavy sectors.

The Treasury curve is steep, and the 10-year yield sits one basis point under 5%. As of Oct. 23, three-month bills yielded 4.03% and the 10-year note 4.99%, a gap of 95.6 basis points. The 30-year bond was at 5.32%. The question for the rates desk is whether 5% on the 10-year acts as a ceiling or becomes a floor.

Where the steepness sits

The headline spread is 3M-10Y, but the curve is not evenly steep along its length. Using the Oct. 23 readings of 4.03% (3M), 4.80% (5Y), 4.99% (10Y) and 5.32% (30Y), the segments break down as follows:

  • 3M to 5Y: about 77bp, roughly four-fifths of the 3M-10Y spread.
  • 5Y to 10Y: about 19bp.
  • 10Y to 30Y: about 33bp.

Most of the steepening is therefore in the front-to-belly section. Moving from bills to five-year paper is where the curve pays the most. Past the five-year point it flattens into a gentle slope, and the 5Y-30Y spread is only about 51bp, spread over twenty-five years of extra maturity.

That shape matters for how the 5% level is read. A curve that kept climbing steeply past ten years would suggest the long end was running away. This one climbs quickly and then flattens, so the 10-year is not an outlier. It sits on a plateau that begins around the five-year point.

What a close above 5% would mean

The 10-year sits about one basis point from the round number, so the line is a psychological threshold more than an analytical one. Round numbers still matter in markets. Mortgage pricing, corporate benchmarks and model-driven allocations are often tied to them.

The sectors most exposed are the duration-sensitive ones: long-dated growth equities, communications and technology, and rate-sensitive real-asset and utility-style income stocks. These are firms whose value rests on cash flows far in the future, so a higher discount rate cuts their present value most. A related piece in this edition (tech-comms-duration-pressure) covers how communications and technology have already slid as long yields weighed on duration.

A single close above 5% would be a marker, not a regime change. Whether it holds depends on a few things:

  • Persistence. One close through the level is weak evidence. Several consecutive closes, with the 10-year holding above rather than retesting from below, would be a stronger one.
  • Long-end confirmation. With the 30-year already at 5.32%, a 10-year break would put the whole back half of the curve above 5%. A 30-year that stalls while the 10-year pushes through would suggest weaker conviction.
  • The front end staying put. If the 3M stays near 4.03% while the 10-year rises, the curve steepens further. If bills reprice upward in step, the move is more of a parallel shift and the spread would hold near 96bp.
  • Cross-asset behaviour. The metals sell-off has been read through real rates (metals-selloff-real-rates), and the oil move bears on the inflation side (oil-and-long-yields-tail). A yield rise that arrives alongside rising oil would look different from one that does not.

What the data can and cannot say

The shape is consistent with a rising term premium, meaning the extra yield investors demand for holding longer-dated debt instead of rolling bills. A curve that is steep across the belly, with a 96bp gap over bills, fits that reading.

The data does not identify the cause. A steep curve like this could reflect expectations of faster growth, higher inflation, heavier Treasury supply, weaker demand for duration, or some mix of them. The yields alone cannot separate these. Separating them would require a decomposition of the 10-year into expected short rates and term premium, plus inflation-breakeven and auction data. None of that is in the readings used here.

The cross-asset picture adds some context but not an answer. Credit has stayed calm (credit-calm-quality-tilt). An energy-led, defensive equity tape is sending a different signal from the long end (energy-led-defensive-tape). Neither fixes the cause of the term-premium reading, and an orderly credit market suggests the move is not yet being treated as stress.

What to watch

The first test is whether the 10-year closes above 4.99% and then 5.00%, and whether it holds there over several sessions. The second is the 10Y-30Y gap. If it stays near 33bp, the long end is moving as a block with the belly. If it widens, the move is concentrating at the far end. The third is the 3M-10Y spread: a climb past 100bp would mark a new stage in the steepening, while a drift back below 90bp would suggest the belly is retracing. The next Treasury yield-curve prints will show which of these is under way.
