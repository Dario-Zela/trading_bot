Financials flat while the curve flattens — the carry arithmetic is worsening

The New York Stock Exchange on Wall Street — the XLF's 20-day rally has stalled as the yield curve flattens and energy-sector credit stress builds. — Luigi Novi / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
XLF's 0.0% week after a 4.5% 20-day run signals that bull flattening, HY credit stress, and a 27% crude slide are collectively eroding the bank carry trade.

The XLF flatline is the kind of signal that looks like nothing and means a great deal. After gaining 4.49% over the prior 20 trading days, the Financial Select Sector SPDR ETF returned exactly 0.0% this week — a full stop on a move that had been pricing in the steeper curve of earlier months. The sector's 20-day run reflected a period when the carry arithmetic for banks looked manageable. That window may be closing.

The problem is that the curve has moved on. Bull flattening — the long end rallying while the short end stays anchored — compresses the spread between what banks fund at and what they lend at. The 5-year/3-month Treasury spread now sits at roughly 46.7 basis points, well off the wider levels that supported the sector's prior bid. If the 3m10y spread compresses further toward 50 basis points — it is already in that vicinity — the carry mathematics deteriorate rapidly. Banks earn their living in the difference between short-duration funding costs and long-duration loan yields; a flatter curve shrinks that margin by accounting identity, not sentiment.

The St. Louis Fed's own analysis, published this month, noted that loan yields at U.S. banks have fallen from a cycle peak of 7.13% in late 2024 to 6.51% in the most recent quarter. That directional shift predates this week's flattening; the curve move accelerates pressure that was already building rather than creating it from scratch.

Two More Pressures

The credit markets are registering the same anxiety by a different route. HYG — the benchmark high-yield ETF — slipped 0.22% on the week while LQD, its investment-grade counterpart, gained 0.39%. A 62-basis-point divergence in a single week suggests investors are beginning to sort credit quality in a way they were not doing a month ago. Banks carry loan books that straddle both grades, and a sustained HY/IG gap is a reliable early-warning for stress in the lower-rated portion of those books — the leveraged lending exposure that expanded during the 2023–2024 private-credit boom.

The energy piece adds a third dimension. USO, the crude oil proxy ETF, closed the week at $105.48 — down roughly 27% from its May high near $148. The decline reflects the U.S.-Iran ceasefire and the anticipated reopening of the Strait of Hormuz, which is expected to restore Middle Eastern supply flows that markets had priced out entirely. The macro narrative may be orderly, but the arithmetic for energy-sector lenders is not: a 27% move in the underlying collateral over two months forces banks with reserve-based lending exposure to revisit borrowing bases and mark loan books against a materially lower commodity price.

Taken together — NIM compression from flattening, credit-quality differentiation in HY, and energy-collateral stress — the picture is of a sector whose 20-day rally was real but whose tailwinds were borrowed from a curve environment that no longer exists.

What to Watch

The immediate catalyst is the 3m10y spread. At current levels it is approaching the 50-basis-point threshold that analysts treat as the point where the traditional bank carry trade turns marginal. A continued long-end rally — consistent with the demand-destruction pricing being registered across risk assets this week — would push that spread tighter still. The next read on bank borrowing conditions arrives with the Federal Reserve's July senior loan officer opinion survey; if credit standards tightened alongside the energy price move, the bid for XLF may not recover meaningfully before major-bank earnings open in the third week of July.
