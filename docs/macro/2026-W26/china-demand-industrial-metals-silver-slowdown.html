The China demand read: industrial metals and silver are pricing a real slowdown

Finished steel coils at a Chinese steel mill. China accounts for more than half of global crude steel production, making its industrial demand the swing variable for base metals prices worldwide. — Antoniorosset / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
China's May retail-sales drop — its first in three years — and copper imports at 15-year lows confirm that the 20-day selloff in base metals and silver has a real demand origin.

The starting premise is simple: when gold, silver, copper, and oil all decline simultaneously, supply cannot be the common explanation. Each commodity has a different supply story. The common variable is demand — and specifically, demand from the world's largest consumer of industrial materials.

China's economic data for May, released in mid-June, does not contradict that read. It corroborates it.

The physical data confirms what the prices are saying

China's official manufacturing PMI fell to 50.0 in May, the lowest in three months and exactly at the contraction threshold. The private Caixin survey eased to 51.8 from 52.2 in April. Neither reading signals collapse, but the sub-indices are harder to dismiss: new export orders contracted marginally, employment slipped, and the National Bureau of Statistics noted with unusual candour that "the domestic imbalance between strong supply and weak demand is acute."

The most striking print was retail sales. Consumer spending fell 0.6% year-on-year in May — the first outright decline in over three years, since December 2022. The categories that dropped hardest map directly onto industrial commodity demand: automobile sales fell 16.1%, household appliances were down 15.6%, building materials declined 13.6%. These goods consume steel, copper, aluminium, and silver.

Physical import data tells the same story. China's refined copper imports fell to 125,350 tons in February, the lowest monthly figure since April 2011. The Yangshan copper import premium — a live indicator of actual Chinese buying appetite for seaborne metal — collapsed to its lowest level since mid-2024. Shanghai Futures Exchange copper inventories hit a record 433,500 tons in early March. Fabricators are not absorbing inventory at current prices; they are sitting on it.

Silver's selloff, at -22.1% over 20 days — steeper than base metals — reflects the same demand read with added exposure. Roughly half of global silver consumption is industrial, centred on electronics and photovoltaic manufacturing. A Chinese consumer who is not buying appliances is also not driving demand for the silver solder and solar cells that those appliances contain.

The one complicating signal is iron ore. Imports surged 11.5% year-on-year in March to 104.7 million tons. But set alongside a 5% fall in Q1 steel production, that import surge looks more like precautionary stockpiling against supply disruption than genuine end-demand — a distinct phenomenon that should not dilute the broader commodity read.

The case for a financial or positioning explanation — leverage unwinds, momentum reversal — cannot be ruled out entirely. But positioning explanations typically produce sharp moves that reverse quickly. DBB is down 6.7% over 20 days, not over five sessions. Silver at -22.1% over a month is a sustained directional move, not a forced liquidation spike. Duration matters.

The two data sets — commodity prices and Chinese economic prints — are pointing at the same origin. The open question is severity and duration. June manufacturing PMI releases on July 1; Q2 GDP follows in mid-July. Those two numbers will determine whether May was a cyclical trough or the clearest reading yet in a trend that has further to go.
