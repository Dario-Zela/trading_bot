Three scenarios for oil at -19% — one is benign, two are not

A crude oil tanker off the Port of Los Angeles — the arteries that carry the commodity whose price is being reassessed. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
USO -19.3% in 20 days: a supply-mechanics read is benign; a China-demand or leveraged-unwind read puts Q2 energy earnings and bank loan books at risk.

USO has lost 19.3% over the past twenty trading days. At $105.48, it has retraced more than two-thirds of the premium the Strait of Hormuz crisis delivered when the US-Israel war shut down roughly 14 million barrels of daily seaborne supply beginning in late February. A decline that fast deserves a scenario framework — not a single causal story.

Three scenarios explain the move. They are not equally benign.

Scenario A — supply mechanics. The US-Iran ceasefire, signed in mid-June, put Hormuz reopening back on the table. Goldman Sachs responded by cutting its Brent forecast to $80 per barrel for Q4, from $90 previously. Natural gas provides the tell: UNG gained 1.1% this week while USO kept falling. If this were pure demand collapse, gas would follow oil down. The bifurcation argues for a supply-specific component — the crisis premium draining out as tanker transits resume. That is the benign scenario.

Scenario B — demand destruction. China's seaborne crude imports are tracking around 6.4 mb/d in June, the lowest since October 2016 and roughly 8% below May. The IEA has revised global demand down 1.1 mb/d for 2026, now forecasting an outright decline over the year. A genuine demand collapse carries broad pass-through: EM oil exporters face a fiscal arithmetic problem, energy-sector credit deteriorates, and commodity-linked economies absorb the shock with no obvious offset.

The leveraged-long question

The third scenario is diagnostic. Silver has lost roughly 20% from its 2026 highs, touching a seven-month low on June 24. Gold broke below $4,000. Base metals shed 6.7% in a week. A commodity-wide selloff of that breadth and synchrony does not come from fundamental repricing alone — it is consistent with forced de-leveraging of positions built when the Hormuz closure sent the commodity complex higher as a scarcity trade. Margin pressure, not new information, drives selling at that speed and across that many uncorrelated instruments.

Scenarios B and C are not mutually exclusive. A demand scare creates the initial momentum reversal; leveraged longs respond to margin calls by selling whatever is liquid, amplifying the move across silver, copper, and crude simultaneously.

For equity markets, the unresolved problem is Q2 earnings. XLE has shed 5.46% over twenty days — a meaningful discount, but still an understatement of the oil-price move. Exxon has already warned of a combined $1.5 billion earnings hit from lower crude and gas prices; Shell projected a $400-600 million loss for the quarter. The Q2 oil-price deck — averaged across a period that ran from Brent at $114 in early April to roughly $69 by late June — is materially lower than the consensus priced at the start of the quarter. Q3 guidance will reset the tape further.

XLF's flat week obscures a quieter stress. Bank loan books with energy-sector collateral are being tested: as asset values fall, the credit risk embedded in those books rises even if no headline loss has been booked yet.

The deciding data arrive in the IEA's July demand report and Exxon's Q2 call, expected around July 25. If energy credit spreads hold, scenario A is carrying the weight. If they widen, the benign reading was wishful.
