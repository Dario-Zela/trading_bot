Bull flattening: the long end is rallying and the 3m10y is compressing toward the danger zone

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C. — headquarters of the Board of Governors — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The 10Y dropped ~8bp to 4.372% while the 3M held at 3.663%, compressing the 3m10y spread to 70.9bp — the bond market is pricing demand destruction without waiting for the Fed.

The yield on the 10-year Treasury note fell roughly 8 basis points in the week ending June 26, closing at 4.372%. The 3-month bill barely moved, still at 3.663%, anchored by a Federal Reserve that voted 12-0 to hold rates at 3.5 to 3.75 percent just nine days earlier. The gap between those two rates — the 3m10y spread — compressed from 79.3 basis points to 70.9, one of the sharper single-week moves of the year.

The direction of that compression matters more than the level. At 70.9bp, the spread is still comfortably positive. But 50bp is the threshold the New York Fed's Estrella-Mishkin recession probability model treats as a meaningful inflection point. At the pace of the past two weeks, the market reaches that zone in five to seven weeks.

The long end is doing the work

Bull flattening means the front end holds while the back end rallies — duration buyers move into long-dated Treasuries, pushing yields down, while the market doesn't believe the front end will move any time soon. That's the current configuration. The Fed is on hold, with a mild hawkish tilt embedded in its June dot plot: nine of the 19 FOMC participants projected at least one additional rate increase in 2026. The 3-month yield reflects that reality with mechanical precision.

The 10-year is telling a different story. Its rally in a week when healthcare surged 7.3% and oil slid more than 4% is the bond market independently pricing demand contraction. When defensive equities outperform cyclicals and long-dated government debt rallies simultaneously, those aren't two separate views on the market. They're the same view from different seats.

The demand-destruction thesis that has been gaining traction in equity rotation is now embedded in the curve. The 10-year doesn't need the Fed to cut to move lower; it needs market participants to believe the next move will eventually be a cut, and to position accordingly today. That positioning is already underway.

What the 30Y is saying — and what it isn't

The 30-year Treasury closed at 4.864% — 49.2 basis points above the 10-year. That steepness at the long end is a partial check on a pure demand-collapse read. When recession becomes the central scenario, the curve from 10 years out tends to flatten as well. When the long bond remains elevated, it suggests the market is still carrying an inflation premium rather than fully pricing a demand-led downturn.

The current structure — a compressed 3m10y at 70.9bp, a flat 2s10s spread of roughly 27bp, but a 10-30y belly of 49.2bp — is consistent with late-cycle positioning rather than outright recession pricing. Investors are buying duration in the belly and the short end of the long end, but haven't committed to the view that growth destruction will suppress long-run inflation expectations enough to drag the 30-year alongside. That tension is real. The curve is saying demand risk is rising, but so is uncertainty about whether supply-side price pressures will persist long enough to keep the long bond pinned.

The belly flattens first; the 30-year lags. If the 3m10y closes toward 50bp over the coming weeks, watch whether the 10-30y spread starts compressing too. That would signal a more complete demand-destruction pricing, and it would make the bond market's message considerably harder for the FOMC to dismiss.

What the curve is telling the FOMC

The July 29-30 FOMC meeting arrives with roughly 69% of futures-market probability still assigned to a hold. That's not surprising. Kevin Warsh has had one meeting as chair; the June 17 statement was deliberately stripped of forward guidance — a signal, per Warsh, that the Fed wants to preserve flexibility rather than pre-commit. Rates held at 3.5 to 3.75 percent, and the word "uncertainty" appeared alongside references to Middle East energy shocks and growth that the statement described as "solid."

But the bond market doesn't wait for FOMC statements. A 3m10y at 70.9bp and moving lower is itself a form of monetary easing — it reduces long-term borrowing costs and signals to the market that the terminal rate view is softening. Every 10bp of further compression makes the case for future cuts more legible in the rates market, even if the dot plot hasn't moved.

If the spread reaches 50bp before July 29, the committee faces a more complicated communication task. The bond market will have already priced a growth slowdown; the Fed will need to either validate that read or lean against it. With Warsh's first test logged as an orderly hold and a hawkish tilt, the second test will be whether his flexibility doctrine means following the curve down or holding the line while the market prices out more risk on its own.

The next meaningful data point is the jobs report for the week of July 4. A labor market read that confirms demand softening — slower hiring, shorter hours — would accelerate the compression and bring the 50bp threshold into view before the August meeting. A strong print would give the curve pause, but probably not reverse it. The trajectory now has its own momentum.
