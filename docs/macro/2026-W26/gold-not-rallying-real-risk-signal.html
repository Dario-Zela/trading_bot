RISK · SAFE HAVENS · By S. Vance · 2026-06-28 · 6 min read

Gold is not rallying in a risk-off week — and that is the real warning

Gold bullion bars — the metal's failure to rally as defensive equities surged is a diagnostic signal about the nature of this week's selloff — Stevebidmead / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
Gold fell 3.5% while defensives surged and bonds rallied — its absence from the safe-haven bid identifies this as a real-rates event, not a financial panic.

Gold is supposed to rally when the world gets nervous. That is the oldest axiom in markets: when equities price in risk, when credit spreads widen, when investors sprint for the exits, gold is where they go. This week upended that script entirely. Healthcare stocks gained 7.3%, utilities added 3.2%, the 10-year Treasury rallied 8 basis points, and high-yield credit underperformed investment-grade by 62 basis points — every textbook indicator of a genuine risk-off rotation. Gold fell 3.5%. Over the past 20 trading days, it is down 9.5%. The metal that is supposed to protect portfolios when everything else fails was not at its post.

This is not a footnote. When a safe-haven asset refuses its role during a genuine risk-off episode, it is telling you something important about the underlying mechanics of the selloff — and about what kind of event this actually is.

Not all fear is the same

A financial panic — bank runs, credit seizure, systemic counterparty risk — drives flows into monetary stores of value: gold, the Swiss franc, the long end of the Treasury curve. A demand-destruction scare — the economy is slowing, profits will contract, volumes will fall — drives flows into defensive equities and quality bonds, but metals can sell off because their industrial thesis is compromised. And a leverage unwind — a position that was too large, financed too cheaply, now being forcibly closed — can send any asset lower regardless of its fundamental valuation.

This week has the fingerprints of all three in different corners of the market. But the gold signal cuts through the noise: if this were a pure financial panic, gold would be higher. The most parsimonious reading comes from the real rates channel. With the 10-year TIPS yield running at approximately 2.16% and nominal yields sitting near 4.5%, US government bonds are offering a meaningful, riskless positive real return. Gold yields zero. When real yields are this high, the opportunity cost of holding gold is not theoretical — it is a direct drag on institutional portfolio allocation. Inflation expectations appear to be falling faster than nominal yields, which pushes real yields higher even as the bond market rallies in nominal terms. That is gold's primary structural headwind, and it is operating in full force.

The World Gold Council's Q1 2026 demand data provides structural confirmation. Global jewellery demand was 300 tonnes, down 23% year-on-year. China's jewellery demand fell 32%, India's dropped 19%, the Middle East recorded a 23% decline. Physical demand at the consumer level has been hollowing out for months. The current price — down from above $4,600 earlier this year toward the $4,100 range — reflects both the macro headwind from real yields and the structural erosion of physical buying. This week's -3.5% is not an anomaly. It is an acceleration.

Silver as the diagnostic

If the real rates channel explains gold's underperformance, it does not come close to explaining silver. Over the past 20 trading days, silver is down 22.1% — more than double gold's 9.5% decline. The gold-silver ratio has expanded sharply to approximately 65:1. That kind of divergence is not the fingerprint of a macro repricing. It is the fingerprint of a leveraged position being unwound.

Silver's first-quarter history matters here. When silver surged from around $30 per ounce in early 2025 to peaks above $120 in late January 2026, the CME Group raised margin requirements on silver futures sharply, forcing leveraged traders to post more collateral or liquidate. Many liquidated. Prices cascaded from over $120 to the $60s within weeks. What is happening now — silver down 22.1% in 20 days while gold loses less than half that — suggests a second wave of forced selling, whether from traders who re-entered long positions too aggressively on the rebound, or from correlated commodity funds caught in a broader metals move.

The commodity cross-section provides supporting evidence. Natural gas is the one commodity posting a gain this week — up 1.1% — while precious and industrial metals sell off broadly. If this were a uniform demand-destruction event driven by China slowdown fears, natural gas would also be under pressure. Its strength points to supply-specific dynamics for energy, not a generalised collapse in global demand. Copper imports into China declined 16% year-on-year in the first two months of 2026, but that is a separately established structural trend; using it to explain this week's silver move is over-reaching. The silver selloff is outsized, discrete, and consistent with margin-driven liquidation rather than fundamental revaluation.

What the diagnostic implies for next week

The three candidate scenarios — rising real rates, demand destruction, leverage unwind — are not mutually exclusive, and the most honest answer is that all three are operating simultaneously. But they differ in their implications for duration and reversibility.

Real rates as the primary driver is a durable headwind. As long as the Fed holds rates and inflation expectations drift lower, positive real yields will mechanically suppress gold's appeal relative to government bonds. This is not a one-week phenomenon.

Demand destruction, if confirmed, would extend the selloff into the commodity complex broadly — copper, silver, and eventually oil if growth fears intensify. The natural gas divergence this week argues against this being the dominant read right now.

A leverage unwind, by contrast, is self-limiting. Forced sellers can only sell what they own, and once the position is cleared, the overhang lifts. If silver stabilises next week and begins recovering relative to gold — if the ratio compresses back below 62:1 — that would be the clearest signal that the mechanical selling is largely done.

The cleanest diagnostic test: if GLD moves positive while HYG (high-yield credit) moves negative next week — the classic flight-to-safety divergence — the leverage-unwind interpretation is favoured, and gold's monetary role may be reasserting. If gold continues to fall alongside high-yield credit, the real-rates and demand-destruction scenarios are dominant, and the selloff has further to run.

The Q2 rebalancing window closes July 3. Institutional flows associated with quarter-end have provided cover for some of the week's volatility. After that date, the alibi expires — whatever remains in the price action is a genuine directional signal. Watch the 10-year TIPS breakeven rate: if it stabilises above 2.2%, the real rate headwind on gold eases marginally. If it compresses further, the headwind intensifies. Gold's failure to show up this week is not a loss. It is a diagnostic. The metal is telling you this is a real-rate and structural-demand event, not a monetary panic — and the coming week's data will say whether it is right.
