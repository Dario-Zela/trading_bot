RATES · LONG END · By G. Mehlman · 2026-11-22 · 4 min read

The 10-year sits 9 basis points from 5% and the 30-year is already past it

In one sentence
Long Treasury yields held near cycle highs as stocks and commodities fell, pointing to term premium and supply, not growth fears, as the driver at the long end.

When investors are frightened, they usually buy government bonds. Prices rise, yields fall, and the safest paper in the world takes in what the rest of the market is dumping. The week to 20 November did not follow that pattern. Equities, commodities and gold sold off hard. The 10-year Treasury ended the week at 4.91%, nine basis points short of 5%. The 30-year closed at 5.30%, already well beyond that line.

The technology sector fell 12.5% over the week, as measured by the XLK fund. Yields at the long end stayed close to cycle-high levels throughout. For a portfolio manager holding both stocks and long bonds, the bonds provided no cushion.

What the curve is saying

The shape of the curve is the first clue. The 3-month bill yielded 4.12% on 20 November and the 5-year note 4.70%. The 10-year sat at 4.91% and the 30-year at 5.30%. The gap between the 3-month and the 10-year was +0.79 percentage points.

That is a steep curve, not an inverted one. An inverted curve is the classic recession signal, with short rates above long rates because markets expect the central bank to cut into weakness. Nothing here resembles that. Short rates sit well below long rates, and the premium for lending to the government for ten or thirty years remains large.

If growth fears were driving the selloff in risk assets, the long end would normally have rallied. Investors would price in slower activity and lower future policy rates, and long yields would fall. They did not. The more natural reading is that the long end is being set by something other than the growth outlook: the compensation investors demand for holding duration, usually called the term premium, and the volume of Treasury debt they are asked to absorb.

This is inference from the price action, not something the data prove. Yields alone cannot separate expected policy rates from the term premium, and published term-premium estimates are model-dependent. But the pattern fits. Yields that stay high while other assets fall point to sellers of duration, or to buyers who will only take it at a higher price, rather than to an economy expected to weaken.

The move also fits the other pieces of the week's picture. The dollar has climbed about 2% over 20 days, and gold failed to act as a hedge. When the safe assets do not rally together with the sell-off, the market is not simply de-risking. It is repricing the cost of financing.

What a close above 5% would mean

Five per cent on the 10-year is a round number, and round numbers matter in markets mostly because positioning and attention gather around them. The 30-year has already passed it, so a 10-year close above it would not be a surprise so much as a confirmation that the entire long end now sits above that threshold.

The first channel is equity valuation. Stock multiples are, in arithmetic terms, a function of the rate used to discount future earnings. A higher long yield lowers the present value of profits that arrive far in the future, which is why long-duration growth stocks are the most exposed. The technology sector's sharp weekly and monthly declines are consistent with that sensitivity, although the sell-off has other causes as well, including crowded positioning.

The second channel is competition. A risk-free 10-year yield near 5% and a 30-year above it raise the hurdle that equities must clear. The gap between the earnings yield on stocks and the bond yield narrows, and the extra return investors demand for owning shares has to come from either lower prices or higher expected earnings. In a week with falling prices, the first is the one on offer.

The third channel, and the one that matters most for portfolio construction, is the stock-bond correlation. For much of the period before 2022, bonds tended to rise when stocks fell, which made the classic 60/40 mix work. When inflation or supply, rather than growth, dominates, that relationship flips: stocks and bonds fall together, because the same force that lifts yields is the one that hurts equity multiples. The past week looks like an example of the second regime. Bonds did not rise as stocks fell, and a 10-year close above 5% would reinforce the view that the old hedge is unreliable.

What to watch

Three things will show whether the long end is still the source of pressure. The first is whether the 10-year closes above 5% on a daily basis, and whether it holds there. The second is the 30-year, which at 5.30% leaves little room before it tests levels that are higher still. The third is the steepness of the curve: if the 3m10y spread keeps widening while risk assets stay weak, the term-premium reading gains weight. If the spread narrows because long yields fall, it would suggest that growth concerns are finally taking over.

Treasury auctions and the next inflation and employment releases are the nearest catalysts. A weak auction, with soft demand at the long end, would be the cleanest evidence that supply is the constraint. Until then, the lesson of the week is narrower: when stocks fell, long Treasuries did not come to the rescue.
