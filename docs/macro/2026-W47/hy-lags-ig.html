CREDIT · HY VS IG · By T. Larch · 2026-11-22 · 4 min read

High yield lagged investment grade by 1.6 points in a week

In one sentence
HYG fell 0.79% while LQD rose 0.86%, a 1.65-point quality rotation in credit that is orderly for now but exposed to oil's slide and high long-end yields.

Credit markets are sorting borrowers by quality rather than running from them. Over the past five sessions the largest exchange-traded fund tracking high-yield corporate bonds, HYG, fell 0.79%. Its investment-grade counterpart, LQD, rose 0.86%. The 1.65-point gap between the two is the widest sign yet that the week's equity selloff has reached the bond market, though it has arrived in a far more orderly form.

A rotation, not a rout

The direction of the moves matters as much as their size. In a true credit panic, investment-grade funds fall alongside junk, because forced sellers dump whatever they can get a bid on. Here the safer product gained. That pattern fits investors cutting risk and keeping their money in credit, rather than leaving the asset class altogether.

There is a rates explanation too. LQD carries far more interest-rate sensitivity than HYG, so a rally in Treasuries would lift it more. But the long end of the Treasury curve has not obliged: the 10-year sits 9 basis points from 5%, and the 30-year is already above it (see the long end near five). If long yields had been falling, the gain in LQD would be easy to explain. With long yields this high, LQD's rise looks less like a duration trade and more like a preference for issuer quality. That reading rests on the price moves alone. It is an inference, not something the data confirms directly.

Set against equities, the move is mild. Technology's 18.7% monthly drop is the centre of gravity for risk assets (see technology's outlier month), and the broader unwinding of crowded positions is covered in the day's lead editorial. A sub-1% decline in HYG is small beside those numbers. High-yield funds have so far behaved like a market under mild pressure, not a market in distress.

The oil problem

The part of high yield to watch is energy. The oil fund USO has fallen 17.6% over 20 days, and the energy sector is down 12.7% over a month (see energy and the oil-gas split). Energy producers have historically made up a large share of the high-yield universe, and their borrowing costs track the crude price closely. Cheaper oil cuts cash flow, thins the cushion for covering interest, and makes refinancing harder for the weakest names.

One caveat is that this piece has not matched HYG's weekly loss to specific energy holdings. A 0.79% fall is small enough that it may reflect broad risk reduction rather than energy-specific stress. But the timing is worth noting. A fall in oil of this size is the type of shock that usually reaches bond prices with a lag, as ratings agencies and lenders reassess the weakest balance sheets. Today's weekly move may be an early reading on that process rather than the whole of it.

The dollar adds pressure. Its 20-day gain of roughly 2% (see the dollar as a quiet tightener) tightens financial conditions for borrowers abroad, and for commodity exporters in particular (see the emerging-market double hit).

What would make it a real episode

A 1.65-point weekly gap is notable, but one week does not make a widening cycle. Several developments would turn this into one.

  • Investment grade stops rising. The orderly reading depends on LQD holding its gain. If the safer fund starts to fall alongside HYG, the story shifts from quality rotation to indiscriminate selling.
  • Outflows from high-yield funds. Persistent redemptions force funds to sell bonds into thin markets, which can push prices down faster than credit fundamentals justify.
  • Oil keeps falling. A further leg lower in crude would extend the pressure on energy issuers, which is where defaults would be likeliest to start.
  • Long yields keep rising. Higher funding costs for the entire corporate sector would hit weaker borrowers first, and would blunt the hedge that investment-grade bonds have so far provided.
  • Safe havens keep failing. Gold's poor showing as a hedge (see why the hedges failed) leaves fewer places to hide if credit does sour.

The next test is the coming week's price action in HYG and LQD relative to the 1.65-point gap. If the gap narrows through a rebound in high yield, this will read as a brief repricing. If it widens while oil stays weak and long yields stay near 5%, quality rotation will start to look like the first stage of a more serious spread-widening episode.
