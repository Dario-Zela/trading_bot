SECTORS · TECHNOLOGY · By I. Aresti · 2026-11-22 · 4 min read

Technology's 18.7% monthly drop is the market's centre of gravity

In one sentence
XLK's 12.5% weekly drop nearly doubles the next-worst sector's loss; whether rates or crowded positioning is behind it will show in how it trades against a 10-year near 5%.

The market's weakness this month has an address, and it is the technology sector. The Technology Select Sector SPDR (XLK) fell 12.5% in the past five trading days and 18.7% over twenty. The next-worst sectors over the week, communication services (XLC) at -8.0% and materials (XLB) at -7.4%, lost roughly two-thirds as much. Most sector sell-offs are a crowd moving in the same direction at different speeds. This one is a single sector moving well ahead of the crowd.

An outlier, not just a laggard

The distinction matters for diagnosis. If the decline were a broad repricing of equities, the sector ranking would be tightly bunched, with technology a little worse than the rest. Instead there is a clear gap between first and second place. Technology's five-day loss is about 1.6 times that of communication services, the runner-up, and about 1.7 times that of materials.

The weekly drop also accounts for most of the monthly one. A 12.5% fall in five days inside an 18.7% fall in twenty means roughly two-thirds of the damage arrived in the last quarter of the window. The sell-off is accelerating, not grinding.

The defensive end of the board tells the other half of the story. Consumer staples (XLP) is the only sector in the weakest-to-strongest defensive group with a positive twenty-day return, at +2.49%. Money has not left equities wholesale. It has left the parts of the market that carry the most expectations.

Duration, or positioning?

Two explanations fit the numbers, and they make different predictions.

The first is duration. Technology is the sector most dependent on earnings that lie far in the future, so its valuation is the most sensitive to the rate used to discount them. The 10-year Treasury yield is near 4.9%, about nine basis points from 5%, and the 30-year is already above that line (see the rates piece in this edition). On this reading, XLK is simply the sector with the longest-dated cash flows, and it is being repriced for a higher cost of capital. The dollar's roughly 2% climb over twenty days points the same way, since it tightens financial conditions on top of the rise in yields (see the FX piece).

The second is positioning. Technology has been the most heavily owned sector in the index, and crowded positions tend to unwind fast and indiscriminately once a few holders are forced to sell. The evidence in favour is the pattern: a sharp acceleration, a large gap to the next sector, and weakness in other consumers of the same trade. Gold's failure as a hedge during the same stretch suggests that selling pressure was not confined to equities (see the risk piece). That is typical of deleveraging, in which holders sell what they can rather than what they want to.

The two are not exclusive. A rate shock can be the trigger and positioning the amplifier. But the relative weights matter, because they imply different recoveries. This piece cannot settle that weighting from price data alone. Sector returns show how much technology fell, not who sold it or why.

What would tell them apart

The test is simple and can be run on prices alone. If duration is the driver, XLK should track yields: it should stabilise only when the 10-year and 30-year stop rising, and it should recover when they fall. If positioning is the driver, XLK can rebound while yields stay where they are, because the selling pressure was forced and has a natural end.

A rebound in XLK without lower yields would therefore favour positioning. A further slide while yields hold near 4.9% would not rule out positioning, but it would make the duration case harder to dismiss. A rebound that arrives only after yields retreat would point firmly to rates.

There is also a mundane reason for caution. After a 12.5% week, even a forced seller's exit tends to produce a sharp bounce, so a single up day in XLK is weak evidence for anything. The useful signal is a rebound that survives a further test of the 5% level on the 10-year.

That test is the next catalyst. A 10-year yield through 5% would hand the duration argument fresh evidence, with the 30-year already past it. A failure to break, paired with technology holding its lows, would suggest the sector's decline had more to do with who owned it than with what rates did.
