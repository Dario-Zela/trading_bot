EDITORIAL · WEEKLY THESIS · By The Editor · 2026-11-22 · 5 min read

Everything that was crowded fell at once

In one sentence
Tech, metals and energy were liquidated together while a 4.91% 10-year and a firmer dollar tightened conditions; the next two weeks will show whether it was a flush or a tightening.

The week's losses were not about one idea going wrong. Technology fell 12.5% in five days and is down 18.7% over twenty. Silver lost 16.2% and gold 9.2%. Energy, already down 12.7% on the month, kept sliding. Every sector in the market finished the five days negative. The pattern looks less like investors changing their minds about a theme and more like a crowd leaving a room all at once.

That is the house reading, and it comes with a test. If the selling was forced, it should end quickly. If it was something else, the bond market will say so.

What fell

A repricing has a story. Rates rise and long-duration growth stocks suffer, or growth fears build and cyclicals suffer, or inflation scares fade and gold suffers. Each leaves a fingerprint, with some assets hurt and others helped.

This week's fingerprint is a smear. Technology, silver, gold and energy share little in their fundamentals. They did share one feature: they were popular. Each had drawn heavy positioning, whether momentum funds in tech, retail and macro money in the metals, or the long-running energy trade. When positions like those are cut together, correlations that look absurd in calm weather become the norm.

The silver and gold moves fit that account. Silver's 16.2% drop is nearly double gold's 9.2%, the usual signature of a more speculative, more levered holder base being shaken out first. A fundamental repricing of precious metals would be unlikely to arrive in the same week as a 12.5% drop in the technology sector.

No clean hiding place

The defensives did not hold. Consumer staples lost 2.05% over five days, utilities 3.07% and real estate 4.30%. These are the sectors investors rotate into when they want to be out of the storm, and none of them rose.

That matters for two reasons. First, it is what indiscriminate selling looks like: when funds must cut gross exposure, they sell what they can sell, not what they would like to sell. Second, it points to a common cause outside equities. Utilities and real estate are bond proxies, valued off the yield curve. They suffer when long rates rise even if nothing is wrong with the underlying businesses.

The companion piece on gold's failure as a hedge takes up the uncomfortable corollary. An asset sold to protect portfolios fell 9.2% in the week that protection was needed.

The squeeze from the other side

The plausible common pressure is visible in rates and currency. The 10-year Treasury yield stands at 4.91%, nine basis points from 5%. The 30-year, at 5.30%, is already beyond it. The dollar index, at 103.81, has risen 1.96% over twenty days. Higher long yields raise the return that every risk asset must beat, and a stronger dollar tightens financial conditions for anyone who borrows in it or earns in other currencies or in commodities priced in dollars. The long end near five and the dollar as quiet tightener are the two halves of this squeeze, and the US financing and emerging-market angles show where it bites.

Credit offers a smaller clue. High yield lagged investment grade by 1.6 points over the week, which is the direction one would expect when funding gets harder at the margin. It is a modest gap, and the credit piece treats it as such. It matters mainly as a thing to keep watching.

The two explanations are not exclusive. Higher yields and a firmer dollar may well have been the trigger that forced the crowded positions out. The editorial question is whether the trigger keeps pulling or the flush simply runs its course.

How the thesis can fail

A thesis that cannot be wrong is only a mood, so here are the terms.

If this was deleveraging, the following should happen within one to two weeks. The 10-year should stabilise below 5% rather than march through it. The dollar should stop rising. Metals and technology, the two hardest-hit and most crowded areas, should bounce, because forced sellers are indifferent to price and their exit leaves cheap assets behind. A bounce need not recover the losses. It only needs to show that the selling was mechanical.

If instead the 10-year breaks above 5% and holds there, the story changes. A yield that crosses a round number and stays across it is a genuine tightening of conditions, and no amount of position-cleaning would explain it away. The same goes for credit. If HYG keeps lagging LQD in the coming days, the market is charging more for weaker balance sheets, and the week's losses will look like the first instalment of a repricing, not a flush.

There is also a reading in which both are true: a flush that ends, followed by a tighter backdrop that caps the recovery. Technology's 18.7% monthly decline, examined in the sector piece, leaves a lot of room for a bounce and not much reason to expect a return to the highs while the 30-year sits above 5%. Energy's slide, which the energy report traces mainly to oil, may take longer to stabilise, since it began before this week.

What to watch

The cleanest evidence arrives early. The first test is the 10-year yield against 5% over the next several sessions. The second is the dollar index: another leg above 103.81 would count against the forced-selling reading. The third is whether silver and the technology sector rally on days when yields are flat. A bounce that needs falling yields to work is a relief trade. A bounce that comes without them suggests the crowd has finished leaving.

Until those answers come in, the week's lesson is narrower than a new macro regime and broader than a bad day for tech. Crowded trades are fragile to funding conditions, and this week the funding conditions moved.
