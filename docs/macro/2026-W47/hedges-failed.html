RISK · HEDGE FAILURE · By S. Vance · 2026-11-22 · 5 min read

Gold failed as a hedge, which is the real warning

In one sentence
Gold, silver, long bonds and defensive stocks all fell together, a pattern that points to forced selling and a funding squeeze rather than a change of view on any one asset.

When stocks fall, the standard portfolio has a short list of places to hide. Treasuries are meant to rally. Gold is meant to hold or rise. Utilities, staples and other defensive sectors are meant to lose less than the rest. In the week equities sold off, almost none of that happened.

The SPDR Gold Shares ETF (GLD) fell 9.17% over five days and 11.93% over twenty. The silver ETF (SLV) did worse: down 16.22% in a week and 24.12% in a month. Consumer staples (XLP), utilities (XLU) and real estate (XLRE) were all negative over the five days. The 10-year Treasury yield stood at 4.91%, with no sign of the duration rally that usually accompanies a risk-off move.

A falling stock market is an ordinary event. Every hedge falling with it is a different kind of signal.

Why the pattern matters

Hedges work because different assets respond to different fears. Bonds pay off when growth worries dominate. Gold pays off when confidence in money or institutions wobbles. Defensive stocks pay off because people still buy toothpaste and switch on the lights. A decline in one of these is routine, and a decline in all of them at once is not.

When everything is sold together, the usual explanation is that the selling is not about the value of any single asset. It is about the seller. An investor facing a margin call, a redemption request or a funding squeeze sells what can be sold, not what should be. Gold and silver are liquid, widely held and, after a strong run, often sitting on large gains. They are natural sources of cash. Silver's 24% monthly fall, against 12% for gold, is the sort of gap a leveraged, crowded position produces.

This is an inference from the price pattern, not an established fact. The data show a correlated sell-off; they do not show who was forced to sell. But the pattern fits a liquidity or margin-driven event better than it fits a change in views on any one asset. It also fits the wider picture in this edition, where technology's 18.7% monthly drop and energy's 12.7% slide point to positions being cut across the board. For the broader account of that unwind, see Everything that was crowded fell at once.

What the missing bond rally says

The most telling absence is in Treasuries. In an ordinary growth scare, money moves into government bonds and yields fall. Here the 10-year sits at 4.91%, nine basis points from 5%, and the 30-year is already past it. Bonds at the long end are not acting as a refuge; they are behaving like one more thing being sold, or one more thing nobody is willing to buy at a higher price. The pressure on US assets is examined in US assets took the hit while the long end refused to help, and the rates picture in the 10-year sits 9 basis points from 5%.

A hedge that fails in the same direction as the thing it protects is worse than no hedge, because portfolios were sized on the assumption that it would not.

What to watch for a second leg

Four markers would separate an orderly repricing from something more disorderly.

  • The 10-year above 5%. The level is a round number and a psychological line more than a mechanical one. A close above it, with equities still falling, would confirm that long-dated bonds are not offering shelter.
  • A widening gap between high yield and investment grade. High yield lagged investment grade by 1.6 points in a week. A move from lag to outright stress, with spreads gapping wider and primary issuance shutting, would show the squeeze spreading from risk assets into funding.
  • A still-rising dollar. The dollar's 20-day climb of roughly 2% works as a quiet tightener. If it keeps rising during a sell-off, it suggests demand for dollar funding is outrunning supply. That is the usual mark of a scramble for cash, and it falls hardest on emerging markets and commodity exporters.
  • Gold and silver failing to stabilise. Precious metals that keep falling while the dollar rises would suggest forced liquidation is still under way. A bounce in gold while equities and bonds keep sliding would suggest the cash need is easing.

What a disorderly second leg would look like

For portfolios built on diversification, the risk is arithmetic. A classic 60/40 mix, with a gold sleeve and a defensive tilt inside the equity half, assumes that at least one component rises or holds when another falls. If stocks, long bonds, metals and defensives fall together, there is nothing left to rebalance out of, and drawdowns compound rather than offset one another. Strategies that target a fixed level of volatility would be forced to cut exposure as volatility rises, adding to the selling. Investors who borrow against their holdings would face more margin calls on the same assets, which would feed the next round of sales.

None of this is yet a verdict. A week and a month of data cannot prove that a funding event is under way, and sell-offs of this kind can burn out quickly once the forced sellers are finished. But the combination of a 10-year at 4.91%, a firmer dollar, weaker credit at the lower end and failed hedges is the combination that precedes the worse version of events. The next tests are plain: whether the 10-year closes above 5%, whether the high-yield gap widens, and whether the dollar's climb continues into the next full week of trading.
