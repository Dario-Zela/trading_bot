Energy is down 12.7% in a month and oil is the reason

In one sentence
XLE has lost 12.7% in 20 days as crude proxy USO fell 17.6%, while natural gas fund UNG rose 10.2%, and long yields are staying high despite cheaper oil.

The energy sector has had a month in which its two main raw materials moved in opposite directions. The Energy Select Sector SPDR (XLE) is down 6.85% over five trading days and 12.72% over twenty. The United States Oil Fund (USO), a proxy for crude, has fallen 4.09% on the week and 17.64% on the month. The United States Natural Gas Fund (UNG) has risen 4.04% on the week and 10.23% on the month. Anyone who treats "energy" as a single trade has had a poor few weeks.

Two commodities, one sector label

XLE holds equities, not futures, and the equities lean toward oil. Its holdings are mostly the integrated majors, crude-weighted exploration and production companies, and the service and refining businesses that depend on drilling activity and fuel margins. The fund's 12.7% decline is smaller than crude's 17.6%, as it should be. Producers hedge, integrated companies earn money downstream, and dividends cushion share prices. It is still a large fall for a sector usually sold as defensive.

Gas is a different business. Companies weighted toward natural gas, and the pipeline and processing firms that move it, are tied to a commodity that has gained 10.2% in a month. UNG's gain does not translate one for one into equities, because futures-based funds carry roll costs and the shares reflect balance sheets and hedging books. But the divergence indicates where the damage sits: in crude, and in the companies that are paid for crude.

The split also undercuts the usual explanations for a broad sell-off. A general flight from commodities would have dragged gas down too. Something specific to oil, whether demand worries, supply, or positioning, is a likelier cause, though the price data alone cannot say which.

What cheaper crude does to inflation expectations

For policymakers, falling oil is normally welcome. Fuel feeds straight into headline inflation and into the inflation expectations households and markets carry forward. A 17.6% drop in crude over a month should, other things equal, pull near-term breakevens lower.

Other things are not equal. The 10-year Treasury yield sits about nine basis points from 5%, and the 30-year has already passed it. If lower oil were easing inflation fears, long yields would be expected to ease with it. They have not. That suggests the long end is pricing something other than energy: heavy Treasury supply, a higher required term premium, or doubts about the fiscal path. Cheaper crude is arriving in a bond market that is not paying attention to it.

The dollar is another factor. Its 2% climb over 20 days is part of the pressure on dollar-priced commodities, and a stronger currency also tightens financial conditions without any central bank action.

What to watch

Three things will show whether the oil slide is a repricing or a pause. The first is whether crude stabilises while long yields stay high, which would confirm that rates are driven by something other than fuel costs. The second is the next CPI release, where the energy component will show how much of the drop has reached consumer prices. The third is whether gas holds its gains or fades back toward oil's trend. A sector split between a falling commodity and a rising one is unstable, and one of the two prices will have to give way.
