RATES · THE CURVE · By G. Mehlman · 2026-10-18 · 5 min read

A 5.10% ten-year and a 103bp curve leave no room for a duration hedge

In one sentence
The curve is steep from 4.07% at three months to 5.44% at thirty years, and the long end is where the pricing pressure sits.

The Treasury curve ended the week with a wide gap between what the government pays to borrow for three months and what it pays to borrow for thirty years. Three-month bills yield 4.07%. Five-year notes yield 4.90%, ten-year notes 5.10% and the thirty-year bond 5.44%. A ten-year yield above 5% is a level that has drawn attention in past episodes. This one arrives with oil sharply higher and investors leaning toward defensive stocks.

One caveat first. The data behind this piece is a snapshot of levels. It does not include last week's closing yields, so it cannot say by how many basis points any point on the curve moved. The only directional evidence is indirect, and it is covered below.

Walking the curve

The distance from the front to the long end is 137 basis points, from 4.07% to 5.44%. The shape is uneven.

  • From three months to five years the curve climbs 83bp, to 4.90%.
  • From five years to ten years it adds only 20bp, to 5.10%.
  • From ten years to thirty years it adds another 34bp, to 5.44%.

The 3M-10Y spread of +1.03 points is a clear upward slope. Most of that slope is built before the five-year point, and the belly-to-long-end segment is comparatively flat. The five-year sits 83bp above bills. That is the market charging for several years of uncertainty, whether about the path of policy rates, inflation, or the volume of debt investors must absorb.

The snapshot cannot divide that 83bp into expected policy moves and a premium for holding duration. A curve this shape is consistent with a front end that is not being priced for aggressive easing. It is also consistent with investors demanding extra compensation to lend for five years or more. The two explanations imply different things for the economy, and the yields alone cannot separate them. What the numbers do show is that a yield of 5.44% at thirty years, 34bp above the ten-year, means the long end is where the extra pricing pressure sits.

Oil, defensives and a 5% long bond

The week's other moves do not obviously fit with yields at these levels. Oil jumped about 9%, a shock covered in Oil's 9% jump is the input that matters for the long end. Investors also reached for safety. Staples and health care led, while technology fell 7.7%, the worst sector move on the board.

In a typical flight to safety, Treasury yields fall as money moves into bonds. Here, a ten-year at 5.10% suggests that bonds are not behaving as a clean haven. An oil spike adds an inflation risk that bondholders must price, and that is especially unwelcome for long-dated paper. Defensive equity leadership says investors want protection from growth risk. The two signals can coexist, but only if the market is worried about slower growth and higher prices together. Duration then offers a poor hedge against either.

That is the argument in the headline. A long bond that yields 5.44% pays well to hold, but it is also the instrument most exposed if inflation worries lift the long end further. Investors who would normally buy duration as insurance against an equity selloff face a trade-off: the yield is attractive, and the mark-to-market risk is not small. The wider regional consequences are discussed in The US faces a 5% long bond and an oil shock at once.

What the credit market implies

The best clue to direction comes from credit. The iShares iBoxx Investment Grade Corporate Bond ETF (LQD) gained 0.97% over five days. Because investment-grade bonds carry long duration, a gain of that size is consistent with long-term yields easing at least somewhat over the week, or with spreads tightening, or both. It would be a mistake to read that as proof that Treasury yields fell. LQD's move reflects both the Treasury component and the credit spread, and the snapshot does not break the return into the two.

It also sits oddly beside the rest of the tape. High-yield bonds slipped in the same week, a split of roughly 1.4 points between the two credit tiers, taken up in Investment grade rallies while high yield slips. Investors appear to have wanted quality, and longer-duration quality in particular, even as the absolute yield on Treasuries stayed high. A modest rally from an elevated starting point does not shrink the cost of borrowing much. It means the pain did not worsen for the holders of investment-grade debt.

Precious metals did not offer an alternative, as Precious metals fail to hedge shows, with silver down 14% in a month. The wider framing of a market paying for safety and oil while growth carries the cost is set out in The market paid for safety and oil this week.

What to watch

The next test is whether the long end holds near 5.4% if oil stays elevated. The 30-year yield and the 10s-30s gap of 34bp are the cleanest gauges: if the gap widens while the front end stays near 4.07%, that points to term premium rather than a change in policy expectations. The next inflation data will show whether the oil move is feeding into prices. Upcoming Treasury auctions will show how readily buyers absorb long-dated supply at these yields.
