RISK · HEDGE FAILURE · By S. Vance · 2026-10-18 · 4 min read

Precious metals fail to hedge: silver is down 14% in a month

In one sentence
In a week of equity stress, gold slipped 0.7% and silver fell 6%, as a firm dollar and 5%-plus real rates left staples, energy and IG credit as the only hedges that paid.

Gold is supposed to be the asset that holds up when everything else is under pressure. This week it did not. Technology stocks had their worst week on the board, and the metals fell with them. Gold slipped 0.7% over five days and silver dropped 6%. Over twenty trading days the damage is larger: the gold fund GLD is down 4.8% and the silver fund SLV is down 14%.

A portfolio built on the textbook assumption that metals cushion an equity drawdown would have lost on both sides at once.

What the tape shows

GLD closed at 382.01, down 0.67% on the week and 4.78% over twenty days. SLV closed at 51.51, down 5.97% on the week and 14.05% over twenty days. Silver has fallen roughly three times as far as gold over the month. That is its usual pattern. It trades partly as a monetary metal and partly as an industrial one, so it tends to amplify gold's moves in both directions, and it is more exposed when growth fears and a strong dollar arrive together.

The equity side of the week was one-directional. Technology fell 7.7%, the worst sector move on the board. The pieces that held up were mostly the unglamorous ones. Consumer staples did well as the market reached for defensives, and energy extended a monthly gain of about 6%. Investment-grade credit rallied while high yield slipped, a 1.4-point split in one week. By the triage tally, the only hedges that paid were staples (XLP), energy (XLE) and investment-grade corporate bonds (LQD). Gold and silver are not on that list.

Why the metals are hard to hold

Gold pays no income. Its appeal rests partly on what else an investor could earn for holding cash or bonds, and that alternative has become more attractive. The ten-year yield is 5.10%, with a 103 basis point curve behind it. With real rates above 5%, an ounce of bullion carries a heavy opportunity cost, and each rise in yields makes it heavier.

The dollar adds to the pressure. It slipped 0.4% this week but is still up 1.6% over twenty days. Metals are priced in dollars, so a firmer currency makes them more expensive for buyers elsewhere. Emerging markets, which are large consumers of both, face a firmer dollar and weaker metals at the same moment.

Oil makes the picture worse. A 9% jump is the input that matters for the long end of the bond market. If higher energy prices keep long yields elevated, they also keep real rates high, and that is the rate that matters most to gold. The US now faces a 5% long bond and an oil shock together. In that setting, a store of value that yields nothing struggles even while equities fall.

The portfolio problem

The lesson for risk managers is about correlation, not about gold's long-run merit. A hedge is useful only if it pays in the scenario it was bought for. Over this month, the scenario was falling technology shares, high real rates and a firm dollar, and the metals lost money in all three. Duration did no better, since a 5.10% ten-year leaves little room for bonds to act as a refuge. The market's reach for safety and oil left growth to pay for it, and the protection that worked came from sectors that are cheap to describe and dull to own.

That narrows the list of dependable hedges to a short one. Staples and energy are equity exposures, so they hedge only against a rotation within stocks, not against a broad sell-off. Investment-grade credit worked this week, but the gap with high yield suggests the buyers were looking for quality, not for protection from rates. None of the three is a clean substitute for the role gold has traditionally played.

What would change the view

The argument is falsifiable. If the equity sell-off deepens and gold rallies alongside it, the metals would be doing their old job and the past month would look like a rates-driven air pocket. A further decline in equities with gold still falling would confirm that the hedge has failed in this regime.

Three readings will decide it. The first is the ten-year yield, which if it holds above 5% keeps the pressure on bullion. The second is the dollar, which needs to stop climbing for metals to stabilise. The third is oil, which feeds directly into the long end. Until at least one of them turns, the 14% fall in silver is better read as a warning about hidden correlation than as a bargain.
