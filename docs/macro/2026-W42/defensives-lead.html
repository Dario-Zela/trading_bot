Staples and health care lead as the market reaches for defensives

In one sentence
Staples rose 5.0% and health care 3.5% in a week, while rate-sensitive real estate and financials lagged, a defensive rotation that now carries crowding risk.

Investors spent the week buying the parts of the market that people usually own for comfort rather than for excitement. Consumer staples, the sector that sells groceries, soap and cigarettes, rose 5.0% over five days, and health care gained 3.5%. Neither group normally leads. When both do, in the same week that technology fell hard, the market is telling a story about risk.

A rotation that has been building

The staples fund XLP gained 5.02% over five days and 5.82% over twenty. Because the 20-day figure is only slightly larger than the 5-day one, most of the month's gain arrived in the past week, but the sector was already rising before then. Health care, tracked by XLV, shows the same pattern: up 3.45% over five days and 4.94% over twenty. Utilities, tracked by XLU, joined more modestly, with +1.93% on the week and +2.70% over the month.

For sectors with low volatility, a 5% weekly move is large. Staples earnings are famously steady, so a move of this size comes from a change in the price investors will pay for that steadiness, not from a change in the outlook for toothpaste.

Not simply a rates trade

The obvious explanation is interest rates. With the ten-year yield at 5.10%, as covered in the Rates desk's account of the steep curve, bond-like equities might be expected to suffer. Yet staples and health care, which pay modest dividends and carry little debt, rose anyway.

The rest of the defensive group points the same way. Utilities, the most rate-sensitive of the three, rose the least. Real estate and financials, the other sectors most exposed to the level and shape of the yield curve, trailed. If investors had been buying whatever benefits from lower yields, real estate would have led. Instead the buying went to companies whose revenue holds up when the economy does not, and the price paid for that protection rose.

That fits the wider picture of the week: investors paid for safety and oil and left growth to cover the cost, with technology down 7.7% and energy extending its gains. Defensives were the quiet side of the same trade, a place to hold capital while the oil shock and the long end of the bond market sort themselves out.

The crowding question

The risk is in the size of the move. A defensive sector that rises 5% in a week is no longer cheap protection. It is a popular position, and popular positions can reverse quickly if the thing they hedge against fades. A rebound in technology, a pullback in oil, or a calmer bond market could send money back the other way, and staples and health care would be where it came from.

Crowding also weakens the hedge itself. Defensives are meant to fall less than the market in a sell-off. After a run like this, they start with more to give back, and a rotation out of them would arrive just as other parts of the market were recovering.

The five-day and twenty-day readings both being strongly positive is the better argument that this is a considered shift and not a one-week scramble. It is also the argument that positioning has had time to build.

What to watch

The test is the next few sessions. If staples hold their gains while technology stabilises, the market is carrying both views at once, which is a sign of unease. If staples give back a large share of the week's move as growth recovers, the rotation was a trade and not a thesis. The path of oil and of the ten-year yield, the two inputs driving the week, will probably decide which.
