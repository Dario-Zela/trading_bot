The dollar slips below 100 — and the usual correlations are not holding

In one sentence
DXY at 99.75 and commodities both falling simultaneously reveals two unrelated unwinds: Iran safe-haven demand deflating the dollar, demand pessimism deflating commodities.

The number everyone is watching this week is 99.75. On Thursday, June 12, the U.S. Dollar Index (DXY) closed at that level — below the psychologically significant 100 mark for the first time in weeks, extending a five-day decline of 0.32%. The threshold matters less than the mechanics behind it: for decades, a sub-100 DXY has reliably accompanied commodity strength. Crude oil, gold, and agricultural products typically rise when the dollar weakens, because a cheaper dollar lowers the effective cost of dollar-denominated commodities for buyers holding other currencies.

That transmission is not working this week. Gold has fallen 2.45% over the past five sessions. Crude oil is down 5.71%. Silver is off 0.45%. The entire commodity complex has declined in lockstep with the dollar — a combination that should not, statistically speaking, coexist.

The explanation is not a breakdown in economic law. It is two separate drivers operating simultaneously, for entirely unrelated reasons.

The safe-haven bid unwinds

The dollar has not been trading like a currency for much of 2026. It has been trading like a crisis asset. When American strikes on Iranian nuclear and military facilities began in March, investors fled to Treasuries and the greenback, pushing DXY into the mid-100s. The logic was standard: a hot war, a potential Strait of Hormuz shutdown, and an open-ended geopolitical disruption all warranted refuge in the world's reserve currency.

What changed this week was the geopolitical calculus. President Trump's decision to halt a third round of strikes on Iran — following reports of a breakthrough in negotiations and signals that Tehran might agree to constrain its nuclear program in exchange for a resumption of shipping through Hormuz — knocked the floor from under safe-haven demand. The dollar is falling not because American economic fundamentals have shifted, but because the perceived tail risk that drove investors into it has diminished. The 20-day DXY return remains positive at +0.88%, a reminder that the broader trend has not reversed, only the most recent impulse.

This distinction matters. An economically motivated dollar decline — falling rates, slowing growth, capital outflows — would typically weaken the greenback while simultaneously bidding up commodities, because both would be responding to the same loosening financial conditions. A geopolitical unwind is different. It is decompression of a risk premium, not a signal about output. The mechanism connecting a weaker dollar to higher commodity prices — improved purchasing power for foreign buyers — operates over weeks and months, not days.

Commodities marching to a different drummer

The commodity complex, meanwhile, has its own problems. Oil's 5.71% five-day decline is not a currency story — it is a demand story. The Energy Information Administration revised its 2026 global oil demand growth forecast from +0.2 million barrels per day to minus 1.1 million b/d: a swing of 1.3 million b/d in a single report cycle. OPEC has downgraded its own demand forecast twice in as many months, with most of the revision concentrated in Asia — the region most exposed to the ongoing disruption of Middle Eastern supply routes.

Gold is telling a similar story in a different vocabulary. The metal had been bid aggressively during the Iran conflict — not primarily as an inflation hedge but as geopolitical insurance. With that premium leaking out as peace talks progress, gold is retreating even as the dollar softens. The two safe-haven trades are unwinding simultaneously, which is why the textbook dollar-gold inverse relationship is absent this week. The commodity complex is falling under the weight of genuine demand pessimism — the kind that a weaker currency cannot, on its own, offset.

That is the crux of the correlation breakdown. The dollar and commodities are both falling, but they are responding to different variables. The dollar is responding to geopolitics. Commodities are responding to growth.

The asymmetry and what comes next

The interesting tension lies ahead. A sustained DXY below 100 normally provides a floor under commodities over medium-term horizons: a cheaper dollar lowers the effective cost of dollar-denominated inputs for non-U.S. buyers, eventually stimulating demand. If the dollar stays soft on the back of geopolitical decompression rather than economic deterioration, that tailwind should begin to register in import volumes over the coming months.

The problem is timing. If the demand deterioration implied by the EIA's revisions is real and sustained, the currency tailwind may not be sufficient to offset it for several quarters. Exchange rate effects percolate through pricing and purchasing decisions over weeks; demand-side contractions can drag for much longer. A floor under commodities is possible — but it may not arrive on the schedule the currency movement suggests.

For emerging-market economies carrying significant dollar-denominated debt, the sub-100 DXY is unambiguously welcome regardless of the commodity picture. It directly lowers the cost of servicing those obligations and widens the space for monetary easing. The relief is likely to show up first in Asian sovereigns carrying both heavy dollar-debt maturity walls and large commodity import bills, who receive the double benefit of cheaper financing and, eventually, cheaper inputs.

The specific catalyst to watch is not the next inflation print — it is the Iran negotiation. A formal ceasefire would drain the remaining safe-haven premium from the dollar and push DXY toward the 97–98 range that prevailed before the conflict began. At that level, the argument for a commodity floor becomes considerably more compelling, and the current correlation breakdown would likely begin to resolve — with the exchange rate and commodity prices moving in their historically expected directions. Until then, the divergence will persist: the dollar falling for one reason, commodities falling for another, and markets waiting to find out which driver runs out of road first.
