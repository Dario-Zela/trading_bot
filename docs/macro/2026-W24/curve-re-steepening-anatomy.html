RATES · YIELD CURVE · By G. Mehlman · 2026-06-14 · 5 min read

The curve has re-steepened to +87 bps — now comes the hard question

The U.S. Treasury Building on Pennsylvania Avenue — the department's auction calendar is a key driver of the long-end yield move. — Rchuon24 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
The 3M-10Y spread has turned positive at +87 bps, ending the inversion signal — but this is a bear steepener driven by a long end approaching 5%, not a bull signal from falling short rates.

For the better part of two years, the U.S. Treasury yield curve hung inverted — an ominous signal that had reliably preceded every American recession since the 1960s. As of June 12, the 3-month/10-year spread closed at +86.9 basis points, the inversion regime is definitively over.

The textbook interpretation is reassuring: a positive curve suggests markets no longer see a near-term downturn as inevitable. But the textbook elides the most important question — why did the curve re-steepen? The answer to that determines whether investors can breathe easier or whether the warning light has simply changed color.

The inversion is over, but a positive spread is not automatically a green light — it depends entirely on which end of the curve is doing the moving.

The anatomy of an uninversion

Yield curves can steepen two ways. In a bull steepener, the front end falls faster than the long end — markets are aggressively pricing Federal Reserve rate cuts, short-term yields collapse, and the spread widens from below. Historically this configuration is associated with a central bank pivoting ahead of economic weakness; it's the curve saying relief is coming.

In a bear steepener, the long end rises faster than the front end. Here the spread widens from above, driven by inflation expectations, supply-demand imbalances in the Treasury market, or mounting concern about the fiscal trajectory. This is a different signal entirely — not relief, but pressure.

The current configuration is unambiguously the second type. The 3-month yield sits at 3.618%, still well inside the Federal Reserve's current 3.50%-3.75% target range — and markets are pricing near-certain odds of no action at the June 16-17 FOMC meeting. The front end has not broken lower in any decisive way. Meanwhile, the 10-year has drifted to 4.487% and the 30-year is closing in on 5%, at 4.975%. The curve is upward-sloping at every node — 3M to 5Y (+59.5 bps), 5Y to 10Y (+27.4 bps), 10Y to 30Y (+49 bps) — and the steepest gradient runs at the long end.

That 49-basis-point gap between the 10-year and 30-year is particularly telling. When the belly and the long end bear the steepening load while the front end barely moves, the curve is telling you that duration risk is being repriced — not that rate cuts are being priced in.

The term premium is the real story

Behind the long-end move sits the term premium: the extra yield investors demand to hold long-dated Treasuries rather than rolling short-term bills indefinitely. For much of the post-2009 era, the term premium was compressed, even negative — the Fed's balance sheet was large, global safe-asset demand was fierce, and the fiscal situation, while never comfortable, was not at the front of the conversation.

That has changed. The May federal budget deficit came in at $293 billion, widening from the year-earlier pace, and the Congressional Budget Office has flagged an additional $4.1 trillion in projected deficit over the next decade from legislative changes enacted in 2025. U.S. debt-to-GDP is running near 124%. The Treasury has responded with heavier auction calendars; the market has responded by demanding more yield for the privilege of absorbing that supply.

This is the mechanism driving the 30-year toward 5%: not exuberant growth optimism, not a Fed that is about to loosen dramatically, but a buyer base that is running the numbers on issuance volume and deciding it needs to be paid more to hold the long end.

For risk assets, that is a meaningfully less comfortable backdrop than a bull-steepener equivalent would be. A falling front end loosens financial conditions — mortgages cheap, corporate borrowing costs easing. A rising long end does the opposite: it lifts discount rates on equities, especially growth-oriented ones; it widens spreads, at least at the margin; it competes with dividend yields on long-duration income instruments. That the investment-grade credit market has recently outrun high yield on a total-return basis — a gap worth watching in its own right — is consistent with a market quietly repricing the cost of duration risk without fully sounding the alarm.

What the signal actually cleared

It would be wrong to overstate the gloom. The uninversion of the 3M-10Y spread does remove the most prominent mechanical recession indicator from the dashboard. Inversions are leading indicators — once they un-invert, the recession signal is extinguished, even if economic conditions remain uncertain. The curve is not screaming contraction; it is expressing something more nuanced about where the equilibrium rate should sit and what compensation bondholders require at the margin.

Persistent inflation, with headline CPI running at 4.2% year-on-year through May, has kept the Fed on hold and kept the 3-month yield anchored near current levels. The front end will only fall decisively if inflation cracks or the labor market softens materially — neither of which has occurred. Until that happens, a bull steepener remains a scenario, not a reality, and the present bear configuration stays in place.

The catalyst that could shift the diagnosis is clear: any convincing move lower in CPI that reopens the rate-cut conversation would begin pulling the front end down and converting the dynamic. Conversely, continued fiscal expansion or heavy Treasury supply without a corresponding increase in foreign demand could push the 30-year cleanly through 5% and widen the long-end spread further — raising discount rates and complicating valuations that have been quietly stress-tested by the move already underway.

The next meaningful data points — the June CPI release in mid-July and the Treasury's quarterly refunding announcement in early August — will either confirm the bear-steepener thesis or begin to complicate it. For now, the curve has returned to its normal slope. The question is whether normal feels, under the surface, anything like reassuring.
