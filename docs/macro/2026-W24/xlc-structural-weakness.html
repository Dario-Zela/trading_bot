SECTORS · COMMUNICATION SERVICES · By I. Aresti · 2026-06-14 · 3 min read

Communication services sat out the rally — and has been sitting it out for a month

Meta Platforms headquarters in Menlo Park, California — alongside Alphabet, it accounts for roughly 38% of XLC's weight and is central to the sector's underperformance. — LPS.1 / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
XLC's near-flat week in a broadly rallying tape extends a -4.66% 20-day drawdown driven by capex anxiety, legal overhang, and ad-cycle risk concentrated in Meta and Alphabet.

Last week's market rally had eleven sectors. Ten of them moved higher. Communication Services — tracked by XLC — did not. The fund managed a return of -0.018% over five trading sessions, a near-perfect standstill in a tape where everything from Financials to Materials posted gains. On its own, that is a curiosity. Stretched across twenty days, it becomes a structural story.

XLC's 20-day return is -4.66%, the worst reading in the sector table by a margin of more than one percentage point. Technology (XLK) posted +2.95% over the same period; Financials (XLF) returned +3.99%. Both sectors have absorbed capital that left Communication Services. The dispersion is not noise — it reflects a sector dealing simultaneously with three identifiable pressures, none of which resolved last week.

The capex trap, the courtroom, and the ad cycle

Communication Services is, for practical purposes, a two-company story. Meta Platforms and Alphabet together account for roughly 38% of XLC's weight. Both reported strong first-quarter numbers in late April — Meta's revenue rose 33%, Alphabet's 22%, with Google Search advertising up 19% — yet the post-earnings lift proved short-lived. The reason: investors are increasingly focused not on what these companies earned, but on what they have committed to spend. Meta guided 2026 capital expenditure at $115–135 billion, more than double its 2025 outlay of $69.7 billion. Alphabet's guidance sits at $175–185 billion, up from $91.5 billion a year earlier. Combined, the two companies are on track to deploy more than $300 billion on AI infrastructure in a single year. Free cash flow compresses accordingly, and the returns remain, for now, theoretical.

The capex story sits alongside a legal one. In late March, a Los Angeles jury found Meta and Alphabet liable for negligent platform design in cases involving social media addiction among minors — a verdict that deliberately sidestepped the Section 230 protections companies have historically relied on, by framing the claim around algorithmic engineering rather than content. If that liability framework is upheld on appeal or replicated in other jurisdictions, the implications for ad-targeting efficiency and platform design are significant. Investors have not dismissed it.

Underneath both of those concerns runs a simpler cyclical one. Digital advertising revenue lags consumer sentiment by roughly one to three months. Consumer confidence indices have been sitting near recessionary thresholds for much of the spring. Alphabet's advertising beat in Q1 was partly driven by financial services and retail verticals; those verticals are sensitive to the macro picture. If the soft-landing trade falters — and commodity markets have been raising that question all week — the ad revenue cycle becomes a much more active risk for XLC than it looked at earnings.

For strategies benchmarked to the S&P 500, the extended underperformance of a high-weight sector is not a passive problem. Communication Services is large enough to drag index returns meaningfully, and at -4.66% over twenty days, it has been doing exactly that while growth capital found better homes in XLK and XLF. The question for the week ahead is whether the Q2 advertising data, when it begins to filter through, corroborates the Q1 strength or starts to show the consumer-sentiment lag. That, more than anything else on the sector calendar, is the number to watch.
