Europe enters the summer with two of its biggest headwinds fading

In one sentence
Oil's 12% slide and a softer dollar are removing Europe's two main cost headwinds — but the prize depends on whether the commodity collapse is supply relief or demand alarm.

For most of the past eighteen months, two constants have dragged on European growth: expensive energy and a strong dollar that kept import costs elevated and the ECB pinned in a defensive posture. This week, both are easing simultaneously — and the timing is significant.

Crude oil, measured by the USO benchmark, has fallen 12.3% over the past twenty trading days. The drop traces directly to progress on a US-Iran ceasefire that is pulling the on-off closure of the Strait of Hormuz toward resolution. Europe imports roughly half its oil and a substantial share of its gas; a sustained move lower in oil cuts the energy portion of the import bill materially. As of this week, Brent crude sat around $86 per barrel — down from a May average of $107, itself the product of the Middle East flare-up that began in February.

Natural gas tells a more cautious story. UNG fell 2.74% on the week but is still up 1.70% over twenty days, which means energy disinflation is oil-led rather than a clean across-the-board retreat. European consumers will notice the difference: gas feeds residential heating and industrial process energy in ways that oil does not. The energy relief, for now, is real but partial.

The dollar adds a second tailwind

The other relief valve is the dollar. DXY at 99.75 — down roughly ten points from its January 2025 peak above 109 — gives the euro modest but meaningful room. A weaker dollar reduces the cost of dollar-denominated commodity imports, compresses one channel of imported inflation, and takes some pressure off the ECB's next move.

That matters because the ECB just moved in the opposite direction. On June 11, it raised its deposit rate to 2.25% — the first increase since 2023 — after eurozone inflation hit 3.2% in May, its highest reading in nearly three years. The hike was a direct response to the energy spike. If oil prices hold at current levels or continue lower, the ECB's June statement — which tied future decisions explicitly to incoming energy and inflation data — gives it room to pause or pivot. Lagarde said as much: the path depends on the data.

The June manufacturing PMI reading of 51.6, still in expansion though slightly below May's 52.2, matters here as a live check. An index above 50 is not consistent with the demand destruction story that would make falling commodity prices a warning rather than a relief.

The risk the market is pricing around

The benign read has a coherent rival. If commodity prices are falling not because Hormuz is reopening but because global demand is weakening, Europe's export sector is the next to feel it. The continent runs a large current account that is sensitive to external demand — particularly from the US and China — and a demand-shock commodity collapse would hurt more than cheap oil helps.

For now the evidence tilts toward supply relief rather than demand collapse. A ceasefire process is visibly driving the oil move; the PMI has not broken; and the dollar's fall reflects US policy uncertainty rather than a global growth signal. That is a reasonably favourable configuration for the euro area as it heads into summer.

The catalysts to watch: the next flash PMI release in late June, any breakdown in Iran ceasefire talks, and whether ECB rhetoric begins shifting toward a pause. The first data point that contradicts the supply-relief story will matter significantly.
