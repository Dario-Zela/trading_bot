The bear steepener deserves a seat at the table this week

In one sentence
The curve's re-steepening to +87 bps looks like a soft-landing signal, but the mechanics point to a bear steepener — raising discount rates on REITs and long-duration assets without the growth payoff.

The US Treasury curve has done something it hadn't managed in more than two years. The 3-month-to-10-year spread crossed back into positive territory and kept widening — sitting at +87 basis points as of mid-June — and the narrative that moved fastest through markets was the comforting one: inversion over, soft landing intact, time to rotate into rate-sensitive sectors.

There is a second reading. And the 30-year Treasury yield, sitting at 4.975% as of June 12, suggests it deserves more than a footnote.

The Steepener That Isn't What It Looks Like

Yield curves steepen in two distinct ways. A bull steepener is the friendly version: the Fed cuts, short rates fall, and investors pile into growth and real estate on expectations of easier conditions ahead. A bear steepener is the opposite — the front end stays pinned while the long end drifts higher, raising the discount rate on long-duration assets without the earnings acceleration that would justify current multiples.

The mechanics of the current move point toward the bear variety. The 3-month bill, at 3.618%, has not moved decisively lower. FOMC minutes from April and May confirm what rate markets have already priced: the Fed's 3.50–3.75% policy band is likely to stay in place through the end of 2026 and into 2027. The front end is sticky because the Fed is not cutting.

The long end is drifting higher for different reasons — structural fiscal deficits, elevated Treasury issuance, and a term premium that turned positive for the first time since 2023. The NY Fed's ACM model decomposed the 10-year yield in late April into 3.72% expected short-rate path and 0.73% term premium. That extra compensation is not a growth signal. It reflects investors demanding more to absorb supply from a Treasury that issues more paper as the deficit widens.

Real Estate Is Priced for the Bull Version

The Real Estate Select Sector SPDR ETF has gained 3.3% over the past 20 sessions. That kind of move fits a bull steepener perfectly — the trade where falling short rates improve REIT financing costs, compress cap rates, and lift property valuations. It is not the trade that belongs in a bear steepener.

When the long end rises without the front end falling, the discount rate on real estate cash flows goes up with nothing to offset it. Utilities and long-duration growth names face the same arithmetic: higher denominator, same numerator, lower present value. The market has repriced REITs as if relief is coming. The rate structure does not support that reading.

The 30-year Treasury at 4.975% is the stress signal hiding in plain sight. The long end is already threatening the 5% threshold that has historically produced cross-asset turbulence. If the 10-year continues its drift toward 4.75–5.00% on term premium and supply dynamics rather than strong growth data, XLRE's 20-day gains face a swift reversal.

The next test arrives with Thursday's retail sales print and the following week's Treasury auction schedule — two near-term events that will either validate or challenge how the market is reading this steepening.
