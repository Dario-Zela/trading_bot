Front end still banks on cuts the long end doesn't buy

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A 3.76% three-month bill still bets on Fed cuts that a 5.25% thirty-year — and a 9.5% weekly oil surge — increasingly resist.

The three-month Treasury bill is telling a different story than the thirty-year bond, and this week the gap between them widened rather than closed.

The bill yields 3.757%, just above the effective federal funds rate of roughly 3.63% and the top of the Fed's 3.50%–3.75% target range. The thirty-year, by contrast, has pushed out to 5.246%, and the ten-year sits at 4.784%. That is a curve built on two different forecasts: the front end pricing a central bank still inclined to ease, the long end pricing a central bank that may not get the chance.

A cutting bias, on paper

The FOMC held rates steady in January by a 10-2 vote, with Governors Christopher Waller and Stephen Miran dissenting in favor of a cut. Miran has been explicit about why: labor-market softness, in his view, "requires the central bank to slash at least four additional 25 basis-point cuts" this year. He has also been explicit about dismissing the counter-argument — calling oil-driven inflation a typical "one-off shock," not a reason to hold the line. Waller has echoed the dovish lean, signaling support for no hike even as hawks inside the building push back. Futures markets, per CME's FedWatch tracking, are not pricing a cut before the October meeting, but the front end's proximity to the top of the current range still reflects a market that expects the next move to be down, not up, once that meeting arrives.

The energy problem the doves are betting against

Set against that is a 9.45% five-day surge in USO, the oil tracking fund, and a 20.3% gain over twenty trading days — a move tied directly to the resumption of U.S.-Iran strikes for the first time in roughly a month, according to reporting on the escalation. WTI has traded near $91 a barrel, within reach of levels last seen during the 2022 energy shock. It is not an isolated move: natural gas, via the UNG fund, is up 2.23% over five days and 8.42% over twenty, suggesting the inflation impulse is broadening across the energy complex rather than sitting in crude alone. Fund manager surveys have picked this up quickly — one recent poll found 45% now expect higher global inflation over the next year, up from just 9% a month earlier.

That is precisely the scenario Miran's "one-off shock" framing is betting will not matter. If it is wrong — if the energy move sits in headline inflation and then in expectations long enough to shift consumer and business behavior — the case for four more cuts gets harder to make, and the front end has further to reprice than the long end does.

Why this would flatten, not steepen

This week's selloff has been a long-end story: the thirty-year breaking 5.25% on term-premium concerns, not on a repricing of near-term Fed policy. A front-end repricing would work differently. If three-month and six-month yields climb toward — or through — the belly of the curve as cut expectations get pared back, the curve flattens from the middle rather than steepening from the long end. That is a distinct mechanism from what has driven yields this week, and it would arrive for a different reason: not a bigger term premium, but a smaller rate-cut discount.

The next real test is the October FOMC meeting, where futures currently price the first plausible cut. Between now and then, the relevant data is less the jobs report than the energy tape — how long oil holds above $90, whether the natural-gas move keeps broadening, and whether breakevens follow. Miran's bet is that they won't. The thirty-year yield says the bond market isn't so sure.
