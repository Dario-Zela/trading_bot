Oil's surge splits emerging markets into winners and losers

An ultra-large crude carrier moves through the Persian Gulf — the kind of tanker traffic now snarled by the US-Iran conflict near the Strait of Hormuz. — Photographer's Mate 1st Class Kevin H. Tierney, U.S. Navy / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
A 9.45% weekly oil spike from the Iran conflict is enriching exporters like Colombia, Mexico and Nigeria while punishing importers like India, Turkey and Indonesia — even as a weaker dollar should help both.

Crude oil's sharpest weekly rally since July is doing something a simple dollar chart cannot: splitting emerging markets into two camps that are having completely different weeks.

The US dollar index slipped 0.54% over five sessions, the kind of move that normally lifts every emerging-market currency at once by easing the cost of dollar debt and making local assets more attractive. But this dollar move is arriving alongside a 9.45% surge in crude — WTI's biggest weekly gain since mid-July — after Iran fired on Kuwait on September 3 in retaliation for earlier US strikes, and fighting along the Strait of Hormuz cut into tanker traffic. Commercial US crude stocks fell to 424.5 million barrels and refinery runs hit 98% utilization, the highest since 2018, while diesel touched a record $5.82 a gallon. For oil producers, that is a windfall. For importers, it's an inflation problem arriving at the worst possible moment.

The exporters are having a good week

Colombia's peso strengthened 1.22% to around 3,169 per dollar this week alone, extending a run that has it up roughly 19% for the year — one of the best performances of any currency globally — on the back of oil and coal export earnings, record remittances, and a central bank holding its policy rate at 12%. Mexico's peso touched 16.89 per dollar, its strongest level since May 2024, helped by a hefty interest-rate differential with the Federal Reserve as well as firmer crude. Nigeria's naira is on pace for its best annual performance since 2018, up close to 12% this year, as Bonny Light crude jumped more than 11% and foreign reserves climbed past $54 billion, an 18-year high, on the back of hydrocarbon revenue and remittance inflows.

These are the textbook beneficiaries of a terms-of-trade shock: countries that sell oil abroad get paid more for the same barrels, their current accounts improve, and their currencies catch a bid even before the weaker-dollar tailwind is added on top.

The importers are not

The same rally that cushions Bogotá and Lagos is squeezing Mumbai, Ankara and Jakarta. The Indian rupee has been pinned near record lows around 95.4 per dollar, with traders pointing directly at renewed US-Iran hostilities and India's dependence on imports for more than 85% of its crude needs. Turkey's annual inflation, already running above 31%, is being kept there in part by energy costs tied to the Strait of Hormuz conflict, even as the lira remains under structural pressure. Indonesia's rupiah has slipped toward 17,780 per dollar, with a jump in fuel imports and accelerating core inflation — the highest since March 2023 — doing the damage. For all three, a softer dollar should be arriving as relief; instead it's being overwhelmed by a bigger, oil-shaped headwind pointed the other way.

A supply story, not a demand story

What makes this an oil story specifically, rather than a broader emerging-market commodity boom, is the base-metals numbers. DBB, a proxy for industrial metals like copper and aluminum, is up just 1.06% over five days and 1.50% over the month — barely moving while crude has spiked nearly 10% in a week. If this were a story about surging Chinese or global industrial demand, metals would be moving with oil, not lagging far behind it. Instead, the gap points to a supply shock centered on the Strait of Hormuz, a chokepoint through which roughly a fifth of the world's oil transits, rather than a strengthening growth outlook that would lift commodity-sensitive EM currencies broadly.

That distinction matters for how long the split lasts. A demand-driven commodity boom tends to lift exporters and, eventually, importers too, as global growth broadens out. A war-premium supply shock does the opposite: it transfers income from importers to exporters with no offsetting growth benefit, and it can reverse just as fast as it arrived if the Strait reopens or a ceasefire holds. Traders have already seen that pattern once this year, when a fragile truce briefly knocked Brent back toward $72 before the latest escalation reversed it.

The next test is whether the US-Iran exchanges keep escalating or find another lull. A durable ceasefire would unwind the oil premium quickly and hand the dollar-weakness tailwind back to importers like India, Turkey and Indonesia in full. A prolonged shutdown of Hormuz shipping lanes would do the opposite, deepening the divide and forcing central banks in oil-importing EMs to choose between defending their currencies and supporting growth already showing signs of softening.
