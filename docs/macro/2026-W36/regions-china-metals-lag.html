Base metals' muted response says China demand isn't the story

Chuquicamata, one of the world's largest copper mines, seen from the International Space Station. — Alexander Gerst / ESA, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Base metals rose just 1.1% while oil surged 9.5% on Hormuz tensions — a gap that says this is a supply shock, not a China demand rebound.

Oil is having the kind of week that usually drags the rest of the commodity complex along with it. Crude jumped roughly 9.5% as US-Iran tensions flared over the Strait of Hormuz, with reported strikes on tankers cutting the waterway's throughput by nearly half. That is the sort of move that, in a China-reflation story, would show up everywhere — in copper, in steel, in the mining equities that live and die by Chinese construction cycles. It hasn't.

The Invesco DB Base Metals Fund (DBB), a proxy for copper, aluminum and zinc, is up just 1.06% over five days and 1.5% over twenty. Materials-sector equities, tracked by the XLB, are actually down — off 1.39% on the week and 0.79% over the month. If oil's surge were a signal that global industrial demand is reaccelerating, base metals would be the confirming witness. Instead they are sitting this one out, and that gap is the more interesting story than the oil headline itself.

A supply story, not a demand story

The distinction matters because oil and copper respond to different things. Oil this week moved on a geography problem: roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne crude passes through the Strait of Hormuz, and renewed US-Iran hostilities put a chokepoint at risk, not a demand forecast. Copper, by contrast, is the more reliable industrial-demand bellwether precisely because it has no equivalent chokepoint story — it responds to what factories, grids and builders are actually ordering.

And what factories are ordering, in China at least, remains ambiguous. The official NBS manufacturing PMI came in at 49.8 in August, its second straight month below the 50 expansion line, even as the privately compiled Caixin index — which skews toward smaller, export-oriented firms — climbed to 51.5. Copper's own price action tells a similarly split story: prices are still about 47% higher than a year ago, but have drifted down roughly 1.6% over the past month even as LME warehouse stocks swung sharply, first collapsing on a supply squeeze earlier in the year, then jumping by more than 35,000 tonnes in mid-August as metal flowed back into exchange warehouses. Some of that stock movement reflects US tariff positioning on refined copper rather than anything about end demand in China.

Why the gap matters for reading the tape

The temptation, when oil rips, is to fold it into a broader narrative — global growth is reaccelerating, China stimulus is finally landing, reflation is back. Base metals are the natural cross-check for that story, because they have no direct exposure to Middle East shipping lanes and a very direct exposure to Chinese construction and manufacturing output. Their muted response argues that this week's move belongs to the supply-and-geopolitics column, not the global-demand column. Materials equities underperforming even as headline commodity indices climb reinforces the same read: investors pricing mining and chemicals stocks are not treating the oil spike as good news for their order books.

None of this rules out a genuine China-led industrial rebound later in the year — the Caixin PMI's strength and structural copper demand from grid buildout and EVs are real countervailing forces. But for now, anyone using this week's oil rally as evidence of a broad reflation trade should note that the metal most sensitive to that exact thesis barely moved. The next test comes with China's September PMI prints in early October, and with whatever the Strait of Hormuz situation looks like by then — if tensions ease and oil gives back its gains while copper holds steady, that will confirm the supply-driven read rather than undercut it.
