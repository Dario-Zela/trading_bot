Long bond breaks 5.25% as term premium reasserts itself

The U.S. Treasury Building in Washington, D.C. — where debt-management decisions now shape the long end of the curve as much as Fed policy does. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The 30-year yield's break above 5.25% while bills sit near 3.75% and the dollar softens points to rising term premium and Treasury supply, not a hawkish Fed repricing.

The bond market delivered a clean, legible signal this week, and it wasn't the one the Federal Reserve was hoping to send. The 30-year Treasury yield pushed through 5.25%, a level that would have seemed exotic before 2007 and has become almost routine since midsummer. What makes this move worth stopping for isn't the round number — it's the shape of the curve underneath it.

Line up the four points and the story tells itself: a three-month bill at 3.757%, a five-year note at 4.550%, a ten-year at 4.784%, and the long bond at 5.246%. The front end has barely budged in recent weeks. The back end has not stopped moving. The 3-month/10-year spread now sits at roughly 1.03 percentage points, and — more tellingly — the gap between the ten-year and the thirty-year has widened to around 46 basis points, meaning more of the curve's steepening is happening beyond ten years than within it.

Not a Fed story

The textbook version of a steepening curve is a bear steepener driven by growth optimism or a hawkish Fed: short rates anchored near policy, long rates rising because the market expects the economy — and eventually the funds rate — to run hotter. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh gave that narrative some fuel at Jackson Hole in late August, warning that inflation progress had stalled and that the central bank might have "work to do," language that pushed the market-implied odds of a September rate hike from roughly one-in-three to better than even. A stronger-than-expected August payrolls report — 162,000 jobs against forecasts near 56,000 — added to the hawkish repricing on Friday.

If that were the whole story, the dollar should be rising with yields: higher expected policy rates typically pull in foreign capital and strengthen the currency. Instead, the dollar has been sliding through the same stretch that the long end sold off, a divergence that shows up clearly on the DXY charts of the past week. That combination — long yields up, dollar down — is not the fingerprint of a market repricing Fed hawkishness. It is closer to the fingerprint of a market demanding more compensation to hold duration, full stop, regardless of what the Fed does next.

Term premium, not growth premium

Strip out inflation expectations and the expected path of short rates, and what's left in a long bond's yield is the term premium — the extra return investors require simply for the risk of holding a security decades from maturity. Estimates of the ten-year term premium have risen from below 0.50% earlier this year to a range closer to 0.80%–1.35%, according to strategist estimates circulating this week, a level not seen in roughly a decade. The proximate cause is supply. Interest payments on the national debt reached $857 billion in the first nine months of fiscal 2026, up 13% year-over-year and now exceeding both Medicare and defense outlays, while the Treasury continues issuing at a pace the market has to absorb without a correspondingly deep bid from price-insensitive buyers — foreign central banks and, until recently, the Fed itself, have both stepped back.

Treasury's own actions this week underline the point. The department announced it would double the size of its liquidity-support buybacks in the 10-to-30-year sector, from a maximum of $2 billion to at least $4 billion per operation, effective September 9 and running through the November refunding announcement. Treasury framed the move as a response to "strong sponsorship" in prior operations — official language for a long end that needs propping up. That's a debt manager treating the back of the curve as a standalone liquidity problem, separate from whatever the Fed does with overnight rates.

A self-reinforcing loop

The uncomfortable feature of a supply-driven term premium is that it feeds itself. Higher long yields raise the government's own borrowing costs, which widens the deficit, which requires more issuance, which — absent a matching increase in demand — presses yields higher still. Economists have taken to calling this dynamic "fiscal primacy": a regime in which the trajectory of government debt, not the central bank's reaction function, becomes the dominant force setting long-term borrowing costs. Insurance companies and pension funds, which need long-dated assets to match long-dated liabilities, have reportedly begun stepping in as buyers near 5.25% — a sign the market is finding a clearing price, but also a reminder that it's structural hedgers, not momentum buyers, doing the work of putting a floor under the market.

None of this makes a near-term Fed decision irrelevant. A September rate move, whichever direction, will still shift the front end and could nudge the 3-month/10-year spread. But it would do comparatively little to the ten-year/thirty-year gap, which is the part of the curve currently doing the most work — and the part that has the least to do with the Fed. The more useful things to watch from here are the November quarterly refunding statement, where Treasury will signal whether coupon auction sizes finally rise after holding steady through 2026, and whether foreign official buyers show up in the next few 30-year auctions' indirect-bidder statistics. Both will say more about where 5.25% goes next than anything coming out of the FOMC.
