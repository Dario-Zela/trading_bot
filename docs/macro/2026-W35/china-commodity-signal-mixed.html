China's commodity signal is muddled: metals flat, energy fading, ag firm

China's soybean purchases are the one direct data point behind this week's agricultural rally. — United Soybean Board / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Flat metals, falling oil and rallying grains send conflicting signals on Chinese demand, with Sunday's PMI print the next real test.

China does not publish a single number that tells traders how its economy is doing this week. Instead, the read comes secondhand — through the commodities its factories, builders and importers actually consume. This week that secondhand read is a muddle: industrial metals are flat, oil is falling, and farm goods are rallying hard. Put together, they do not tell a coherent story about Chinese demand so much as three different stories that happen to share a border.

Start with the metal that is supposed to be the cleanest proxy. DBB, the base-metals fund tracking copper, aluminium and zinc, slipped 0.27% over five days, though it is still up 1.27% over the past month. Copper alone is instructive because of how concentrated its demand is: China accounts for roughly half to sixty percent of global copper consumption, tying the metal's price more tightly to Chinese factory activity than almost any other commodity. China's official manufacturing PMI came in at 49.2 in July, a 1.1-point drop that pushed the gauge deeper into contraction across firms of every size. Refined copper consumption kept growing into midsummer, but with downstream demand in its seasonal slack period and prices already elevated, analysts expect August consumption to ease at the margin. The August PMI print lands Sunday, and it will either confirm that softening or complicate it — for now, flat is about the most honest description available.

Oil's decline is not really a China story

USO fell 3.67% over the week, the sharpest move of the three funds, but the drivers trace back to Vienna and Paris more than Beijing. The IEA cut its 2026 demand growth forecast by another 510,000 barrels a day in its August report, pushing the expected slowdown to 1.6 million barrels a day, while OPEC trimmed its own demand growth estimate to 580,000 barrels a day from 780,000 the month before. A separate, blunter number did more damage to sentiment: U.S. crude inventories jumped by 17.4 million barrels in the week ending August 7, a build large enough to overshadow the roughly 8.3 million barrels a day of Gulf output still shut in by regional supply disruptions. None of that is a Chinese signal specifically — it is a global demand markdown plus an American storage glut. Oil's weakness this week says more about the world outside China than inside it.

Agriculture's rally has a Chinese fingerprint, but not only that

DBA is up 3.07% for the week and 6.11% over the month, the clearest directional move of the group — and the one with the most direct evidence of Chinese buying behind it. New-crop U.S. soybean sales jumped to nearly 2.5 million metric tons as China loaded up on cargoes, a concrete data point that the other two commodities lack entirely. But the rally is not a China story alone. Wheat has led the move higher on concerns about Black Sea shipping disruptions tied to the war in Ukraine, and corn and soybean futures got an additional lift when the USDA cut its yield estimates more than expected after a hot, dry Midwest summer damaged the crop. Strip out the weather and the war, and the Chinese purchasing is real support — just not the whole explanation for a three-week high in corn.

What the mix actually says

Taken individually, each commodity has a story that only partly involves China: metals are waiting on a PMI print, oil is reacting to global inventory and demand-forecast shifts, and grains are being driven by war risk and weather as much as by Chinese buyers. Taken together, though, there is a modest signal worth noting — the one direct data point available, Chinese soybean purchasing, points toward steady rather than collapsing import demand, even as the PMI print suggests factory activity is still contracting. That is consistent with an economy leaning more on consumption and food security than on industrial throughput, but it is a thin reed to hang a growth call on.

The next real test arrives Sunday, when China's August manufacturing PMI is released. A move back above the 50 line, or a further slide from July's 49.2, would do more to settle this week's mixed commodity signal than another five days of price action in DBB, USO or DBA.
