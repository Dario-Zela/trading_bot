The dollar's sharpest weekly bounce in a month — bear rally or reversal?

Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh, whose hawkish Jackson Hole remarks lifted rate-hike odds and the dollar. — Federal Reserve / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
DXY's 0.91% weekly pop, sparked by Warsh's hawkish Jackson Hole speech, looks as much like record-short covering as a genuine reversal.

The dollar just had its best week in a month, and it happened for a reason that should worry anyone betting against it: the bond market stopped giving the Federal Reserve the benefit of the doubt.

The ICE Dollar Index closed at 99.70 on Friday, up 0.91% over five trading days — its sharpest weekly advance since late July. Zoom out and the picture is less flattering: over twenty days the index is still down 0.10%, meaning this week's bounce did little more than erase a month of drift. The question dominating FX desks is whether that's the start of something or the top of it.

What actually moved

The proximate cause is easy to name. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh used his first Jackson Hole keynote, delivered Friday, to push back hard on the idea that inflation is cooling enough to justify easier policy, saying markets were "showing few signs of policy restraint." Traders took the hint: odds of a 25-basis-point hike at the September 16 meeting jumped from roughly 35% earlier in the week to above 56% in Fed funds futures within minutes of the speech.

That repricing showed up first and hardest at the long end. The 30-year Treasury yield reached 5.206%, extending a bear-steepening move that has been building since a $25 billion 30-year auction earlier in the month priced at the highest yield since 2001 (see [[curve-bear-steepens]] and [[us-term-premium-center-stage]]). Rising long yields alone don't necessarily help the dollar — if anything, a term-premium-driven selloff can spook risk assets and drag currencies down with them. What moved the dollar was that Warsh's comments raised the odds of Fed action across the whole curve, not just at the thirty-year point, widening the rate-differential case for holding dollars against the euro and yen just as the European Central Bank and Bank of Japan give no comparable signal. EUR/USD slid to seven-day lows below 1.1600 on the week; the yen held closer to 159 per dollar, cushioned by its own rate story — markets are pricing an 87% chance the BOJ hikes in September.

The case for a squeeze, not a reversal

The timing invites a less flattering explanation: positioning. Heading into this week, speculative bets against the dollar were, by some measures, the most one-sided in fourteen years — a hangover from a year in which the index touched five-year lows around the January FOMC meeting and never mounted a convincing recovery. That kind of crowding makes a rally mechanically easier to produce, because it doesn't take much genuinely new information to force short-covering once a catalyst appears. A Warsh speech that moves futures pricing by twenty points in an afternoon is exactly the kind of catalyst that turns a one-sided book into a scramble.

There's also a recent precedent for how fast this can run in reverse. Less than two weeks earlier, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent's announcement that the government would at least double the size of long-dated bond buybacks briefly did the opposite trade — it pulled long yields down and handed the dollar its worst single session in nearly three weeks. The index went from a supply-driven selloff to a hawkish-Fed rally in the space of ten trading days without any change in the underlying growth or inflation data. That volatility is itself evidence that the market is trading policy noise, not a settled repricing of relative rates.

And the hawkish read on Warsh is not unanimous. Adam Posen of the Peterson Institute argued the speech was notable for what it left out — "accountability, credibility, and responsibility" — criticizing the chair's retreat from forward guidance. Michael Strain at AEI put it more bluntly: the speech "raises the stakes for the September meeting" without necessarily resolving anything, and it followed a July decision that produced the most FOMC dissents since 1970. A Fed this divided is not a Fed markets should assume will actually deliver the hike now priced at better than even odds.

What would settle it

A durable reversal needs the rate-hike odds to survive contact with actual data, not just a speech. The next PCE inflation print and the September jobs report, both due before the FOMC meeting, are the real tests: if core inflation holds or accelerates and payrolls don't crack, a September hike becomes a live outcome rather than a 56% coin flip, and the dollar's rate advantage becomes structural rather than a one-week repricing. Confirmation would also show up as the rally broadening beyond a knee-jerk yield move — the dollar gaining against the yen and Swiss franc as well as the euro, and holding gains even on days when Treasury yields pause or pull back, evidence buyers are backing the currency rather than just the spread.

Falsification is just as identifiable. If the 30-year yield's climb stalls — plausible given how aggressively the market has already repriced, and given that heavy Treasury issuance eventually needs buyers at some price — the relative-rate story loses its engine. Watch too for the FOMC dissent pattern: another meeting that splits three ways or more suggests Warsh cannot deliver the hike the market now expects, in which case this week's short-covering reverses as fast as it arrived, and the index heads back toward the 97 support level it was defending as recently as three weeks ago. For a market that spent most of 2026 pressing the dollar lower, one hawkish Friday is a real data point. It is not yet a trend.
