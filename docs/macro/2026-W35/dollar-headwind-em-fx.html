A firmer dollar is a fresh headwind for EM currencies

A stronger dollar raises the cost of servicing dollar debt and importing goods across emerging markets. — Milad Mosapoor / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
The dollar's post-Jackson Hole bounce is tightening conditions on emerging markets just as several of their central banks look to keep cutting rates.

The dollar had a good week, and for a stretch of the emerging world, that is not good news.

The DXY dollar index rose 0.91% over five sessions, its firmest run in a month, after Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh used his debut Jackson Hole keynote on August 28 to warn that policymakers may still have "work to do" on inflation. Markets read that as an opening for a rate hike rather than a cut. Odds of a 25-basis-point increase at the September Fed meeting jumped to roughly 55% from about 35% before the speech, according to CME FedWatch pricing, and the dollar caught a bid across the board.

For most emerging-market currencies, a firmer dollar is a mechanical headwind: local currency weakens, dollar-denominated debt gets costlier to service, and imported goods — fuel, machinery, anything priced in dollars — get more expensive at the till. This week that mechanism was already visible. The South Korean won, the Hungarian forint and the Brazilian real were among the week's bigger losers, and the South African rand and Indian rupee also slipped, per Bloomberg's tracking of EM currency moves.

The timing problem

What makes this bounce awkward isn't its size — 0.91% is a routine weekly move — but its timing. A number of EM central banks are mid-cycle on rate cuts, having spent much of 2026 unwinding the high policy rates they built up fighting their own inflation. Brazil's Copom has trimmed the Selic rate in four straight 25-basis-point steps since March, taking it from 15% to 14% as of its August 5 meeting, and left the door open — pointedly not closed — to another cut in September. A stronger dollar complicates that calculus: it pushes imported inflation higher just as a central bank is trying to demonstrate inflation is beaten, and it makes local-currency assets less attractive at the margin, which can leak back out as further currency weakness. Analysts at asset managers including State Street and J.P. Morgan have flagged this same dynamic — that EM central banks are only roughly halfway through their easing cycles and now have to weigh Fed-driven dollar strength against domestic room to keep cutting.

What commodities are — and aren't — saying

One would expect a stronger dollar to flatten commodity prices, since most are dollar-priced and a pricier dollar means less can be bought per unit. Base metals mostly obliged: the DBB base-metals basket was roughly flat, down 0.27% over the same five days, a muted reaction that suggests industrial demand expectations are doing more work than the currency move. Agriculture did not cooperate at all — the DBA basket rose 3.07%, bucking the strong-dollar drag entirely, likely on crop-specific supply factors rather than anything to do with the Fed. That divergence matters for EM inflation math: food and fuel are the two imported-inflation channels that hit household budgets fastest, and right now one is firming for reasons that have nothing to do with monetary policy.

None of this amounts to a currency crisis. A single-week, sub-1% dollar move is not the stuff of 2013's taper tantrum. But it is a reminder that EM disinflation was always partly a function of a Fed that was expected to ease, and Warsh's Jackson Hole remarks just reopened the question of whether that assumption holds. The next test lands with the September Fed meeting and, a few weeks later, Copom's own decision — with the real's behavior in between likely to influence which way Brazil leans.
