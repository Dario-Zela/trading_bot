SECTORS · HEALTH CARE · By I. Aresti · 2026-08-30 · 4 min read

Health care's month-long rally stalls out in a single week

Prescription medications — health care stocks led the market for a month before this week's sharp reversal. — J. Troha / National Cancer Institute (public domain)
In one sentence
XLV rallied 5.3% over a month but fell 1.98% this week — the market's worst sector — with energy showing a milder version of the same reversal.

For most of August, health care was the sector to own. Quietly, without much fanfare, it climbed to third-best in the S&P 500's eleven sectors over the trailing month, up 5.30% — a stretch that included UnitedHealth's sharpest bounce in more than a year and continued blowout growth from Eli Lilly's obesity-drug franchise. Then, in the space of five trading days, it gave the market a different story entirely: XLV, the sector's benchmark ETF, was the single worst-performing sector of the week, down 1.98%.

That is not a small swing. A sector does not usually go from top-three over a month to dead last over a week without something breaking. The question worth asking is whether health care just took a breather after running too far, too fast — or whether the week's reversal is an early tell that the rotation into defensives is running out of road.

What actually turned

The most obvious catalyst sits in trade policy. Section 232 tariffs on pharmaceutical imports — a 100% duty on patented drugs and active ingredients from companies that didn't sign onto the White House's pricing framework — took effect for large manufacturers on July 31 and are set to widen to the rest of the industry on September 29. Through the spring, more than a dozen major drugmakers, including Johnson & Johnson, Merck, Novartis, Sanofi and GSK, struck voluntary most-favored-nation pricing deals in exchange for tariff relief. That arrangement bought the sector a rally through the summer. But as the September 29 deadline for smaller and non-signing firms approaches, investors have had a fresh reason to price in supply-chain friction and margin compression across the group — a headline risk that doesn't discriminate between a stock that ran hard and one that didn't.

The rally itself supplied the other half of the explanation. Health care's 20-day gain wasn't broad-based blue-chip drift — it was concentrated, propelled by two of the most crowded trades on the desk. UnitedHealth, which spent the first half of 2026 reeling from a near-20% collapse tied to Medicare cost overruns and its first projected revenue decline in three decades, ripped higher in August after Berkshire Hathaway disclosed a $1.6 billion stake. Eli Lilly, still compounding on 54% year-over-year revenue growth from its GLP-1 franchise, kept adding to a valuation that was already stretched. Positions built that fast, on names that concentrated, tend to unwind faster than they were built when the news flow turns even modestly less friendly. That looks like the more parsimonious explanation than a wholesale rotation call: profit-taking in the sector's most-owned names, catalyzed by a real tariff deadline, rather than investors abandoning defensives as a theme.

The tell in energy

The pattern isn't unique to health care, which is what makes it worth flagging rather than dismissing. Energy shows the same shape in miniature: XLE is up 5.26% over the past month — consistent with a sector that has led the S&P 500 all year, up over 30% year-to-date on the back of the Strait of Hormuz standoff and Brent holding near $85 — but it slipped 1.51% over the past week as crude eased off its geopolitical highs. Two sectors with almost nothing in common fundamentally, both up strongly on a month view, both red on a week view. That's a signature more consistent with a market trimming winners across the board than with a healthcare-specific policy scare or an energy-specific demand shock.

What to watch

The cleanest test arrives with the September 29 tariff deadline, when the levy widens to manufacturers that haven't signed pricing deals — a date that will either confirm health care's move as a genuine repricing of policy risk or, if the sector stabilizes well before then, support the read that this was simply a crowded trade cooling off. Between now and then, UnitedHealth's ability to hold its August gains without another Berkshire-sized catalyst, and whether Eli Lilly's growth numbers can keep justifying its valuation, will matter more to XLV's path than any macro variable. A second down week for both health care and energy together would be the strongest signal yet that this is rotation, not noise.
