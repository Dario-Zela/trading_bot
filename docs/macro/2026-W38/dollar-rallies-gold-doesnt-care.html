The dollar rallies and gold doesn't care

The dollar posted its best weekly run in months after the Fed's first rate hike since 2023. — Milad Mosapoor / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
DXY jumped 1.11% on a hawkish Fed hike, yet gold and silver bounced too — a break that looks like twin short squeezes, not a new regime.

In one sentence: The dollar posted its best week in months on a hawkish Fed hike, and gold rallied right alongside it — a correlation break that looks like two short squeezes colliding, not a new regime.

The textbook says these two should not move together, least of all this week. The dollar index (DXY) closed at 100.22, up 1.11% over five sessions and 1.33% over the month — its strongest stretch since the summer. Gold, priced in the same currency that just got more expensive, should have wilted. Instead the SPDR Gold Shares ETF (GLD) rose 0.60% on the week, and silver (SLV) did something closer to a leap, up 3.11%. Both are still down over the past month — gold 3.39%, silver 2.81% — so this is a bounce inside a drawdown, not a reversal. But it happened on exactly the days it wasn't supposed to.

A hawkish surprise, not a flight to safety

The dollar's move has an obvious trigger. On September 16, the Federal Reserve raised its policy rate a quarter point — the first hike since 2023 — under new Chair Kevin Warsh, who was confirmed into the role in May. It was unanimous, and it came with guidance that left little room for a dovish read: 16 of 18 officials penciled in at least one more increase this year, and futures markets moved to price roughly three hikes through the middle of 2027. Bloomberg described the dollar's reaction as its biggest one-day jump since mid-June.

That is a rate-differential story, not a risk-off one. Traders had spent the prior week leaning the other way — positioning for a dollar already softened by weak data and jittery bond markets — and Warsh's tone forced a rapid unwind. FXStreet's read on the move was blunt: the bearish dollar bets built up since the previous Friday had to be squared all at once, producing a short squeeze rather than a fresh conviction trade. That distinction matters for what comes next. A dollar rally built on offside positioning is a different animal from one built on genuine safe-haven demand, and it tends to fade once the squeeze is done rather than compound.

Gold's own short squeeze

Gold's bounce carries a nearly identical fingerprint. Spot gold fell roughly 1.2% in the immediate aftermath of the hike — the textbook reaction — before rebounding hard, pushing back toward $4,400 an ounce over the following two sessions. FXStreet's weekly gold note attributed part of the move to the same mechanical dynamic driving the dollar: "an extra impulse from short covering occurred as the vast majority of traders were positioned for lower prices in tandem with the widely anticipated rate hike." In other words, the hike was priced in, gold sold off into the event as expected, and once it happened, sellers who had nothing left to sell became buyers covering their shorts.

There is a second thread underneath the mechanical one. The ten-year Treasury yield actually eased over the same stretch, slipping to roughly 4.95% from just above 5%, as a pullback in crude oil took some of the edge off inflation expectations even as the Fed tightened at the short end. Lower long-end yields reduce the opportunity cost of holding a non-yielding asset like gold, which gives the bounce a more fundamental leg to stand on than pure positioning — and it dovetails with the split this edition has flagged elsewhere between an anchored front end and a long end doing its own thing. Layer on the standing bid from central-bank reserve buying and the geopolitical premium tied to the ongoing Middle East conflict, and gold's resilience looks less like a fluke and more like several forces pulling the same direction at once, even against a stronger dollar.

Which story is the tell

Silver's outsized move is the detail worth sitting with. A 3.11% weekly gain against gold's 0.60% is a much larger beta than the historical gold-silver ratio would suggest for a defensive, safe-haven bid — silver's demand profile leans more industrial and more speculative, and sharp outperformance during a bounce is more consistent with a squeeze in a beaten-down, heavily shorted market than with investors rotating into hard assets. That argues for reading this week's gold-dollar divergence as two separate positioning unwinds that happened to land on the same days, rather than a signal that gold has decoupled from real rates for good.

The cleaner test arrives with the next inflation print and the next Fed commentary. If Warsh's committee reiterates the hawkish path and long-end yields catch up to the front end instead of diverging from it, gold's rate-sensitivity should reassert itself and this week's bounce will look like exactly what the positioning data suggests it was. If yields keep drifting down even as the Fed hikes — on oil, on growth concerns, or on the geopolitical premium building elsewhere in commodities — the divergence has a more durable explanation, and the dollar-gold relationship will have more resolving to do than a single week's data can settle.
