Energy stocks aren't buying oil's rally

A pumpjack in the Permian Basin, West Texas — energy equities are pricing production economics, not the war premium in the spot barrel. — Eric Kounce (TexasRaiser) / Wikimedia Commons, public domain
In one sentence
USO is up 14% in a month on the Iran war and a closed Strait of Hormuz, but XLE has captured less than 1% of that move — a sign equities see the spike as fragile, not durable.

Crude oil has had one of its sharpest runs of the year. The United States Oil Fund (USO) is up 14.33% over the past 20 trading days, a move driven by the war between Israel, the United States and Iran, the closure of the Strait of Hormuz, and a drone strike that knocked out Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline. WTI touched a four-month high near $106 a barrel this week before easing back toward $100.

Energy stocks have not gone along for the ride. The Energy Select Sector SPDR (XLE), the sector's benchmark ETF, captured just 0.88% of that 20-day move — roughly one-sixteenth of oil's gain — and is actually down 1.27% over the past five days even as crude held roughly flat over the same stretch. XLE closed Friday near $64.31, well off the week's highs.

That gap is the story. When crude rallies hard and energy equities barely move, it usually means one of two things: investors don't trust the rally to hold, or they've decided the cause of the rally is bad for producers' margins rather than good for them. Right now, both appear to be true.

A war premium, not a demand story

The oil market's move higher this month has nothing to do with stronger global demand — the more typical, and more durable, driver of energy-sector outperformance. It is a supply shock tied to the Iran conflict. Shut-in production averaged 6.7 million barrels a day in August, up from 5.0 million in July, and shipping traffic through the Strait of Hormuz fell to single digits per day over the weekend, according to tracking cited by CBS News. Iran has said it will not reopen the strait until Washington meets its conditions for ending the war.

That kind of premium is inherently fragile, and there are already signs it is being priced for reversal. Oman postponed a planned meeting between Iran and Gulf states this month "in the interest of consensus," and Saudi Arabia's foreign minister publicly thanked the Trump administration for giving diplomacy "a chance to reach an acceptable agreement" restoring freedom of navigation through the strait. Meanwhile, satellite imagery shows Saudi Arabia has already begun rerouting crude around the blockade — moving 2.8 million barrels a day through the strait via ship-to-ship transfers at Ras Tanura in the past week, up from just 700,000 barrels a day in August. Bloomberg Intelligence's Vincent Piazza has pointed to a broader pattern this year: geopolitical stress from Venezuela to Ukraine to Iran has repeatedly added a "risk premium" to oil that markets treat as separate from, and less durable than, the underlying price.

Why equities aren't paying up

Energy stocks are claims on future earnings, not on the spot price of a barrel. A supply-driven spike caused by tankers being unable to move is, paradoxically, not obviously good for producer margins the way a demand-driven rally would be — it raises input and logistics costs, threatens to be reversed the moment shipping normalizes, and does nothing to change the volumes companies can actually sell. StoneX Wealth Management's Michael Lytle made a version of this point after energy stocks' near-40% surge in the first quarter: current valuations depend on oil staying elevated, and "any sort of pressure that's released" — a ceasefire, a reopened strait, restored pipeline flows — puts that premium at risk of unwinding fast. XLE's five-day decline, even as crude held its ground, looks like the market pre-positioning for exactly that kind of release valve.

Natural gas is a useful contrast. UNG is up 2.36% over five days and 4.00% over 20 — a smaller move than oil's, but one built on a demand story markets seem more willing to believe: LNG export flows have risen to 18.3 billion cubic feet a day this month from 17.2 bcfd in August, and above-normal temperatures have kept air-conditioning and power-generation demand elevated into late September. That's the kind of grind-higher move equities can underwrite. Oil's spike, by contrast, is a headline that could unwind on a single diplomatic breakthrough.

The next test is the Oman-brokered Iran-Gulf talks, still pending after this month's postponement. A resumption of normal Strait of Hormuz traffic — or confirmation that Saudi Arabia's Ras Tanura workaround is offsetting the bulk of the shut-in barrels — would be the clearest signal that the market's skepticism about energy equities was justified. Until then, the gap between USO and XLE is less a market inefficiency than a fairly rational bet on how a war-driven oil spike usually ends.
