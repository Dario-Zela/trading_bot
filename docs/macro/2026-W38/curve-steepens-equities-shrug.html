EDITORIAL · WEEKLY THESIS · By The Editor · 2026-09-20 · 5 min read

The curve steepens, and equities dare it to matter

The New York Stock Exchange trading floor — where equities shrugged off a week of rate-market warnings. — Eduard Hueber / Asymptote Architecture, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Yields and the dollar surged on Fed hawkishness while tech and gold rallied anyway — the real tell is credit, where high yield has lagged investment grade for five straight days.

The bond market spent the week telling a story about pain to come. The dollar spent the week confirming it. And equities — tech especially — spent the week acting as if neither memo had arrived.

Start with the curve, because that is where the argument begins. The 30-year Treasury yield pushed up to 5.33% while the 3-month bill sat at 3.98%, leaving the 3-month/10-year spread at 1.02 points, its widest stretch in months. This is not the gentle re-steepening of a curve normalizing after inversion. It is what traders call a bear steepener — long rates rising faster than short rates — and it usually shows up when the market is repricing the cost of money further out, not celebrating a soft landing closer in. The Federal Reserve raised its policy rate a quarter point to 3.75%–4.00% on September 16, its first hike since 2023, with Chair Kevin Warsh blunt about the reasoning: "the plain fact is that inflation is too high, and has been for too long." Sixteen of eighteen officials see at least one more increase this year.

A hawkish Fed with a long way still to go on inflation is, in the textbook version of events, bad news for risk assets and good news for the dollar. Half of that showed up exactly on schedule.

The dollar did its part

The dollar index rose 1.11% over the week and 1.33% over twenty trading days, closing above its 200-day moving average — a level that, per desk chatter, has tended to invite further buying rather than mean-reversion in prior breakouts this year. JPMorgan and Standard Chartered both flagged the Fed decision as removing the last real obstacle to further dollar strength. That is the currency market doing precisely what a hawkish, rising-real-rate regime says it should do.

Utilities did their part too. XLU was the week's worst-performing sector, down 3.04% over five days and 6.10% over twenty — a textbook reaction to rising long yields, since utility stocks trade partly as bond proxies and lose their relative appeal the moment 30-year paper offers 5.33% risk-free. Only two of eleven S&P sectors finished the week positive: healthcare and technology.

Equities and gold didn't get the memo

And that is where the contradiction sharpens. Technology was not just positive — it led, with semiconductor names driving the gains after Nvidia announced a $5 billion investment in Intel, including a plan for the two companies to jointly develop PC and data-center chips. Synopsys jumped 16% on the week; Applied Materials rose 13%. This is the kind of concentrated, growth-led rally that typically wants cheap money and a falling discount rate, not a Fed chair promising more hikes and a 30-year yield near six-year highs.

Gold, meanwhile, did something stranger still. Spot gold traded near $4,386 an ounce, up close to 1% on the week, snapping what had been a three-week losing streak — this despite a rate hike and a surging dollar, the two forces that are supposed to be gold's natural enemies. An early-week selloff driven by near-5% Treasury yields and oil price pressure gave way to a sharp recovery once the hike was actually delivered, as investors apparently concluded the Fed's longer-run path was shallower than feared even if this week's move was real.

So the week produced two coherent stories running in opposite directions at the same time. Rates and the dollar are pricing a central bank still fighting inflation with real conviction. Equities and gold are pricing something closer to relief — that the hiking cycle is nearly done, that growth holds up regardless, that this is a curve normalizing rather than a curve warning.

Where the tell actually is

Equity indices are a poor place to adjudicate this argument, because index-level strength can hide a great deal of internal disagreement — and did. The more useful place to look is credit, and specifically the relationship between high-yield and investment-grade bonds. Over the same five sessions that saw tech stocks lead and the S&P hold up, high-yield debt underperformed investment-grade debt by 0.45 percentage points. That is a small number, but it is a directionally important one: high-yield issuers are the borrowers most exposed to a genuinely higher-for-longer rate environment, and their bonds are starting to say so even while their equity cousins in the same risk-on trade are not.

Credit spreads are not shouting yet. Investment-grade paper trades around 81 basis points over Treasuries and high-yield spreads remain historically tight — well inside their twenty-year averages — so this is not a market pricing distress. It is a market where the most credit-sensitive corner has quietly stopped agreeing with the most growth-sensitive corner, five days running. Equity investors buying the dip in growth names are, in effect, betting that this is noise. Credit investors, who tend to move first and complain about it later, are behaving as though it might not be.

What resolves it

The steepening curve, the strong dollar and the beaten-up utility sector all point toward tightening finally biting somewhere. Tech leadership and a gold bounce point toward a market still willing to look through it. Both cannot be right for long. The high-yield/investment-grade gap is the falsifiable piece — if it keeps widening into next week while equities stay firm, that is the credit market front-running an equity repricing rather than sending a false signal. If it closes instead, this week's divergence was just noise around a Fed decision, and the steepening can be filed under "normalization" rather than "warning." Either way, the next print on the 3-month/10-year spread and another week of HY-versus-IG performance will do more to settle the argument than anything the S&P does on its own.
