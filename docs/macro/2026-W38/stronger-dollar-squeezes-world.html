A stronger dollar starts to squeeze the rest of the world

A stronger dollar raises the cost of dollar-denominated debt and imports for economies outside the United States. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
DXY's best run in weeks tightens conditions for the whole world outside the US just as oil, metals and grains pull in three different directions.

The dollar has put together its best stretch in weeks, and for markets outside the United States that is not a footnote — it is a tightening event. The DXY index is up 1.11% over five trading days and 1.33% over twenty, a quiet but real increase in the cost of anything priced or financed in dollars, from a Jakarta importer's letter of credit to a Lagos telecom's Eurobond coupon. It is arriving at an unusually awkward moment, because the commodities that matter most to those same economies are moving in opposite directions at once.

Oil is the loudest of them. USO is up 14.33% over the past twenty trading days, a rally rooted in the 2026 Iran war and the effective closure of the Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne crude normally passes. Gulf exports have fallen to around nine million barrels a day from close to seventeen million before the conflict, and the September 11 shutdown of the East-West Crude Oil Pipeline added a fresh supply shock on top of a market already paying elevated war-risk insurance premiums on Gulf-transiting tankers. Brent spiked as high as $120 a barrel in March and has since settled back toward $103, but that is still a materially higher band than the one the world budgeted for at the start of the year.

Base metals are firmer too, but for a completely different reason. DBB is up 2.09% over five days and 3.10% over twenty, driven largely by copper, which touched a record $14,533 a tonne on September 7. That rally is about supply — labor unrest and declining ore grades in Chile and Peru, which together produce roughly eight million tonnes of the world's copper a year — colliding with AI-driven infrastructure demand that mining output has not kept pace with. Agriculture is the outlier moving the other way: DBA has fallen 2.70% over five days after the USDA's September report raised its forecasts for the American soybean crop and lifted world wheat stocks estimates well above trade expectations, pulling soybeans, corn and wheat lower into the weekend.

Who actually gets squeezed

A stronger dollar landing on top of dearer oil is straightforwardly bad for the world's large net oil importers, and India is the cleanest case. The country imports roughly 85% of the crude it consumes, about half of it transiting the Hormuz corridor, and analysts estimate that every $10 increase in the price of oil widens its current account deficit by 40 to 50 basis points. A firmer rupee had been offsetting some of that pressure through the summer, but a dollar rising broadly against most currencies takes that cushion away. The same arithmetic applies, with local variations, to Turkey, much of North Africa and the food- and fuel-importing economies of South and Southeast Asia.

The picture inverts for net commodity exporters, and even there it splits by commodity. Brazil now counts oil as its single largest export category on the back of pre-salt production growth, so the crude rally is a genuine terms-of-trade gain even as the real, down roughly 20% against the dollar over the past year, keeps debt-servicing costs elevated. Chile and Peru are collecting a similar windfall from copper. Grain exporters get no such relief — Brazil, Argentina and the Black Sea producers are net losers from the USDA's bearish supply read, even as they benefit from the oil side of the ledger. There is no single "EM story" here; there is a matrix of exposures, and which quadrant a given economy sits in depends on its specific export basket, not on any broad emerging-market label.

The next data points worth watching are India's current account figures for the September quarter, any further disruption to Hormuz transit volumes, and the October USDA supply report that will either confirm or reverse this month's grain weakness.
