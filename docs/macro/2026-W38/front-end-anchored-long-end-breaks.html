Front end anchored, long end breaks higher

Fed Chair Kevin Warsh, whose September 16 rate hike was largely priced in before it happened — leaving the front end of the curve anchored even as the long end kept climbing. — Federal Reserve / Wikimedia Commons (official portrait)
In one sentence
The curve isn't steepening evenly — the front end is anchored near 3.98% while the 30-year has broken above 5.33%, pointing to a term-premium story, not a policy one.

The Treasury market is not moving as one piece right now. Short-dated yields have barely budged in the past week, while the long end has broken to levels not seen since before the 2008 financial crisis. The gap between the two — the spread between the 3-month bill and the 10-year note — widened to 1.02 percentage points as of September 18, the steepest the curve has been in months. But the shape of that steepening matters as much as its size, and here it tells an unusual story: this is not the classic bull steepener that shows up when a central bank starts cutting rates and short yields fall out from under the curve. The front end hasn't moved much at all. Almost the entire adjustment is happening at 30 years.

The 3-month bill was yielding 3.978% as of the most recent read, comfortably inside its range of the past several weeks. The 10-year sits at 4.998%, effectively pinned to the 5% threshold it first breached on an intraday basis back in October 2023 and has now returned to on a more sustained footing. The 30-year, though, has pushed through 5.331% — its highest level since 2007 — and the five-year, at 4.856%, sits well behind the long bond's advance, meaning the belly of the curve has barely participated either. Put simply: the curve isn't rotating, it's tilting, with the pivot point somewhere around the 10-year note.

A hike that was already priced

The timing matters. The Federal Reserve raised its target range on September 16 to 3.75%–4.00%, a quarter-point hike delivered on a unanimous 12–0 vote and the first increase since the current tightening cycle began. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh, confirmed to the role in May after Jerome Powell's term ended, told reporters that inflation has been "too high ... for too long" and that the committee needed to be "confident that underlying inflation is moving to our objective clearly and at sufficient speed" before easing up — a standard he said had "not been satisfied."

What's notable is how little the front end reacted. Warsh had spent weeks laying the groundwork — an August 28 Jackson Hole speech on financial conditions was widely read as a signal that a hike was coming, and by late August markets were already treating a September move as close to a done deal. By the time the FOMC actually acted, 3-month bills had already absorbed the news. That's why the front end looks anchored rather than surprised: there was no repricing left to do. Eight of twelve committee participants now see another hike in 2027, and only four project cuts next year, but that guidance, too, was largely consistent with what was already in the price.

The long end is pricing something else

If the front end reflects a well-telegraphed policy path, the long end is answering a different question: how much compensation does a lender need to hold that duration for three decades? That premium — economists call it the term premium — is the part of a bond's yield that isn't explained by the expected average path of short-term rates. It spent much of the 2010s and early 2020s suppressed by quantitative easing and a global savings glut. It has been reasserting itself for months.

Pacific Investment Management Co. put a number on the shift last month, arguing the extra yield investors demand for holding long-dated government debt is likely to stay elevated barring an unexpected downturn. "Current yield levels look increasingly appealing by historical standards, offering a compelling entry point for long-term investors," PIMCO's Marc Seidner and Pramol Dhawan wrote, framing the higher term premium as an opportunity rather than a warning — though the framing itself is an acknowledgment that something structural, not cyclical, is behind the move. The firm noted long-term yields have climbed similarly in the UK, Japan and continental Europe, which argues against a purely domestic explanation tied to the Fed.

The more common domestic explanation is fiscal: persistent deficits, a growing stock of Treasury issuance to fund them, and a bond market less willing than it once was to absorb that supply without being paid more for it. Analysts have taken to calling this a return of the "bond vigilantes," though the more accurate description is quieter than the phrase implies — not a sudden strike against a specific policy, but a slow repricing of duration risk as the market concludes that large-scale borrowing is now a permanent feature of the fiscal landscape rather than a temporary, pandemic-era response.

Why the belly hasn't moved

The five-year note is the clearest evidence for the term-premium reading. At 4.856%, it sits closer to the 10-year than to the 30-year, and its relative stability suggests the market isn't extrapolating a broad-based repricing of the entire curve — it's specifically demanding more to hold the longest maturities. If this were a story about the market losing confidence in the Fed's inflation path more generally, the five-year, which is sensitive to the average expected policy rate over a medium horizon, would be moving with the 30-year. It isn't. The 10-year holding at 5% while the 30-year runs another 33 basis points past it is a curve that's getting steeper specifically at its far end, which is where term-premium effects concentrate and where policy-expectation effects fade out.

That distinction has practical consequences beyond the bond market. A curve steepening because the Fed is expected to cut is typically read as constructive — cheaper short-term funding, an economy edging toward easier conditions. A curve steepening because long-term lenders want more compensation for duration and supply risk is a different signal: it raises the cost of long-dated corporate borrowing, mortgage rates tied to the 10-year, and the government's own interest expense on new issuance, all without any change in the near-term growth or inflation outlook implied by the front end.

What comes next

The next Treasury refunding announcement, expected in early November, will show whether the Treasury Department leans further into longer maturities or shifts issuance back toward bills — a decision that directly affects how much long-duration supply the market has to absorb. Between now and then, the clearest tell will be whether the 5-year starts to catch up to the 30-year's move. If the belly stays anchored while the long end keeps climbing, that keeps this a term-premium story. If the five-year starts moving with it, the market will be telling a broader story about the inflation and policy outlook — not just about who's willing to lend for thirty years and at what price.
