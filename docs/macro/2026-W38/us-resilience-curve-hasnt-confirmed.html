US markets are pricing resilience the curve hasn't confirmed

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C., where the FOMC raised rates a quarter point on September 16 — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The curve, the dollar and equities are pricing three different US economies at once, and September's data will force a resolution.

The tape this week is telling four different stories, and only one of them can be true.

Start with the numbers. The spread between the 3-month bill and the 10-year note — a favorite recession-timing gauge precisely because it captures where the Federal Reserve is against where the market thinks the economy is headed — sits at 1.02 percentage points, close to the widest it has been all cycle. The 30-year bond yield closed at 5.33%, the highest since 2007, having spent August grinding through one 19-year high after another. The dollar index has rallied 1.11% in five sessions and cleared 100 for the first time in weeks. And the S&P and Nasdaq are sitting near records, with defensive sectors — utilities, real estate, staples — getting sold as if duration itself were radioactive.

Each of those facts, alone, is a clean story. A steep curve says growth is coming. A surging dollar says the US economy is strong relative to everyone else's. A tech-led equity tape says risk appetite is intact. A defensive rout says investors are fleeing bond-like stocks because rates are going up for good. Put together, they contradict each other, and the contradiction is the story.

A hike that didn't settle anything

The proximate cause of this week's dissonance is the Federal Reserve. On September 16 the FOMC raised its target range a quarter point to 3.75%–4.00%, its first hike since 2023, with Chair Kevin Warsh telling reporters that inflation had been "too high … for too long" and that the committee's confidence standard on returning to 2% "has not been satisfied." Sixteen of nineteen policymakers now see at least one more increase before year-end.

That ought to have flattened the curve, not steepened it. Instead, the two-year note — the part of the curve most sensitive to what the Fed does next — touched 4.744% on Friday, its highest since July 2024, but nowhere near the roughly 5.3% level the long bond has already reached. The reason is the second half of the Fed's own dot plot: push the projections out to 2027 and the committee splinters, with four of eighteen officials already sketching a path back toward cuts even as the median holds near 4%. Goldman Sachs has told clients the market's own pricing — futures imply something like three more quarter-point moves over the coming year — is "too hawkish" relative to where the economy is actually likely to land. The front end, in other words, has priced a Fed that tightens now and probably has to give some of it back later. That is not the front end of an economy that's plainly overheating; it is the front end of an economy that might be, hedged against the possibility that it isn't.

The long end isn't listening to the Fed at all

The 30-year bond doesn't care what the dot plot says beyond 2027, because the 30-year bond isn't really pricing Fed policy anymore. It's pricing term premium — the extra compensation investors demand to hold duration through decades of deficits, auction supply and inflation uncertainty that no one meeting's dot plot can resolve. The Treasury sold $25 billion of 30-year debt in August at 5.216%, the richest yield for that auction since 2001, and later moved to double its buyback program for long-dated debt from $2 billion to at least $4 billion — an unusually direct admission that the department is worried about how the far end of the curve is absorbing supply. A hike that's meant to look temporary at the front end reads as permanent at the back end. Those are not compatible readings of the same central bank.

Equities are choosing a side the bond market hasn't

The dollar's rally fits the hawkish-Fed story: real yields up, rate differentials widening, capital pulled toward dollar assets. But the equity tape doesn't fit either story cleanly. The Nasdaq-100 has run to fresh records, up more than 30% off its spring low on the back of chipmakers and AI capital spending — a bet on structural growth that has nothing to do with this week's Fed statement. Meanwhile utilities, real estate and consumer staples — the sectors that behave like long-duration bonds with a dividend attached — have been sold across every recent window, punished by exactly the long-end move that tech has shrugged off entirely. A market genuinely worried about a hawkish, higher-for-longer Fed should not be simultaneously making new highs in its most rate-sensitive growth names. A market pricing eventual cuts should not be gutting the sectors that benefit most when cuts arrive.

What forces the resolution

These four signals — a front end still hedging toward cuts, a long end pricing permanent duration risk, a dollar behaving like the hawks are right, and an equity market that's stopped listening to bonds altogether — cannot all be correct going into year-end. The next test is data, not rhetoric: September's payrolls and CPI prints will tell the front end whether Warsh's hawkishness is data-justified or a one-meeting overreach, while the Treasury's November refunding announcement will show whether the buyback program is actually denting term premium or merely slowing its rise. Until one of those breaks, the curve, the dollar and the equity tape will keep pricing three different economies at once.
