RATES · POLICY VS TERM PREMIUM · By G. Mehlman · 2026-09-20 · 4 min read

Two different curves are being priced at once

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C. — the front end of the curve still answers to policy set here; the long end increasingly doesn't. — AgnosticPreachersKid / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
The front end of the curve is calm on Fed cuts; the 5Y-30Y segment is steepening on its own, and that's what's hitting REITs and utilities.

Look at the Treasury curve two different ways this week and you get two different stories.

From three months out to five years, the move has been almost polite. The 3-month bill sits at 3.978%, the 5-year note at 4.86% — a gap of well under 100 basis points that has barely budged in recent sessions. That is the part of the curve that answers to the Federal Reserve, and it is telling a fairly boring story: rate cuts are still plausible, policy is not about to lurch, and the market isn't pricing any near-term shock to the front end.

Keep going, though, and the picture changes. From five years to thirty, the curve steepens by another 47 basis points, taking the long bond to 5.33%. Put the two segments side by side and the back half of the curve is doing roughly 48 basis points more work than the front half — nearly all of this week's steepening is happening past the five-year mark, not before it.

Two curves, two explanations

That split matters because the two segments are priced by different things. The front end mostly reflects what traders expect the Fed to do with the overnight rate over the next few years. At 3.978% on the 3-month bill, there is little sign that expectations for an easing-biased Fed have shifted much — the near-dated end of the curve looks anchored, not alarmed.

The long end is a different animal. Nobody is pricing the 30-year yield off next quarter's FOMC statement. It moves on term premium — the extra compensation investors demand for the risk of holding duration for decades, exposed to inflation surprises, deficit financing needs, and whatever the Fed does over a horizon nobody can forecast with confidence. When the 5s30s segment steepens while the 3-month-to-5-year segment sits still, that is a signal the market is charging more for holding long paper, independent of what it thinks the policy rate will do next year.

Where it shows up in equities

This is not an abstraction for rate desks alone. Over the past 20 trading days, the two worst-performing sectors in the S&P have been Real Estate (XLRE) and Utilities (XLU) — the two groups most mechanically sensitive to long-duration discount rates. Both sectors carry bond-like cash flow profiles: steady, long-dated, and valued today largely as a function of what a dollar received in year twenty is worth. When the discount rate on that dollar rises, the present value of the whole cash-flow stream falls, and it falls harder than it would for a business whose earnings show up next quarter rather than next decade.

That is the mechanism connecting a 48-basis-point spread differential to a sector laggard board. It is not that the Fed is hawkish — the front end says otherwise. It is that the price of duration itself is rising, and REITs and regulated utilities are, in effect, long-duration bond proxies wearing equity tickers.

The read for the week ahead

The useful distinction for anyone parsing this tape is that "the curve is steepening" is not one fact but two, and only one of them is a policy story. A trader watching the 3-month bill for clues about the next Fed move will see calm. A portfolio manager holding XLRE or XLU for their yield and stability will feel the other half of the curve directly, through the discount rate applied to their holdings. Both are reading the same yield curve correctly — they are just reading different segments of it.

The next test comes with fresh long-bond supply and the following inflation print, both of which speak more directly to term premium than to the Fed's rate path. Until the 5s30s segment stabilizes, duration-sensitive equity groups are likely to keep trading as a proxy for the long end, not for Fed policy.
