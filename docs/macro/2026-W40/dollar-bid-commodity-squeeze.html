A dollar bid of 3% in 20 days is squeezing metals and commodity currencies

In one sentence
A dollar up nearly 3% in 20 days, backed by a 5.28% US 10-year yield, is pressing metals and commodity currencies and exposing energy importers and EM borrowers.

The dollar has had a strong three weeks, and the things priced in it have paid for it. The US Dollar Index stands at 101.93, up 0.95% over five days and 2.96% over twenty. Over the same stretch gold and silver have fallen, base metals have dropped sharply and agricultural futures have slipped. The timing is unlikely to be coincidence. Most of the world's raw materials are priced in dollars, so a stronger dollar makes them dearer for nearly every buyer outside the United States and tends to pull the dollar price down.

The squeeze in the numbers

The base-metals fund DBB fell 4.0% in five days, the sharpest move in the commodity set. The agriculture fund DBA lost 1.2% over five days and 3.0% over twenty, a slower decline that fits a market adjusting to a stronger currency and not reacting to a single shock.

Precious metals add a complication. Gold and silver normally gain when investors want shelter. This time they have fallen alongside the broader complex, which suggests the dollar and rising yields are the stronger force. A related piece in this edition, Gold is falling with equities, looks at what that does to the usual hedge. Here the point is narrower. The same move that lifts the dollar lowers the appeal of assets that pay no interest.

The mechanism has two parts. A firmer dollar raises the local-currency cost of metals and crops for buyers in Europe, Asia and Latin America, and some of them cut back. Rising returns on dollar assets also pull funds out of commodity positions, which earn nothing until sold.

Why the dollar is bid

The 10-year Treasury yield is 5.28%, and that is the main support. A yield at that level gives holders of dollars a carry advantage over most of the developed world, and capital flows toward it. The curve is steep, with the long end doing most of the work, a theme taken up in The curve is steep at 128bp. It is the long end that matters for currency, because it sets the price of money for borrowers everywhere, as discussed in US long yields set the global price of money.

The causal chain therefore runs from long Treasury yields to the dollar and then to commodity prices. The data in hand show the three moving together. They do not by themselves prove the first link drives the second, but the five-day and twenty-day windows both point the same way, and the dollar has risen in each of the last two measurement windows.

Who is most exposed

Two groups stand out.

The first is the importers of dollar-priced energy. Oil and gas are invoiced in dollars, so a country that buys fuel with a weakening currency pays twice, once through the commodity price and once through the exchange rate. Economies that run large energy import bills and thin foreign-exchange reserves, a category that includes much of South Asia and parts of emerging Europe, feel this fastest in their trade balances and in inflation. Energy has so far been the one real-asset hedge holding up, as set out in Energy is the last hedge, which means the import bill is not falling to offset the currency effect.

The second is emerging-market borrowers. Companies and governments that issued dollar bonds must service them in currency that is losing value against the dollar. When Treasury yields at the long end are above 5%, refinancing costs rise as well. Exporters of metals and crops face a separate hit: their revenue is dollar-priced and falling, which weakens the commodity currencies, such as those of Australia, Canada and Brazil, that normally move with those prices. The fuller emerging-market picture is in Falling base metals and a firm dollar.

Credit markets give a hint of how the stress is arriving. High yield and investment grade have fallen together, which points to duration, not default, as the source of the selling (see here). The pressure is coming from the price of money, not from a sudden judgment that borrowers cannot repay.

The call and what would change it

The forecast here is that the DXY holds above 101 as long as US long yields stay above 5.2%. The 10-year sits at 5.28%, only eight basis points above that line, so the margin is thin. The dollar's carry support depends on that gap, and a decline in long yields would remove it quickly.

The risk to the view runs both ways. A slide in the 10-year below 5.2% would undercut the dollar and could give metals and commodity currencies room to recover. A further rise in yields would extend the squeeze, and the pressure on importers and dollar borrowers would build. The real-yield angle, in which the dollar and real rates reprice everything except technology, is covered in Real yields and the dollar.

The next test is the long end of the Treasury curve at the coming auctions and in the next inflation data. Those releases will show whether 5.2% is a floor for yields or merely a pause on the way to somewhere else.
