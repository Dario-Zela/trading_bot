Financials and health care lead the weekly losses despite a steep curve

In one sentence
XLF fell 2.5% on the week and 8.7% over 20 days despite a 128bp curve, and the evidence points to bond-portfolio losses from rising long yields, not credit stress.

A steeper yield curve is supposed to be good news for banks. They borrow at short rates and lend at long ones, so a wider gap between the two usually fattens their margins. This week the curve was steep and bank shares fell anyway. The Financial Select Sector SPDR (XLF) lost 2.46% over five trading days and 8.66% over 20. Health care did worse on the week: XLV fell 2.65%, the weakest of the eleven sectors.

The gap between the three-month and ten-year Treasury yields stands at 128 basis points. That is wide by recent standards, so the puzzle is real. Three explanations are plausible, and the market data on hand favours one of them.

Unrealised losses

Banks hold large portfolios of bonds bought when yields were lower. When long yields rise, the market value of those bonds falls. Much of the loss never reaches the income statement, but it erodes tangible capital and makes investors wary of any lender that might have to sell. The same logic explains why the 2023 regional-bank failures were tied to duration rather than to bad loans.

The pattern this week fits that channel. The companion piece on the long end of the curve argues that the steepness comes from long yields climbing, not from short rates falling. That is the unhelpful kind of steepening for a bank, because it lowers the value of what the bank already owns before it raises what the bank earns on new loans. Real estate points the same way. XLRE is down 7.8% over 20 days, close to the financials' 8.7%, and it is a sector whose valuations follow long rates closely.

Funding costs

The second candidate is the cost of money banks pay for deposits and wholesale funding. If it rose as fast as long yields, the steep curve would deliver no margin benefit. This is plausible in principle, but nothing in this week's price data tests it. The 128bp spread is measured against the three-month bill, and the long end is doing the work, so short rates do not look like the driver. Deposit costs and bank-level margin guidance will need to show the squeeze. Until they do, this explanation is a hypothesis, not a finding.

Credit quality

The third candidate is that investors fear loan losses. If so, the damage should show up in credit markets as well as bank equities. The credit piece finds that high yield and investment grade fell together. Higher-quality debt falling alongside the weaker kind points to a move in the discount rate, which is duration. If default fears were driving the selling, high yield would have lagged investment grade. Credit quality may yet become a problem for banks, but this week's prices do not show it.

Health care and the missing defence

Health care's 2.65% drop has no comparable rate story. Its weakness is more consistent with the wider breakdown in defensive positioning described in the piece on hedges failing together. With technology the lone sector holding up, the defensive sectors had little cover to offer.

What to watch

The test is whether financials stabilise if the ten-year yield does. If they do, the securities-loss story holds. If they keep falling while long yields steady, funding costs or credit become the better explanation. Bank earnings reports, with their deposit-cost and securities-loss disclosures, will settle the question.
