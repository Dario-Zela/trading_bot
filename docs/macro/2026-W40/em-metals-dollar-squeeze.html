Falling base metals and a firm dollar test the emerging-market picture

In one sentence
Base metals, silver and farm prices fell as the dollar firmed, squeezing metals and crop exporters, while oil's 20-day gain leaves energy exporters better placed.

Commodity exporters had a poor week on two fronts at once. Base metals, silver and agricultural prices all fell while the US dollar firmed, which cuts what producers earn and raises what it costs them to borrow and repay in dollars. Oil is the exception, and that splits the emerging-market picture into two groups.

Metals and farms take the hit

The sharpest moves were in industrial metals. The Invesco DB Base Metals Fund (DBB), a basket of aluminium, copper and zinc futures, lost 4.0% over five trading days and 3.5% over 20, so the weekly drop is bigger than the monthly one. Nearly all of the month's decline arrived recently. The iShares Silver Trust (SLV) fell 5.8% in five days. Agricultural benchmarks also finished lower, though the triage data for this piece does not give a clean figure for them.

The dollar is the second half of the squeeze. The DXY index, which measures the dollar against a basket of major currencies, stood at 101.93. Commodities are priced in dollars, so a stronger dollar tends to push prices down on its own. It also makes dollar debt heavier for borrowers whose revenues are in local currency. Our FX desk puts the dollar's gain at about 3% over 20 days; see the dollar and commodity squeeze piece.

The countries most exposed are those that sell metals and crops and also owe dollars. Producers of copper, zinc, aluminium and silver, and large grain and oilseed exporters, are losing export revenue just as external financing gets more expensive. The two pressures reinforce each other. Weaker commodity income tends to weaken the local currency, and a weaker currency raises the local cost of dollar debt.

Oil breaks the pattern

Energy exporters are in a different position. The US Oil Fund (USO) is up 3.7% over 20 days. That is a modest gain, but it is a gain, and it runs against the rest of the commodity complex. Oil producers still face the firmer dollar and the higher cost of dollar funding, but they have rising revenue to set against it. Our Risk desk looks at how crowded that trade has become in energy as the last hedge.

The divide matters for how "emerging markets" gets read. An economy that exports crude and one that exports copper or soybeans can sit in the same benchmark index and still have opposite terms-of-trade stories this week.

What the data cannot say

The evidence here has limits. All the figures come from exchange-traded funds and one currency index, not from emerging-market currency, bond or equity data. ETF returns are a proxy for futures markets and can differ from spot prices. USO's 20-day gain is also small, and a single month of data says little about a durable trend. There is no direct reading in this dataset of emerging-market spreads or currencies, so the stress described above is inferred from commodity and dollar moves, not observed in those markets.

The wider backdrop is that US long yields are setting the global price of money (see US yields and global conditions), which adds to the funding strain on borrowers outside the US.

The tests ahead are whether the dollar holds near 102 and whether base metals stabilise after the week's drop. If both persist, the pressure on metals and agricultural exporters will start to show in their currencies and external borrowing costs. Oil's 20-day gain is the figure to watch for the other group.
