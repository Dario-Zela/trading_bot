EDITORIAL · WEEKLY THESIS · By The Editor · 2026-10-04 · 6 min read

Real yields and the dollar are repricing everything except tech

In one sentence
A 10-year at 5.28%, a firmer dollar and falling gold and credit prices point to tighter financial conditions that only technology has so far shrugged off.

A ten-year Treasury yield of 5.28%, a dollar that has gained almost 3% in a month, and gold, silver and corporate bonds all lower in the same week: the cross-asset tape reads less like investors fleeing a weakening economy than like the price of money being marked up. Technology is the one part of the equity market that has so far ignored it. The House view is that this is a rates-and-dollar shock, that it is not yet a growth scare, and that the narrowness of the stock market's resistance is now the thing to worry about.

What a growth scare would look like

The distinction matters because the two stories call for different behaviour. In a growth scare, long-dated government bonds rally, the dollar tends to find a haven bid, gold holds or rises, and cyclical sectors lead the losses. This week, nearly all of that ran the other way.

The ten-year yield stands at 5.28% and the thirty-year at 5.63%, as of 2 October. The dollar index closed at 101.93, up 0.95% over five sessions and 2.96% over twenty. Gold, as tracked by the GLD fund, fell 3.4% on the week. Silver, through SLV, fell 5.8%.

That last pair is the most telling. Precious metals are ordinarily the asset that people reach for when equities wobble. They fell hard in a week when equities were also soft, which is the signature of a rise in real yields and a stronger dollar rather than of fear. Gold pays no coupon. When the return on holding a safe, inflation-adjusted bond rises and the currency in which gold is priced strengthens at the same time, the metal is squeezed from both sides. The dollar's role is examined in more detail in the dollar-and-commodities piece, and the failure of the usual hedge in the risk desk's account.

Duration, not default

Credit adds a second piece of evidence. High-yield and investment-grade bond prices fell together. In a genuine deterioration of the economic outlook, the two would be expected to diverge: weaker borrowers would be repriced sharply while higher-quality paper was cushioned, or even helped, by falling government yields. Instead the whole asset class moved with the risk-free rate.

That pattern is what duration risk looks like. A bond's price falls when the discount rate rises, and the longer the bond, the more it falls. Investment-grade portfolios carry long maturities and little spread cushion, so they are exposed to precisely this kind of move. The pressure here seems to come from the base rate and not from a rising probability that borrowers fail to pay. The credit desk's analysis takes the point further.

The shape of the curve supports the reading. The spread between the three-month bill and the ten-year note is +128 basis points, and the long end is doing most of the work. A steep curve built by rising long yields is a different animal from one steepened by the central bank cutting at the front. The first tightens financial conditions for anyone who borrows long, including mortgage holders, utilities and corporate issuers. The rates desk and the regional view explain why a move in American long yields travels so easily to other markets.

The one sector that did not blink

The equity picture is where the evidence turns from consistent to uncomfortable. There are eleven sectors in the S&P 500. Ten of them are lower over the past twenty trading days. The exception is technology, with the XLK fund up 7.4% over the same period.

A market in which one sector rises 7.4% while ten others fall is not a market that is shrugging off higher rates. It is a market in which a single group is carrying the index. That has two implications. The first is that headline index levels are flattering the underlying condition: the average stock is already behaving as one would expect under a 5.28% ten-year. The second is that the tech leadership depends on conditions that the rest of the tape suggests are deteriorating. Long-duration growth equities are, in theory, the most sensitive of all to rising discount rates. Their resilience so far may reflect earnings strength, or it may reflect crowding, with investors rotating into the one place where results have kept arriving. The tape cannot distinguish between the two, and the sector desk is careful not to claim it can.

The oddities elsewhere deepen the sense of a market under rate pressure rather than growth pressure. Financials and health care led the weekly losses, despite a steep curve that would normally help bank margins; that puzzle remains unresolved. Falling base metals alongside a firm dollar also test the emerging-market picture, a theme explored in the regional coverage. Energy is the lone real-asset hedge still working, though it is crowded.

The thesis, and what would break it

The House position is conditional, and it is stated so that it can be proved wrong.

If the ten-year holds above 5.25% and the dollar index holds above 101.5, the squeeze on duration, metals and breadth should continue. Under those conditions, the pattern of the last twenty days would be expected to persist: more sectors joining the decline, credit staying heavy, and technology's lead shifting from a source of strength to the market's main vulnerability. A lone leader is only as safe as the conditions that let it lead. If the discount rate keeps rising, the premium it commands has to be defended with earnings alone.

The falsifier is equally specific. A ten-year close below 5.10%, together with the dollar giving back the week's gain, would say that the move was a spasm of positioning rather than a regime. It would take the pressure off gold and silver, relieve credit, and give the nine-in-ten of the market that has been falling room to stabilise. At 5.28%, the ten-year is 18 basis points from that line. The dollar needs to retrace roughly one percent.

Neither condition has to wait long to be tested. The next ten-year auctions and the coming labour and inflation releases will show whether buyers will take long duration at these levels, and whether the data lean toward a growth story or a rates one. For now the evidence is on the side of rates. The exception, for the moment, is technology.
