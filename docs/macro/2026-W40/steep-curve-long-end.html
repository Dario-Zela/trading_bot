The curve is steep at 128bp, and the long end is doing the work

In one sentence
With the 3M at 3.99% and the 30Y at 5.63%, the market is demanding term premium rather than pricing imminent cuts, and the week's data favours a steep curve staying steep.

The US Treasury curve is steep, and the steepness comes from the far end. The three-month bill yields 3.99%. The ten-year yields 5.28%, a gap of about 128 basis points. Stretch out to the 30-year at 5.63% and the 5-year at 5.05%, and the span between those two adds another 58bp. Short-term borrowing costs are not rising. Investors are asking for more compensation to lend for a decade or three.

A premium, not a forecast

A steep curve can mean two quite different things. It can say the central bank is about to cut, pulling the front end down while the long end holds. Or it can say investors want more for tying up money, pushing the long end up. The shape of this week's curve points to the second reading.

If cuts were being priced for the coming months, the 3M-10Y gap would be mostly a front-end story, with bills sliding under a stable 10-year. Instead the 3M sits at a level that implies no urgency, while the 10-year has moved above 5.25%. The 5Y-30Y spread of 58bp says the pressure grows with maturity. The market is charging for duration: for inflation uncertainty, for the supply of government debt, or for both.

Corporate credit fits that account. The iShares investment-grade bond fund LQD fell 1.34% over five days. High yield and investment grade fell together, a pattern that points to rate sensitivity rather than worry about borrowers' ability to pay. The related credit piece in this edition makes the same case in more detail.

Where a 5.25% ten-year bites

The 10-year is the reference rate for a large share of the economy, so its level matters well beyond the bond market. Mortgage pricing follows it closely, which is why housing is the most exposed. Utilities, heavily indebted and valued partly as bond substitutes, feel it through both financing costs and the competing yield on offer from Treasuries. Financials are the awkward case. A steep curve is traditionally good for lenders, which borrow short and lend long. Yet the sector has been among the weekly laggards, a puzzle taken up separately in this edition. One plausible explanation is that rapid long-end moves damage the value of banks' bond holdings and slow loan demand, offsetting the wider margin.

None of this is new in principle. A 10-year above 5.25% is a different financing environment from one at 4%, and much of the economy was priced for the latter.

What would flatten it

There are two ways out. Either the front end reprices upward, or the long end rallies.

The first route, front-end repricing, would mean the 3M and the short coupons rising toward the long end. That requires the market to conclude that policy will stay tight or tighten further. It would flatten the curve through higher short rates, which is a tightening of conditions, not a relief.

The second route is a long-end rally, with the 10-year and 30-year falling back toward the front. This is the benign flattening, but it needs a reason: softer inflation readings, a retreat in term premium, or a growth scare strong enough to draw buyers into duration.

The week's evidence favours neither cleanly. Real yields and the dollar have been rising together, and the dollar is up about 3% in 20 days. That is the pattern of a market repricing the path of policy rates and real returns, not one buying duration as a haven. Gold has fallen alongside equities, which removes another sign of flight to safety. A rally in long bonds usually needs a haven bid or a clear drop in inflation expectations, and the cross-asset picture shows neither. If anything, the balance of evidence leans toward the long end staying heavy and the curve staying steep. The more likely near-term flattening risk is a hawkish front-end repricing, not a rally in 30-year bonds.

Technology remains the one sector holding up, which suggests investors are still willing to pay for growth despite the rate backdrop. That tolerance has limits, and a longer stay above 5.25% on the 10-year tests it.

What to watch

Three things will settle the question. The next inflation print will show whether the term premium has an inflation story behind it. Treasury auction results at the long end will show how much demand exists at these yields. And the policy-rate path implied by short-dated pricing will show whether the front end starts to move. A 10-year that closes below 5.25% on soft inflation data would be the first sign that the long end is ready to take the curve down. Until then, the steepness is the market's price for waiting.
