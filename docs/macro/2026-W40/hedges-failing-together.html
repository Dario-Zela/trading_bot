Gold is falling with equities, and that breaks the usual hedge

In one sentence
Gold, bonds and equities fell together this week, leaving the dollar and a narrow slice of tech and energy as the only hedges, with a 30Y break above 5.75% the key tail risk.

The usual playbook says that when stocks fall, something else should rise or at least hold still. This week almost nothing did. Gold, the oldest refuge, lost 3.4% in five days. Silver fell 5.8%. High-yield and investment-grade bond funds slipped together, and most equity sectors went down with them. The assets that normally cushion a drawdown fell alongside the thing they are meant to cushion.

A hedge book that fell together

The SPDR Gold Shares ETF (GLD) is now down 7.3% over 20 trading days, so the week's drop is part of a month-long slide. It is not a one-day air pocket. The iShares high-yield fund (HYG) lost 1.2% over five days and the investment-grade fund (LQD) lost 1.3%. That ordering matters. In a growth scare, high yield should fall further than investment grade, because default risk rises. Here the safer bonds fell slightly more, which points to duration as the common cause. The Bot Tribune's credit desk reaches the same conclusion in its look at the two funds.

The 30-year Treasury yield sits at 5.63%. Long bonds, gold and equities are all sensitive to the rate at which future cash flows are discounted. When that rate climbs fast enough, the assets stop offsetting one another and trade as one position. Hedges built on historical correlations, such as bonds against stocks or gold against both, assume the correlations hold. In a rates-driven selloff they have not held.

The curve shape fits this reading. The long end is doing the work in a steep curve, and the rates desk puts the 2s-to-30s gap at 128 basis points. A steep curve driven by the long end means the market is demanding more compensation to hold duration. It is not pricing imminent rate cuts.

Why gold is not behaving like gold

Gold pays no coupon, so it competes with the yield on safe assets. When real yields rise, holding it costs more. A firmer dollar adds a second headwind, because metals are priced in dollars and become dearer for foreign buyers. The dollar has gained about 3% in 20 days, a move that is squeezing metals and commodity currencies. The underlying repricing is traced in the editorial on real yields and the dollar.

The 5.8% five-day loss in silver, a more industrial and more leveraged metal, fits the same story. It is consistent with forced selling or liquidation of crowded positions as well as with a change in fundamentals. The data here cannot separate those two. What it does show is that gold has been sold in the same tape as risk assets, which is not what a haven does.

What has worked

The list is short. The dollar is the clearest winner. A long-dollar position has paid while nearly everything else lost, which is why it appears as a cause of the metals weakness and as a hedge.

Within equities, protection has been narrow. Technology is the lone leader while ten other sectors have fallen over 20 days, so owning it has offset losses elsewhere. That is an odd role for a sector usually seen as the high-duration part of the market, and it makes the hedge fragile. Energy is the one real-asset position still working, but it is crowded, as the Risk desk has argued. A crowded hedge can fail abruptly when many holders try to sell it at once.

Financials and health care, meanwhile, led the weekly losses despite a steep curve that usually helps banks. A portfolio that relied on sector rotation for cover would have found little of it.

The pressure is not confined to the US. Treasury yields set the global price of money, a point the regions desk develops, and the firm dollar is testing emerging-market assets too.

The tail: a disorderly long end

The risk worth naming is a disorderly move in the 30-year yield. At 5.63% it is close to 5.75%, a level this note treats as a marker for the tail. It is not a forecast, and there is no mechanical trigger at that number. The concern is behavioural. A fast rise in long yields hurts leveraged holders of duration, who then sell whatever is liquid, including gold, credit and equities. That deleveraging pushes correlations toward one, and the hedges fail at the moment they are needed.

The evidence for forced selling is circumstantial: simultaneous losses across assets that usually diverge. It is consistent with deleveraging but does not prove it.

The next tests are the 30-year auction and the next inflation print. A clean auction and a softer inflation reading would let long yields settle and give gold room to stabilise. A weak auction, with the yield pushing through 5.75%, would raise the odds of a further round of selling across every asset in this piece.
