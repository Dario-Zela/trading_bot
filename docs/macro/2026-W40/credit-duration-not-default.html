CREDIT · IG VS HY · By T. Larch · 2026-10-04 · 3 min read

High yield and investment grade fell together, so the selling is duration

In one sentence
HYG fell 1.22% and LQD 1.34% in five days, a pattern that fits a rates shock hitting duration rather than a credit scare, though financials' 8.7% slide merits watching.

Credit markets had a bad week without having a credit event. High-yield bonds, the debt of riskier companies, fell 1.22% over five days as measured by the HYG exchange-traded fund. Investment-grade bonds, the debt of the safest borrowers, fell slightly more: LQD lost 1.34%. The gap is 12 basis points, and it points the wrong way for anyone expecting a default scare.

That ordering is the story. When investors fear companies will fail to repay, they dump the weakest paper first and hardest. High yield underperforms investment grade, usually by a wide margin, because the extra yield on junk bonds is compensation for exactly that risk. This week the safer fund lagged. The sell-off looks like a response to interest rates, not to credit quality.

What a rates shock looks like

Investment-grade bonds tend to have longer maturities than high-yield bonds, and a longer bond loses more when yields rise. The measure of that sensitivity is duration. LQD holds a portfolio weighted toward long-dated corporate debt, while HYG's holdings are shorter and carry fatter coupons that cushion price moves. A jump in Treasury yields therefore bruises LQD more, even if nobody doubts the issuers' solvency.

The backdrop fits. The 10-year Treasury yield stands at 5.28%, and the curve is steep, with the long end doing most of the work (see the curve at 128bp). A steep curve punishes long-duration holders specifically. Elsewhere the same pressure shows up in a firmer dollar and in real yields repricing most assets except tech. A 12bp gap in IG's disfavour is what a pure duration move predicts.

Why no widening is not the same as safety

The benign reading has limits. Credit spreads, the extra yield over Treasuries, are the cleaner test of stress, and the evidence here is indirect: relative ETF returns, not spread levels. Both funds also hold bonds whose prices embed the rise in base yields, so a modest widening could be hiding inside a larger rates move.

High yield is also not a clean signal in a week when everything fell. Gold is falling alongside equities, and ten sectors are down over 20 days, with technology the lone leader. When the usual cushions fail together, the absence of a credit blow-up may reflect how little time has passed.

The place to look for stress is financials. The XLF sector fund dropped 2.5% in five days and 8.7% over 20. That is a large decline for a group that might be expected to benefit from a steep curve, which usually helps bank margins; the puzzle is examined in financials and the steep curve. Several explanations are possible. Mark-to-market losses on bond portfolios would be a rates story. Worry about loan quality or funding costs would be a credit story, and it would show up first in bank and lender bonds before it reached the broad high-yield index.

What would change the read

The falsifier is simple. If HYG underperforms LQD by more than one percentage point in a week, the duration explanation fails and a credit explanation takes over. This week's gap was 0.12 points in the opposite direction, a long way from that line.

Until then, the evidence supports a rates sell-off with credit as bystander. That is a narrower claim than "credit is fine." Further moves in the 10-year yield, and whether financials stabilise or keep sliding, will decide whether the bystander stays out of it.
