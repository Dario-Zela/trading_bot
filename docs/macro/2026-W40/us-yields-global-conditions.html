US long yields set the global price of money this week

In one sentence
A 5.28% US 10-year yield and a dollar up 2.96% in 20 days export tighter conditions to emerging markets, European energy importers and China, though foreign data is needed to confirm it.

The price of borrowing money anywhere in the world starts, to a large degree, in Washington's bond market. This week it started high. The US 10-year Treasury yield stands at 5.28% and the 30-year at 5.63%, while the dollar index has climbed 2.96% over 20 trading days. A higher yield on the world's reference asset and a stronger currency to buy it with amount to one thing: tighter financial conditions for everyone who borrows, imports or prices in dollars.

The data available for this piece covers US rates, the dollar and a handful of commodity proxies. It has no foreign yields, no non-US credit spreads and no capital-flow figures. What follows therefore separates what the US numbers show from what they imply for other regions. The second category is inference, and is labelled as such.

The source of the shock

The move is concentrated at the long end. The curve is about 128bp steep, as the rates desk details, which means the pressure comes from what investors demand to hold duration, not from expectations about the next few policy meetings. The credit market agrees: high yield and investment grade have fallen together, a pattern that points to rate sensitivity rather than default fear.

That distinction matters for transmission. A shock driven by US growth would tend to lift export demand abroad. A shock driven by the price of long-dated US money does the opposite. It raises the benchmark against which every other sovereign and corporate borrower is measured, and it does so while the dollar strengthens. The dollar's 3% rise in 20 days is the second half of the same story.

Emerging markets: borrowing in a currency that is getting dearer

The most exposed group is emerging-market borrowers with dollar debt. Their liabilities are fixed in a currency that has just risen about 3%, and the rate at which they would refinance has risen with the 10-year. Neither figure here measures their funding costs directly. The dollar and Treasury moves are the evidence, and the stress itself is inferred.

Base metals offer a market-based read. The DBB base-metals fund is down 4.0% over five days. Metals are priced in dollars, so part of that fall is simple currency arithmetic: a firmer dollar makes them dearer for holders of other currencies and tends to weigh on demand. The size of the one-week drop suggests more than arithmetic, but the data cannot say how much. The emerging-market piece takes up the question of whether commodity exporters are absorbing the hit.

Europe: the energy bill in a stronger dollar

Oil points the other way. The USO oil fund is at $147.37, up 3.7% over 20 days. Europe imports most of its energy, and crude is priced in dollars. A rising oil price and a rising dollar therefore compound each other for a European importer: the same barrel costs more in dollars, and the dollars cost more in euros or sterling.

The exposure is plain even without European data. What the snapshot cannot show is whether European yields have risen in sympathy with Treasuries, or whether the European Central Bank has room to resist. That is the missing piece, and it would be the first thing to check. If European long yields have stayed put, the squeeze falls on currencies and import bills. If they have followed Treasuries up, it also falls on public and corporate borrowing costs.

The energy trade itself is worth a caution. Energy is the one real-asset hedge still working, and it is crowded. A crowded position can reverse quickly, which would ease the import bill but unsettle the only hedge left.

China: managing a currency against a rising dollar

China sits in a different position. Its currency is managed, not freely floated, so a stronger dollar presents Beijing with a choice. It can let the yuan weaken against the dollar, which supports exporters but risks encouraging capital outflows. Or it can defend the exchange rate, which tightens domestic conditions at a time when growth is the priority.

The snapshot holds no yuan data, so which path China is taking cannot be established here. The base-metals decline is the available read-through. Industrial metals are a common proxy for Chinese construction and manufacturing demand, and a 4.0% weekly fall is consistent with markets doubting that demand. It is also consistent with the dollar effect described above. The two explanations cannot be separated with the numbers on hand.

What the pattern says, and what to watch

Two things in the broader tape fit the idea that US rates are the common driver. Gold is falling with equities, and the cross-asset repricing described in the editorial analysis points to real yields and the dollar as the organising forces. Within equities, technology stands alone as the leader, which is what a market looks like when it is rewarding assets that can fund themselves.

The next tests are specific. Foreign long yields are the missing evidence: if European and Asian benchmarks are moving with the 5.28% US 10-year, the export of tight conditions is confirmed. The dollar index is the second gauge. A pause in its 3% advance would take pressure off emerging-market borrowers and European importers even if Treasury yields stay high. The next US inflation and labour-market releases are the likeliest triggers for either, since they feed directly into how much term premium investors demand at 30 years.
