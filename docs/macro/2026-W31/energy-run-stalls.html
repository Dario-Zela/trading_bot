SECTORS · ENERGY · By I. Aresti · 2026-08-02 · 5 min read

Energy's monster run stalls out

An offshore oil platform in the North Sea — energy's summer rally was built more on a Middle East war-risk premium than on production economics. — Erik Christensen / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
XLE's 11.9% monthly surge stalled flat this week as oil fell 5.5%, with a fading Hormuz war-risk premium and a fresh OPEC+ supply hike unwinding the rally's real driver.

For most of July, energy was the easiest trade on the desk. The Energy Select Sector SPDR Fund (XLE) climbed 11.9% over twenty trading days, crushing every other sector in the S&P 500 and turning what had been a sleepy, unloved corner of the market into 2026's top performer — up 29.4% on the year, ahead of even technology's 23% gain. Then, in the span of about a week, it just stopped. XLE finished essentially flat, down 0.12%, while the commodity that had been doing all the work underneath it — crude oil — fell hard.

The United States Oil Fund (USO), a reasonable proxy for spot crude, dropped 5.5% over five trading days after having rallied 24.2% over the prior twenty. That is not a gentle cooling-off. It is a sharp reversal, and it landed at almost the exact moment XLE's momentum ran out. The two charts are telling the same story from two different vantage points: the rally in energy stocks was, more than anything else, a rally in the price of oil itself, and the thing driving that price higher has started to go the other way.

What was actually driving the rally

Energy's summer surge was not really about balance sheets or production discipline. It was about the Strait of Hormuz. A ceasefire between the United States and Iran, brokered in mid-June, collapsed in July after Iran struck commercial vessels that had bypassed a preapproved shipping route. What followed was an escalating exchange — Iranian strikes on Gulf shipping and on U.S. allies including Bahrain and Kuwait, American retaliatory strikes, and eventually two consecutive nights of what the U.S. called "offensive" action against Iranian targets. Brent crude, which had been trading in the mid-$70s in June, spiked above $100 a barrel on July 23 for the first time since May — a 7% move in a single session.

That is the backdrop against which XLE's 11.9% twenty-day run has to be read. It was a war-risk premium, not a demand story, and it showed up directly in earnings. Chevron reported its largest quarterly profit ever on July 31 — net income near $12 billion, up nearly fourfold from a year earlier — while ExxonMobil posted $14.5 billion in profit, its best haul since the early days of the Russia-Ukraine war. Exxon still missed estimates on refining-margin trouble, and its shares fell more than 2% on the print even as Chevron's rose about 1%, a split that hinted the market was already starting to look past the headline crude price toward the mechanics underneath it.

The reversal, in real time

The unwind came fast, and from two directions at once. First, the geopolitical premium started to deflate: reports emerged that the U.S. would hold off on further strikes after discussions between the American side and Saudi Arabia's Mohammed bin Salman, with signs that a framework to reopen the Strait of Hormuz to normal shipping was taking shape. Second, OPEC+ moved to add supply. The group agreed to raise output by roughly 188,000 barrels a day starting in September — its fifth consecutive monthly increase and, by its own account, the completion of the unwind of the voluntary cuts it had maintained since 2023. A Rystad Energy analyst put it plainly: "Having completed the restoration campaign, OPEC+ has little incentive to rush into further supply changes," which markets read as one less reason for prices to stay elevated.

The combination hit crude hard. Brent fell from roughly $91 to $84 and WTI slid to around $81, a decline of more than 13% from the July highs. That is the move USO's 5.5% five-day drop is picking up. It is worth noting the reversal was violent enough that, over a weekend when futures markets were closed, traders reportedly rotated into crude-linked perpetual contracts on crypto platforms and prediction markets just to keep a position on — a sign of how much of the recent energy trade had become a geopolitical bet as much as an investment thesis.

Consolidation or something more

The honest answer is that both readings are defensible, and the difference matters for how the desk should be positioned into September. The bull case is straightforward: XLE went from laggard to sector leader almost entirely on a war-risk premium that was always going to be volatile, and a flat week after an 11.9% run is not a breakdown — it is digestion. Energy-company fundamentals, as Chevron's record quarter shows, were strong even before the spike, and a return to a calmer Strait of Hormuz does not erase the underlying demand for oil or the capital discipline producers have shown since 2023.

The more cautious reading leans on the supply side. Wall Street's consensus going into the second half of 2026 was already for oversupply — Reuters' analyst poll had WTI averaging near $59 for the year, and the IEA had flagged a glut approaching 3.84 million barrels a day. Julius Baer's Norbert Rucker framed it bluntly before the latest escalation even began: oil prices were "bloated with a decent geopolitical risk premium," and once that risk premium fades, "the focus should return on the supply glut and the lasting pressure on prices." If the Strait of Hormuz genuinely stabilizes and OPEC+ keeps adding barrels into a market analysts already thought was oversupplied, the argument for XLE re-accelerating gets much harder to make. A flat week would then look less like a pause and more like the first data point of a longer fade.

What happens next depends less on anything in XLE's own price action than on whether the Hormuz de-escalation holds. Iran has broken ceasefire terms before — the June memorandum of understanding lasted barely a month before shipping attacks resumed — and a second collapse would likely send crude, and energy stocks with it, right back toward the July highs. Barring that, the next real test comes with September OPEC+ compliance data and the first full month of the additional 188,000 barrels a day reaching the market, which will start to show whether the supply-glut case Wall Street had been building before the war interrupted it was right all along.
