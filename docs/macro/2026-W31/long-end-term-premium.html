RATES · TERM PREMIUM · By G. Mehlman · 2026-08-02 · 3 min read

A 5.275% 30-year is a fiscal story as much as a monetary one

The U.S. Treasury Building in Washington, D.C. — issuance decisions made here are doing more to move the long bond than the Federal Reserve is. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The 30-year yield at 5.275% versus a 3.68% 3-month bill shows the long end pricing deficits and supply, not Fed policy.

The federal funds rate has sat at 3.50%–3.75% since the Federal Reserve's fifth consecutive hold in July, and three-month Treasury bills trade at roughly 3.68% — a fair reflection of where the policy rate actually is, not where traders think it's going. If the story at the long end of the curve were about the Fed, the 30-year bond wouldn't be sitting at 5.275%.

That's a 159-basis-point gap between the bill and the bond, and it isn't closing. The 10-year, at roughly 4.74%, sits about 53 basis points inside the 30-year — a gap that has been widening for weeks as the long bond cheapens relative to the belly of the curve. None of this moves on Fed-meeting dates. It moves on auction results, refunding announcements and inflation prints that arrive months apart.

A premium for time, not for policy

What's repricing is the term premium — the extra yield investors demand to hold long-dated debt rather than rolling over short paper, compensation for the risk that inflation, deficits or policy surprises erode the value of a bond over three decades. For most of the post-2008 era, quantitative easing kept that premium pinned near zero or negative. It has been reasserting itself for months, and the Treasury's own financing arithmetic explains why: the CRFB and market participants both put the fiscal 2026 deficit near $2 trillion, and Treasury's own estimates point to roughly $5 trillion in combined new borrowing and refinancing needs this year, with about $3 trillion of that rolling over existing debt. Treasury has signaled it is still weighing increases to nominal coupon auction sizes to cover the gap.

Supply of that size doesn't clear itself. Every additional coupon auction needs a buyer willing to take duration risk at a price, and the marginal buyer this year has needed convincing. Core PCE inflation running at 3.4% in May, up from 3.0% in December, hasn't helped the case for locking in today's yields for thirty years — it raises the odds that the real return on a 5.275% bond looks a lot thinner by the time it matures.

Why front-end cuts wouldn't fix this

The mechanical link between Fed policy and long yields runs through the expected path of short rates, and that path is already reasonably well understood by the market — hence a stable 3M yield near the current funds rate. A cut or two at the front end would pull the 3-month bill down, but it would do little to the term premium sitting on top of the 30-year, which is priced off supply, deficit trajectories and inflation risk over a horizon the Fed doesn't control. That's the mechanism behind the 10s30s spread holding near 53 basis points even as short-rate expectations stay anchored: the belly of the curve tracks policy reasonably well, and the long end increasingly doesn't.

This matters beyond the Treasury market. A 5.275% risk-free rate over thirty years sets a floor under discount rates used to value everything from equities to private credit, and it raises the government's own borrowing costs at the moment its deficit is at its widest. Risk assets can keep grinding higher against that backdrop, but the room for multiple expansion narrows every time the long bond cheapens another few basis points — a repricing shock at the long end would hit valuations well before it hits Main Street borrowing costs.

The next real test arrives with the August refunding announcement and auction calendar, where Treasury will show whether it leans further into long-dated coupons or shifts the burden back toward bills. A weak 30-year auction — measured by a wide tail or soft indirect bidder participation — would be the clearest sign yet that the term-premium repricing has further to run.
