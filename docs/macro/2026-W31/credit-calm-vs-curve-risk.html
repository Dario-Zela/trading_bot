RISK · CREDIT VS RATES · By S. Vance · 2026-08-02 · 5 min read

Credit's calm assumes a soft landing the curve hasn't confirmed

In one sentence
HY spreads near 2007 tights and a ripping XLY both bet on a soft landing, but a steepening curve at 5.275% and oil's 24%-to-negative reversal haven't confirmed it.

Everything in markets this week has been leaning the same direction. The dollar broke lower. High-yield bonds outran investment-grade debt. Consumer discretionary stocks, left for dead a month ago, snapped back hard. Taken together, it looks like a classic risk-on rotation — the kind of tape that shows up when investors decide the economy is going to be fine and rates are no longer the thing to worry about.

The problem is that nobody told the long end of the Treasury curve. The 30-year yield sits at 5.275%, within a hair of its highest level of the move, and it got there not because growth is booming but because the curve keeps steepening — the unmistakable signature of a market pricing more term premium, not more optimism. Credit markets are behaving as though that tension has already been resolved. It hasn't.

The rotation everyone's leaning into

The numbers on the credit side are genuinely striking. High-yield spreads have compressed to roughly 281 basis points over Treasuries, according to ICE BofA index data — inside the richest decile of the index's history and a level breached only about 5% of the time since 2000. Investment-grade spreads, at roughly 81 basis points, sit near territory last visited in 1998. Over the past five trading days, high yield has outperformed investment grade by close to 30 basis points even as the long bond sold off — a pattern that only makes sense if credit investors believe higher long rates reflect a stronger economy rather than a fiscal or liquidity problem.

Equities have told the same story. Consumer discretionary — the sector most directly levered to household spending and most sensitive to financing costs on autos, homes and credit cards — posted the strongest weekly gain in the S&P 500, clawing back a chunk of its recent laggard status. That kind of move usually needs two things in place at once: confidence in the consumer, and confidence that rates aren't about to choke off the credit that consumer relies on. Right now the market has the first without much proof of the second.

The long end isn't confirming any of it

The 30-year didn't get to 5.275% on strong data alone. The move has been driven by what fixed-income strategists increasingly describe as a bear steepener — short rates anchored or falling as the Federal Reserve eases, with effective fed funds around 3.63%, while long rates climb on concerns that have little to do with the business cycle: the volume of Treasury issuance the market has to absorb, persistent federal deficits, and a term premium that had been suppressed for over a decade and is now reasserting itself. The 2s10s spread has widened to roughly 35 basis points, a steepening that analysts at firms including DoubleLine have tied explicitly to fiscal anxiety rather than growth optimism.

That distinction matters enormously for credit. A steepening curve driven by strong growth is bullish for spread product — more revenue, more earnings, easier debt service. A steepening curve driven by term premium and supply concerns is a different animal entirely: it raises the cost of capital across the board, pressures anything long-duration or rate-sensitive, and does so without the offsetting benefit of a genuinely stronger economy. Credit spreads this tight aren't distinguishing between the two.

Oil's whipsaw is the warning shot

If there's a preview of how quickly sentiment can turn, it happened in the commodity market over the past ten days. Crude had rallied more than 24% over the prior month as fears of a wider Middle East conflict built a geopolitical premium into the price. That premium came out almost as fast as it went in: after the U.S. and Iran signed a memorandum of understanding to de-escalate and reopen the Strait of Hormuz to fuller traffic, WTI reversed by roughly 5.5% over five trading days, falling back below its 200-day moving average.

That kind of reversal — a 24% rally erased in days on a single geopolitical data point — is exactly the sort of shock that credit spreads at 2007-era tights are not built to absorb gracefully. It didn't take a recession or a default cycle to move oil that violently. It took one memorandum of understanding. A term-premium shock working the other way, or a supply surprise reversing the reversal, would ask more of credit markets than 281 basis points of spread is currently pricing.

The hedges got sold at the wrong moment

What makes the current setup more exposed, rather than less, is what happened to the assets that normally cushion a rate scare. Utilities fell 4.19% over five days and real estate dropped 1.92% — the two sectors most directly hurt by higher long rates, and traditionally the ones investors rotate into as ballast when the curve looks dangerous. Instead, both were sold hardest, alongside the broader rotation out of defensives and into cyclicals. The portfolio effect is that the market has simultaneously bid up the assets most levered to a soft landing and dumped the assets that would perform best if the long end keeps cheapening. That is a coherent bet only if the soft landing is the sure thing credit spreads imply. The curve, still steepening, still fiscally driven, has not confirmed that.

The near-term catalysts are clear enough: the next Treasury refunding announcement will test appetite for long-duration supply directly, and any reacceleration in oil — whether from renewed Middle East friction or an OPEC+ supply decision — would reintroduce the same volatility that just rattled crude into a market that has priced almost none of it into spread product. Until the term-premium story resolves one way or the other, the gap between what credit assumes and what the curve is actually saying remains the most underpriced risk in the tape.
