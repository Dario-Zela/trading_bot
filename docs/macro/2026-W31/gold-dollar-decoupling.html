Gold isn't playing along with the weak dollar

400-ounce gold bars — bullion's usual inverse relationship with the dollar broke down this week. — Andrzej Barabasz / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Gold fell even as the dollar broke below 100 — a decoupling that points to firm real yields and hawkish Fed positioning overriding the currency effect.

Currency traders had a clean story this week: the dollar cracked below the 100 level on the DXY index, its worst weekly showing in three months, and risk assets took it as a green light. Gold was supposed to be the easiest beneficiary — bullion is priced in dollars, so a weaker greenback should mechanically make it cheaper for foreign buyers and more attractive as a hedge. Instead, GLD slipped 0.10% over five days and 1.74% over twenty. Silver did worse, with SLV down 0.44% and 4.83% over the same windows. Base metals went the other way entirely: DBB, the industrial-metals proxy dominated by copper, gained 1.90% over five days and 5.01% over twenty.

That's three different metals complexes moving three different directions against a single, supposedly dominant, currency signal. When gold and the dollar move in the same direction, it usually means something else is setting the price of gold — and this week, the obvious candidate is real interest rates.

The Fed didn't cooperate

The Federal Reserve held its benchmark rate at 3.50%–3.75% on July 29, its fifth straight hold, but the vote was 9–3, with three dissenters pushing for a hike rather than a cut. That's a hawkish split dressed up as a hold. The June dot plot had already nudged the committee's own year-end rate expectations up, to a range of 3.6%–4.1% from 3.25%–3.75% previously, and renewed Middle East tension has kept oil-driven inflation fears alive enough to harden that stance further. None of that supports lower real yields, which is the channel gold actually trades on. TIPS-implied real rates staying firm — even as nominal Treasury yields and the dollar soften on separate fiscal and term-premium pressures — raises the opportunity cost of holding a non-yielding metal regardless of what the currency is doing.

A rally that already ran hot

Context matters here. Gold had already had an extraordinary year, spiking toward $5,595 an ounce in January on aggressive rate-cut bets before the Fed's hawkish turn — driven partly by the same Iran-linked oil shock — sent speculative money rushing back out of the ETF complex. That means positioning going into this week's dollar break was already leaner and more skittish than it was at the start of 2026, which makes gold more sensitive to a hawkish surprise and less inclined to simply track a currency chart. Silver's steeper drop fits the same pattern with an added twist: speculative and leveraged length in the metal has been unwinding faster than in gold, and industrial substitution — solar manufacturers thrifting silver paste in favor of copper plating — has chipped at the demand story that once made silver the more bullish half of the precious-metals pair.

Base metals, by contrast, are trading on a fundamentally different demand book. Copper — more than half of DBB's weight — is being pulled by tight concentrate supply, tariff-driven stockpiling ahead of possible US import duties, and genuine electrification and data-center demand. That's an industrial story with almost no connection to the Fed's real-rate stance, which is exactly why DBB could rally through the same week gold and silver fell.

The read here is not that the dollar-gold relationship is broken for good — it has decoupled and re-coupled before, most notably through 2024 and 2025 when central-bank buying replaced ETF flows as the marginal source of demand. It is that this week's move should not be filed under a simple "weak dollar, reflation trade" narrative. The next test comes with July's jobs report and the September FOMC meeting, where futures markets are still debating whether the Fed's next move is a cut or, as three committee members already want, a hike. Until that real-rate picture clarifies, gold looks like it is trading its own book, not the currency's.
