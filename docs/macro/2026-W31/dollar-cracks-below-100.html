The dollar cracks below 100

The dollar index broke below the 100 level for the first time in months, closing the week at 99.80. — Milad Mosapoor / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
DXY closed at 99.80, down 1.65% in a week that saw its slow monthly drift suddenly accelerate — a sign of rotation out of dollar assets, not a safe-haven scramble.

The dollar did something Friday it hadn't managed in months: it closed below 100 on the index traders use as shorthand for its overall strength. The Dollar Index (DXY) ended the week at 99.80, down 1.65% over five sessions. The round number gets the headlines, but the more interesting story is in the shape of the move — this wasn't a slow drift finally crossing a line. It was a lurch.

In one sentence: DXY closed the week at 99.80, down 1.65%, as a month of slow dollar drift suddenly accelerated into a sharper break — evidence of capital rotating out of dollar assets rather than a rush to safety.

A slow leak becomes a break

Over the trailing 20 sessions, DXY is down 1.05%. If the decline had been steady — a grind lower, a few basis points a day — the five-day figure would look like roughly a quarter of that number. Instead the five-day move, at -1.65%, is about one-and-a-half times the size of the 20-day move. Put plainly: more than the entire month's worth of losses arrived in the final week, and then some. The dollar wasn't creeping toward 100. It fell through it.

That kind of acceleration usually has a trigger, and this week supplied two. On July 29 the Federal Reserve held its benchmark rate at 3.50%–3.75% for a fifth consecutive meeting, a decision that split the committee 9-3. The dissents were the unusual part: Cleveland's Beth Hammack, Minneapolis's Neel Kashkari and Dallas's Lorie Logan all wanted a quarter-point hike, arguing that inflation risk tied to the ongoing Middle East conflict warranted tighter policy. Chair Kevin Warsh, five weeks into the job, held the line with the majority. A Fed that could plausibly have tightened chose not to — and declined to hand the dollar the rate-differential support a hike would have provided.

Not a flight to safety

A falling dollar usually tells one of two stories. Either the Fed is easing and everything gets a lift together, or capital is fleeing US assets in a panic — in which case risk assets tend to wobble too. Neither quite fits here. The Fed didn't cut. And rather than a broad risk-off unwind, the same week produced a distinctly risk-on tape: high-yield credit outperforming investment grade, and consumer discretionary stocks snapping back hard. Spreads tightening while the funding currency weakens is not the signature of a scramble for safety. It's closer to the signature of money that had parked itself in dollar assets deciding it's comfortable moving elsewhere.

That reading lines up with what several bank strategists have been arguing about 2026 more broadly — that the dollar's softness this year looks cyclical rather than structural, driven by narrowing rate differentials and a rising global growth surprise index that's making non-US assets look more interesting again, not by a loss of confidence in the currency itself.

Tokyo's hand is showing too

The same week carried a second thread. After the yen slid to a 40-year low against the dollar, Japanese authorities intervened to buy yen on Thursday and Friday, with the New York Fed reportedly selling euros for yen on behalf of the US Treasury — the first coordinated US-Japan intervention since 2011. Finance Minister Satsuki Katayama confirmed the joint action once the yen had rallied more than 1% against both the dollar and the euro.

The yen carries a heavy weight in the dollar index's basket, so a sharp yen rally mechanically drags DXY down regardless of what's happening elsewhere. But it's not a separate story from the rotation thesis so much as a related symptom: a lot of the yen's multi-year weakness was funded by carry trades borrowing cheap yen to buy dollar assets. When that trade even partially unwinds — whether prompted by intervention or by the same shift in relative growth expectations pulling capital toward cyclicals and credit — the dollar side of the ledger takes the hit too.

What the rotation is pricing

Put together, the week reads less like a dollar crisis and more like a repricing: a market deciding that the growth and policy gap between the US and the rest of the world is narrower than it assumed, and reallocating accordingly. That's consistent with credit desks staying calm even as the currency breaks a psychological level, and with cyclical stocks catching a bid instead of selling off alongside the dollar. Whether it holds depends on the data still to come.

Friday's jobs report — due August 7 — is the next test of the growth-differential story underpinning the rotation; a soft print would reinforce the case for a Fed cut and likely extend the dollar's slide, while a strong one would complicate the rotation narrative considerably. The Fed's September meeting, and whether Thursday's hawkish dissents gain company, will show whether this week's hold was a pause or the start of a longer standoff between the committee and its own inflation hawks.
