Dollar catches its breath, but the trend hasn't turned

In one sentence
DXY's flat week (+0.07%) inside a 1.26% monthly slide looks like consolidation, as rising 30-year yields fail to lift the dollar amid deficit and term-premium concerns.

The dollar index closed the week at 99.67, up a scant 0.07 percent from where it started. That is not a rally. It is a currency market catching its breath — and even that modest gain sits inside a much larger retreat: DXY is still down 1.26 percent over the past 20 trading days, and off nearly 2 percent from its late-July high near 101.70.

What makes the week worth a second look isn't the flat headline number. It's what didn't happen. The 30-year Treasury yield has been sitting above 5.25 percent — a level unseen since 2007 — and in the textbook version of currency markets, that kind of long-end move should be pulling capital toward dollar assets and lifting the greenback with it. Instead, the dollar shrugged. The rate-differential playbook that has driven FX for a generation didn't show up for work this week.

A pause, not a turn

The technical picture explains the stillness. DXY has spent August compressed between its 50-day moving average near 100.6, which is declining, and its 200-day average near 99.2, which is gently rising — a narrowing wedge that traders read as indecision rather than direction. A support shelf between roughly 99.45 and 99.58 has now held twice this month, catching the index on August 4 and again on August 11 before this week's marginal bounce.

Three consecutive soft data points did most of the damage that set up this consolidation. The July jobs report, released August 7, missed badly enough to knock the wind out of hawkish Fed bets. Consumer prices on August 12 came in at 3.4 percent year-over-year, down from 3.5 percent, with core inflation at 2.5 percent — the softest print since early in the year. Producer prices followed on August 13, flat month-over-month against expectations for a 0.2 percent rise. Together, those three releases pulled the market's odds of a September rate move sharply away from a hike: pricing for a September increase fell to roughly 40 percent from above 54 percent the week before.

That detail matters because it reframes what's actually being debated at the Fed right now. This isn't a straightforward cutting cycle. At the July 29 meeting, three policymakers dissented in favor of raising rates rather than holding — a hawkish revolt driven by inflation that has proven stickier than hoped, with producer prices still running at a 4.7 percent annual pace. The dollar's malaise, in other words, has been unfolding against a backdrop where a meaningful bloc of the Fed actually wants tighter policy, not looser. That the currency still can't find a bid says something about what's weighing on it beyond the rate path.

The playbook that didn't fire

Higher long-end yields are supposed to be dollar-supportive because they widen the return advantage US assets offer over German bunds or Japanese government bonds. That mechanism worked reliably for years. It has stopped working cleanly in 2026, and the reason increasingly traces back to why yields are high in the first place.

The 30-year yield's climb past 5.25 percent isn't a story about a hawkish Fed defending against inflation — it's a term-premium story. Investors are demanding more compensation to hold long-dated US paper because of what's piling up on the other side of the ledger: a federal deficit that hit $1.8 trillion for the first ten months of fiscal 2026, already $169 billion ahead of the same stretch last year, with the Congressional Budget Office now projecting a full-year shortfall of $2.1 trillion. Interest payments on the debt reached $857 billion over the first nine months of the fiscal year, up 13 percent from a year earlier, as older, cheaper debt rolls into today's higher rates.

When yields rise because the Fed is fighting inflation, the dollar tends to benefit — it's a signal of monetary tightening. When yields rise because investors are pricing in more supply and more fiscal risk, the read is murkier, and can cut the other way. A widening current account deficit compounds the problem: the US has increasingly relied on short-term portfolio and equity inflows to fund that gap rather than the kind of durable, yield-chasing capital that reinforces a currency. Strip away the confidence that flows are sticky, and a bigger deficit becomes a currency liability rather than a currency asset.

Natixis has pointed to DXY drifting toward 98 by year-end on this logic, and Morgan Stanley has staked out a similar depreciation call, arguing that the short-term rate support propping up the dollar is fading faster than the structural drags are easing. The dollar's real effective exchange rate remains in the upper half of its recent range, still carrying an overvaluation premium that strategists have treated as a standing bearish offset even during weeks when the currency holds firm.

What the rest of the market is doing

The cross-currency picture backs up the consolidation read. The euro traded near $1.1567 this week, close to two-month highs, helped along by a modest bid for anything that isn't the dollar. The yen has been the more interesting mover — USD/JPY slipped toward 159.30, and the yen has strengthened almost 1.8 percent against the dollar over the past month even as it remains down more than 8 percent over the past year. None of this reads as a dollar collapse. It reads as a market that has stopped extending the same trade it ran through most of the summer and is waiting for a reason to pick a direction again.

Oil above $83 a barrel, on the back of the ongoing US-Iran standoff and disruption risk around the Strait of Hormuz, has added a competing pull — geopolitical stress usually finds some safe-haven bid for the dollar, and it may be the main reason the index hasn't broken cleanly below its 200-day average already. That tension, between a rate story arguing for a weaker dollar and a risk story arguing for a stronger one, is precisely what a compressing chart looks like when neither side wins.

What's next

The next real test arrives August 27-29 at the Kansas City Fed's Jackson Hole symposium, where Kevin Warsh will deliver his first policy address as Fed chair since being sworn in on May 22. Warsh has spent his confirmation process insisting he won't simply deliver the rate cuts Trump has pushed for, and how he frames the inflation-versus-growth tension — with three of his colleagues on record wanting to hike — will tell the market more about the dollar's next leg than any single data print. Until then, the August payrolls report due in early September and whatever the deficit tracker shows for the close of the fiscal year in September are the numbers most likely to break DXY out of its shelf between 99.45 and 100.60, in one direction or the other.
