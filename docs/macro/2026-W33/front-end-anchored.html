The Fed's front end hasn't gotten the term-premium memo

The U.S. Treasury Building in Washington, D.C. — long-end auctions have drawn weak demand even as the front end stays anchored to the Fed's policy rate. — Rchuon24 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
The 3-month bill sits at 3.70% while the 30-year yields 5.27% — a twist, not a Fed-cutting signal, and it changes what curve steepeners are actually betting on.

The bond market has produced a genuinely strange picture this month, and it is worth sitting with the numbers before reaching for a headline. The 3-month Treasury bill yields 3.70%. The 30-year bond yields 5.27%. That is a gap of roughly 156 basis points spanning the length of the curve — and almost the entire move belongs to the long end.

This matters because the two ends of the curve are supposed to be telling related stories. They are not, right now. The front end is anchored to the federal funds rate, which the Federal Reserve has held at 3.50%–3.75% since its July 29 meeting, with the effective rate sitting at 3.63%. Futures markets are not pricing cuts anytime soon — if anything, after a run of firmer core inflation prints and an energy-price shock tied to the Iran conflict, some desks have started pricing a chance of a hike before year-end. The bill market has taken note: 3.70% is barely different from where the funds rate already sits, which tells you the market expects the Fed to stay put for a while.

The 30-year, meanwhile, closed July at 5.27% — a level last seen in 2007 — and has kept climbing into August on the back of soft demand at recent coupon auctions. A 30-year sale on August 13 was graded poorly by auction trackers, extending a run of subpar results for long-dated debt. Interest on the national debt reached $857 billion in the first nine months of the fiscal year, up 13% from a year earlier, with total federal debt near $39.8 trillion. Investors are not panicking about that math, but they are charging more to hold it.

Where the curve actually bends

The 5-year note offers the cleanest evidence that this is not a uniform sell-off. At 4.36%, it sits much closer to the anchored front end than to the surging back end — roughly 66 basis points above the 3-month bill, versus 91 basis points between the 5-year and the 30-year alone. Whatever is repricing here is concentrated beyond the ten-year point, not spread evenly across maturities.

That distinction separates two very different explanations. A bull steepener — front rates falling faster than back rates — would say the market expects the Fed to cut soon. A twist, where the front stays flat while duration well out the curve gets repriced, points somewhere else entirely: term premium, the extra compensation investors demand for the uncertainty of holding long paper through decades of deficits, inflation surprises, and unknown Fed reaction functions. Bloomberg strategists have flagged exactly this shift in recent weeks, describing a structurally higher term premium and persistent deficits as a plausible new equilibrium for Treasuries rather than a passing auction wobble.

Why the label matters

For a fund running an outright long-duration position, the distinction is close to irrelevant — a loss on the 30-year is a loss regardless of what's driving it. But for anyone positioned around the shape of the curve rather than its level, the difference is the whole trade. A steepener built on the expectation of Fed cuts assumes the front end eventually falls to meet the back — a bull-steepening resolution. A twist steepener driven by fiscal supply and term premium carries no such promise; the front end can sit at 3.70% indefinitely while the long end keeps drifting, because nothing about the Fed's reaction function is forcing it to move. Curve strategies calibrated for the first scenario are exposed to a very different risk profile in the second.

The next real test comes with the September FOMC meeting and the accompanying dot plot, which will show whether policymakers still see room to cut into 2027 or whether the hawkish repricing markets have already made is now showing up in the Fed's own projections. Until then, the front end has nothing new to say — and the long end, for now, is doing all the talking.
