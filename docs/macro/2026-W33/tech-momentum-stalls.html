Tech's monthly run is stalling in real time

Market data displayed on screens in Times Square — technology has led the market's monthly gains but is now feeling the pull of higher long-term yields. — bfishadow / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
XLK's monthly gain hides a stalling week as 30-year yields near 5.3% squeeze duration-sensitive tech and discretionary stocks alike.

Technology stocks are still, on paper, the best story of the month. The Technology Select Sector SPDR Fund — XLK, the ETF proxy for the S&P 500's tech giants — is up 8.2% over the past 20 trading days, comfortably the top-performing sector in the index. But look at the last five sessions alone and the picture changes: XLK managed just 1.1%. The engine that has been pulling the market higher all month is idling, and it is idling at the exact moment long-term borrowing costs are climbing to levels not seen since before the 2008 financial crisis.

That timing is not coincidental. Technology, and especially the AI-capex-heavy names that dominate XLK's weighting, are what portfolio managers call long-duration assets: their value depends on cash flows expected years or decades from now, which makes them unusually sensitive to the discount rate used to value those future dollars. When long yields rise, that discount rate rises with them, and the math behind today's tech valuations gets less forgiving. It is the same logic that governs a 30-year bond's price sensitivity to a rate move, just expressed through a chipmaker's price-to-earnings multiple instead of a coupon.

A month of leadership, a week of hesitation

The monthly number is real and it is large. Nothing else in the S&P 500's sector lineup comes close to XLK's 8.2% twenty-day gain, and year-to-date the sector has been the market's undisputed leader, dragged upward by AI infrastructure spending that shows no sign, at the headline level, of slowing. Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Microsoft are collectively on pace to lift capital expenditure by roughly 77% this year to something near $725 billion, according to Financial Times reporting — a number so large it has become its own market narrative independent of quarterly earnings.

But fund-flow data is starting to tell a different story than the price chart. XLK has seen roughly $840 million in net outflows over the trailing five days, a sign that at least some money is stepping off the trade even as the sector's monthly total return stays impressive. Outflows and a flat week don't mean the AI rally is over — the index is still near its highs — but they mean the marginal buyer has gotten more cautious, and that caution is showing up before the monthly return numbers catch up to it.

The long end is doing the talking

The more direct explanation sits in the Treasury market. The 30-year yield closed July at 5.27%, its highest level since 2007, and has stayed elevated through the first half of August. Analysts have flagged 5.25% specifically as a threshold beyond which equity valuations tend to see a "more durable pullback" rather than a shrug-and-move-on reaction — and tech, as the sector with the longest effective duration in the index, is first in line to feel it.

The move at the long end isn't primarily a Federal Reserve story — the front end of the curve has been comparatively anchored, which is itself the subject of a companion piece in this section. It is a term-premium story: widening federal deficits, heavier Treasury issuance, and tariff-driven inflation expectations are pushing investors to demand more compensation for holding duration, independent of what the Fed does with short-term rates. That distinction matters for tech investors, because it means the pressure on valuations isn't obviously going away with a rate cut. It is structural, tied to how much the government needs to borrow and at what premium the market will lend.

Discretionary is the tell

If this were purely an AI-specific worry — chip-demand jitters, circular financing concerns among the hyperscalers, the kind of thing that knocked more than a trillion dollars off semiconductor names in a single stretch of trading in late July — it would show up only in tech. It isn't only in tech. Consumer discretionary, tracked by the XLY ETF, was the single worst-performing sector this week, down 1.38%. Discretionary retailers, travel and leisure names carry their own duration exposure through financing costs and, more directly, through a consumer whose spending power erodes as borrowing gets more expensive across the board.

Two sectors with genuinely different business models — one selling cloud infrastructure and semiconductors, the other selling vacations and home goods — losing ground in the same week, for what looks like the same underlying reason, is a stronger signal than either one alone. It suggests the market is repricing rate sensitivity as a factor, not just re-rating individual AI-spending stories on their own merits.

What a full rotation would look like

None of this amounts to a rout. XLK's monthly return is still the envy of every other sector, and a single soft week is well within normal variation for a group that has run this hard. The question worth watching is whether the deceleration compounds — whether next week's XLK number comes in flat or negative alongside a 30-year yield that keeps grinding higher, and whether defensive, short-duration sectors keep picking up the flows that tech and discretionary are shedding. Utilities' defensive bid this week, even against a negative month overall, is an early data point in that direction.

The next real test arrives with the next batch of hyperscaler capex commentary and any fresh 30-year Treasury auction results, both of which will show whether the market's newfound rate sensitivity is a one-week blip or the start of the leadership rotation that duration-sensitive growth investors have been bracing for since yields first pushed through 5%.
