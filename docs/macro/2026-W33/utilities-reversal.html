Utilities catch a defensive bid even as the month stays negative

High-voltage transmission towers carry power across the grid — utilities' AI-driven demand story is now competing with the sector's traditional bond-proxy identity. — Stefan Andrej Shambora / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
XLU's 1.6% weekly gain — its best non-energy sector showing — reversed a negative month just as 30-year yields hit 5.2%, and it's unclear yet if that's rotation or just a bounce.

Utilities were the one corner of the non-energy market that moved the right way this week, and the timing is what makes it worth a second look.

The Utilities Select Sector SPDR Fund (XLU) rose 1.61% over the past five trading days, the best showing among non-energy sectors, even as the group remains down 1.90% over the past month. That's a sharp reversal happening inside a negative trend — and it landed in the same stretch that long-dated Treasury yields pushed to their highest levels in years, with the 30-year hitting 5.216% at a Treasury auction on August 13, a level not seen since 2001.

On paper, that timing shouldn't work. Utilities are dividend-heavy, rate-sensitive stocks that are supposed to struggle when bond yields climb, because a 5% risk-free Treasury competes directly with a utility's payout. That relationship broke down for most of July too — XLU, along with names like NextEra Energy and Constellation Energy, outperformed the S&P 500 even as the 10-year yield rose from 4.47% to 4.73% over the month, according to Finimize reporting on the sector's unusual resilience.

Two stories, one chart

There are two honest readings of the bounce, and they're not mutually exclusive.

The first is defensive rotation: with the long end of the curve un-inverting and term premium reasserting itself — the subject of no shortage of ink this week — some money is treating utilities as ballast, a place to sit while rates work out what they're pricing. That's the classic playbook when yield volatility spikes: investors don't necessarily believe rates are done rising, but they'd rather hold a regulated, inelastic-demand business than a duration-heavy bond fund while the long end sorts itself out.

The second reading is simpler and less dramatic: utilities were beaten down going into this week, off nearly 2% on the month, and a bounce off oversold levels doesn't require a rotation story at all. Mean reversion happens.

The wrinkle: utilities aren't just a bond proxy anymore

What complicates both readings is that the sector's investment case has genuinely shifted. Utilities are increasingly being priced as an AI infrastructure play rather than a pure income trade — data centers are projected to drive roughly 55% of new electricity demand growth in the US, and utility capital spending tied to AI buildout is estimated near $240 billion this year, per Motley Fool reporting. Regulated utilities can also pass rising costs through to ratepayers, which blunts some of the inflation exposure that would otherwise punish the group when yields rise on inflation fears — as this week's Treasury auction results suggested they are.

That combination — regulated pricing power plus a genuine growth narrative from power-hungry data centers — gives the sector a floor that a pure bond-proxy trade wouldn't have. It also means the weekly bounce doesn't have to be read as a single-factor signal. Some of the buying may be classic risk-off positioning; some may be generalist money still chasing the AI power-demand theme regardless of what the 30-year is doing.

What would resolve it

The cleanest way to tell the two stories apart is what happens if long yields keep climbing. A genuine defensive rotation should hold up, or even extend, if the 30-year pushes further past 5.25% and equity volatility broadens. An oversold bounce, by contrast, should fade back toward the monthly trend once the short-covering or rebalancing flow that drove it is spent. The next Treasury refunding auctions and the September Fed meeting are the obvious checkpoints — both will show whether the term-premium repricing at the long end keeps investors reaching for utilities, or whether this week was just noise inside a still-negative month.
