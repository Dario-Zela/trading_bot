Commodity exporters are the quiet winners of this week's macro mix

Crude oil has surged this week amid the Strait of Hormuz crisis, a direct tailwind for oil-exporting economies. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A softer dollar plus an oil and precious-metals breakout favors commodity-exporting EM currencies — but this week's dollar firming is worth watching.

The dollar has been sliding for most of August, and two commodities have been climbing hard enough to notice: oil and precious metals. For the group of countries that sell those things to the rest of the world rather than buy them, this is about as favorable a setup as macro throws up — a weaker unit to invoice against, and a stronger case for the invoice.

Over the past 20 trading days the dollar index is down 1.26%. Over the past five days alone, the oil tracking fund USO is up 7.3%, driven by a Strait of Hormuz crisis that has choked roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne crude since Iranian forces resumed attacks on tankers in July after an earlier ceasefire broke down. Gold has added 9% over 20 days and silver 15%, helped along by a July payrolls report that showed US employment shrinking by 23,000 jobs — a shock that knocked down rate-hike odds and sent traders back into bullion.

In one sentence: A softer dollar plus an oil and precious-metals breakout is the classic setup for commodity-exporting currencies to outperform — for now.

Terms of trade, in the exporters' favor

For an economy that ships iron ore, copper, crude or gold rather than importing it, the math is straightforward: the same barrel or ounce buys more of everything else when its dollar price rises and the dollar itself is cheap relative to other currencies. Latin American commodity producers — Brazil, Colombia, Chile, Peru — have been cited by asset managers this month as unusually insulated from the broader energy shock precisely because they're net sellers, not buyers, of what's rallying. South Africa's gold and platinum-group export basket sits in the same camp. Westpac's commodity export price index rose roughly 3% in August on the back of gains across gold, crude, iron ore and copper — a rare instance of the index's energy and metals components moving in the same direction at once.

That's a genuine fiscal and current-account tailwind. Export revenues rise in dollar terms while the dollar cost of servicing external debt eases, a combination that has historically shown up in tighter EM sovereign spreads and firmer currencies within weeks, not months.

The catch in the currency chart

The dollar's weekly move — a 0.07% gain, small on its own — is the detail worth watching. It reverses none of the 20-day decline, but it arrives at a moment when the same forces pushing gold and silver higher (soft US jobs data, wavering rate-hike conviction) are ambiguous for the dollar itself: a Fed seen as less likely to tighten further can just as easily strengthen the currency as weaken it, depending on what the rest of the world is doing with its own rates. If this week's firming turns into a trend rather than a blip, the tailwind for EM borrowers reverses on the financing side even as the commodity windfall keeps building on the trade side — a split that has caught exporters out before, when strong export receipts masked a currency quietly losing ground against a resurgent dollar.

The next test is data-driven: the following US payrolls print and any signal from Iran-Oman talks on reopening Hormuz will do more to settle the dollar's direction than anything happening in commodity markets themselves.
