Thirty-year yields push past 5.25% as the curve un-inverts for good

In one sentence
The Treasury curve is steepening entirely from the long end — the 30-year at 5.265% reflects rising term premium and deficit worries, not Fed rate-cut hopes.

The last time the U.S. Treasury curve looked like this — every maturity yielding more than the one before it, in a clean, ascending line from three months out to thirty years — investors could tell a simple story: the economy is fine, the Federal Reserve is done cutting, buy risk. That story does not fit anymore.

As of August 14, the three-month bill yields 3.697%, the five-year note 4.362%, the ten-year note 4.696%, and the thirty-year bond 5.265%. The spread between the three-month bill and the ten-year note — the slice of the curve the New York Fed watches most closely for recession risk — is back out to essentially 100 basis points, the widest reading in months and about as far from inversion as the curve has been since before the 2022–2024 inversion began. But the more interesting number sits further out: the gap between the ten-year and thirty-year yields, at roughly 57 basis points, is unusually wide on its own. Nearly all of the curve's re-steepening is happening past the ten-year mark.

That distinction matters. A curve that steepens because short rates fall — the textbook "bull steepener" — tells you the Fed is cutting into a slowing economy. A curve that steepens because long rates rise while the front end stays put is a different animal entirely: it says investors want more compensation to hold long-dated government debt, full stop. This is the second kind.

A curve steepening from the top down

The front end has barely moved. The Federal Reserve, under chair Kevin Warsh since May, held its target range at 3.50%–3.75% at the July 29 meeting, with three of twelve voting members pushing for a hike rather than a hold — a rare degree of open dissent for a Fed still nominally committed to a 2% inflation target it has missed for five straight years, largely on the back of an energy-price shock that has kept headline inflation sticky. Warsh has said explicitly the Fed will "deliver the 2% inflation target," but swaps markets are now pricing meaningful odds of a hike, not a cut, at the next meeting. That is why the three-month bill and even the five-year note have stayed roughly anchored: nobody is pricing imminent easing.

The thirty-year, by contrast, has done almost all the moving. It cleared 5% earlier this summer and an August 13 auction of $25 billion in fresh thirty-year paper priced at a high yield of 5.216% — the richest the Treasury has had to pay for three-decade money in a quarter century. The sale itself was not a disaster: the tail, the gap between the yield dealers expected and what the auction actually cleared at, came in at just 0.4 basis points versus a historical average closer to flat. But the bid-to-cover ratio, at 2.39 times, ran below its recent average, and dealers were left holding more of the issue than usual after softer-than-typical demand from both domestic and international buyers.

The bill, not the growth outlook

That combination — a clearing auction with weak internal metrics — is the clearest evidence that what is driving the long end is a term-premium story, not a growth story. "We're not really at a level where people seem to be going crazy, saying 'I want to buy the 30-year,' and that should be a warning," John Fath of BTG Pactual Asset Management said after the sale. Michal Stanczyk of Allspring Global Investments made a similar point: a successful auction "shouldn't be confused with strong structural demand for long-duration assets."

The fiscal backdrop explains why. The federal deficit has run to roughly $1.17 trillion through the first ten months of this fiscal year, up about 15% from the same period a year earlier, as refinancing costs on a debt stock that has effectively doubled since 2018 — now near $31 trillion outstanding — collide with higher rates on everything the Treasury issues. Interest costs on the debt have climbed to roughly $857 billion over nine months, more than the government now spends on Medicare or the military. None of that is news to bond investors, but it is increasingly showing up in the price they demand for locking up money for thirty years. The New York Fed's own term-premium model (ACM) has the ten-year component back around 0.8% as of mid-August, having spent much of 2016 through 2023 near or below zero. Fath's diagnosis was blunt: "The only clear solution I see is the US government tightening its budget."

Two curves, one signal

Put together, the front end and the long end are telling two different stories that happen to share a graph. The three-month-to-five-year segment reflects a Fed that is not cutting soon and might, if inflation proves stubborn, even tighten again. The ten-to-thirty-year segment reflects investors pricing a structurally higher cost of capital for a government that keeps issuing more debt than the market wants to absorb without a concession. Both are real; neither is a recession signal in the conventional sense, since an un-inverted curve driven by rising long yields does not carry the same growth-slowdown implication as one driven by falling short yields.

The next test comes quickly. The Treasury's quarterly refunding announcement due in early November will show whether issuance plans lean further into long-dated debt or shift back toward bills, and the September 17–18 FOMC meeting will settle whether Warsh's hold survives the internal dissent that showed up in July. Until then, the thirty-year bond is trading less on what the Fed does next and more on how much the market is willing to charge Washington to keep the lights on.
