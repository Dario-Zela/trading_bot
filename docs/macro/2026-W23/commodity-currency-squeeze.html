Commodity FX in the crossfire: metals down, dollar up, terms-of-trade shock accumulating

The Paraburdoo iron ore mine in Western Australia's Pilbara region — a benchmark for AUD's commodity anchoring — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
AUD, BRL and ZAR face a double hit as dollar strength compounds an independent commodity selloff, shrinking export revenues on two fronts simultaneously.

The currencies of the world's commodity exporters face a particularly uncomfortable week. For once, the commodity complex and the US dollar are moving in the same direction — and that direction is wrong for resource-linked economies. The dollar is climbing while the prices of what those economies sell are falling, compounding losses in local-currency terms through two simultaneous channels.

The DXY index gained 1.17% over the past five trading sessions, a move that mechanically reduces the dollar price of every commodity quoted in the currency. But this week's damage runs deeper than exchange-rate arithmetic. Agriculture is down 3.12% on a five-day basis and 5.04% over twenty days — a persistent, broad-based retreat, not noise. Base metals shed 1.62% over the same window. Silver is the worst single print in the complex: off 9.89% in five sessions, a move that sits alongside gold's double-digit losses and marks a broad precious-metals breakdown rather than a silver-specific story. Together, the commodity indices are falling on their own terms, independent of the dollar move.

The three currencies sitting most directly in the crossfire are the Australian dollar, the Brazilian real and the South African rand. For the AUD, the trouble arrives through iron ore, copper and lithium — which retreated simultaneously on Friday, stripping the ASX materials sector of its internal offsets. The AUD/USD slid to around 0.71, its weakest level since April. For the BRL, the pressure comes through soybeans and iron ore, commodities whose multi-year structural softness has already been compressing Brazilian trade surpluses. The USD/BRL rose to roughly 5.17 on June 5, up more than 2% on the session.

The rand's double exposure

The ZAR sits in a particularly exposed position. Platinum — South Africa's largest export category by value — gold and other platinum-group metals are all falling together, which means the country's terms of trade are deteriorating on their highest-value export lines at the same moment the dollar is making imports more expensive in rand terms. The USD/ZAR eased to around 16.3. The South African Reserve Bank added a 25-basis-point hike to 7% at its late-May meeting — the first rate increase since May 2023 — in recognition of the imported inflation and currency fragility already accumulating. That provides some carry support, but rate differentials are a slow-moving buffer against a fast-moving commodity price rout.

If the terms-of-trade deterioration extends beyond a second week, the effects move from currency screens into current-account arithmetic. Central banks in South Africa, Brazil and Australia have reserves deep enough to smooth short-term drawdowns, but sustained compression of export revenues changes the trajectory of reserve accumulation — and eventually the credibility of passive currency management.

Oil is the narrow exception that matters. USO posted a 3.04% gain over the same five-session window, driven by renewed US-Iran tensions and supply uncertainty around the Strait of Hormuz. That carve-out insulates Gulf exporters and a handful of EM oil producers — Nigeria, Colombia among them — from the worst of the week's commodity squeeze. But it is a narrow corridor. Commodity currencies outside the energy complex have no equivalent shelter.

The 20-day agricultural trend is the clearest diagnostic to watch. At -5%, it already exceeds most models' threshold for a durable terms-of-trade shift. A third consecutive week of joint dollar strength and commodity weakness would likely move the conversation at more than one of the three central banks now under pressure from passive reserve drawdown.
