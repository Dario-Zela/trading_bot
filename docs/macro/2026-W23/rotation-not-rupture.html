Rotation, not rupture: reading a week where everything sold off except credit

The floor of the New York Stock Exchange — where the week's trading told a more nuanced story than the headlines suggested. — Ken Lund / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Dollar, tech, and precious metals all moved against the growth-revival consensus this week, but the absence of spread widening argues for repositioning over regime change.

The week ending June 7 looked, at first pass, like a textbook risk-off episode. The dollar gained 1.17% to reclaim the symbolic 100 level on the DXY. Technology stocks shed 5.61% in five sessions, nearly erasing a month of gains. Gold fell 5%, silver close to 10%. Commodity currencies wobbled. Any visitor scanning those headlines would be forgiven for reaching for recession hedges.

But one asset class failed to cooperate with that narrative: credit.

The map of the selloff

The catalyst arrived in two parts, almost simultaneously. Broadcom reported fiscal Q2 results on June 3 that beat on adjusted earnings per share but delivered Q3 AI semiconductor revenue guidance of roughly $16 billion — a billion short of the $17.2 billion consensus. CEO Hock Tan's suggestion that Google might diversify its custom-chip relationships added another wrinkle to an accelerator thesis priced for perfection. NVIDIA fell 6.2%, Broadcom 7.9%, and the semiconductor index lost more than 10% in a single session — roughly $1 trillion in combined market capitalization gone in an afternoon.

The second blow landed the next morning: the May jobs report showed 172,000 new positions added, more than double the 85,000 consensus estimate. A labor market that won't slow is a labor market that doesn't need rate cuts. Markets rapidly repriced the Fed path, pushing the 2-year yield toward 16-month highs and the 10-year back to 4.54%.

Together, the two events collapsed the "growth revival with rate relief" trade that had been building through late spring. Technology rode that consensus hardest — XLK had gained 6.25% over the preceding 20 days — and paid the steepest price when it unwound. Precious metals, levered to a falling-dollar, falling-rate worldview, were hit even harder on a percentage basis: gold's 5-day loss of 5%, silver's nearly 10% decline, represent the most direct expression of a real-rates repricing. The DXY's return to 100 was less a currency event than a clearing trade — every position built against the dollar needed to be unwound, and it was.

The dog that didn't bark

Here is where the week gets analytically interesting. HYG and LQD — the dominant high-yield and investment-grade bond ETFs — each fell roughly 1.1% on the week. That is a meaningful decline for fixed income. But the HY-IG return differential was effectively zero: just -0.0076%.

That number is the week's most important data point.

In genuine regime breaks — real recessions, credit crunches, financial-system stress events — high-yield underperforms investment grade by hundreds of basis points, sometimes within days. The mechanism is straightforward: when investors price rising default probabilities, they flee lower-quality debt first, spreads widen sharply, and HYG craters while LQD holds or even rallies on a flight-to-quality bid. None of that happened. High-yield and investment-grade moved in near-perfect lockstep, which means what happened in credit markets this week was duration selling — not credit selling. The market repriced interest-rate risk. It did not reprice default risk.

That distinction matters enormously for how to read everything else. If credit had broken, the risk-off signals in tech and precious metals would have gained a more alarming character. Instead, the bond market is doing what it should do when a blowout jobs report reprices the rate path: it adjusts duration mechanically. Underlying fundamentals remain broadly supportive — trailing high-yield default rates running below 3%, investment-grade OAS near historically tight territory around 80 basis points. The credit market is not forecasting corporate distress. It is forecasting higher rates for longer. That is uncomfortable for leveraged portfolios; it is not the leading edge of something systemic.

An orderly unwind, not a regime break

The other evidence against a genuine regime change lies in what held. Defensives didn't crack. Energy and Health Care have led for a second consecutive month — a performance pattern that fits neither a pure "risk-on" nor a "crisis" template. It fits something more specific: a rotation away from rate-sensitive and consensus-crowded positions toward sectors whose earnings don't require rate cuts to materialize or AI multiples to hold firm.

The yield curve reinforces this reading. A positively sloped curve — with the 10-year yielding materially more than the 2-year — has historically signaled that markets expect real economic activity to continue, not contract. An inverted curve is the recession flag; that flag is not flying. At current term structure levels, with the long end anchored above 4.5%, the message is approximately: rates stay higher for longer, but the expansion survives them.

The dollar's return to 100 completes the picture. A DXY rally built on a jobs beat is not the same animal as a DXY rally built on capital flight. This was rate-differential buying — the mechanical consequence of US yields repricing upward faster than peers. No one is treating the dollar as a crisis currency this week. They are buying it because US fixed income suddenly yields more.

Read together: gold down, silver down, dollar up, rates up, defensives steady, credit spreads unchanged. That is the fingerprint of a crowded consensus being unwound in an orderly fashion — not of a new bearish regime taking hold.

The question heading into next week

The unresolved issue is technology. XLK enters the coming week having shed nearly its entire monthly gain in five sessions. Its 20-day return stood at +6.25% before this selloff; that anchor is now threadbare.

One bad week in a rising-rate environment, following a concentrated positioning unwind triggered by a single guidance miss, is normal market mechanics. Two bad weeks would start to look like distribution — institutional selling into any recovery rather than tactical repositioning. The key watchpoints are: whether AI-related names stabilize after Broadcom's guidance reset established a lower baseline for Q3 expectations; whether the 10-year yield holds above 4.5% as the jobs-report shock fades, or retreats enough to relieve rate pressure on growth multiples; and whether the next round of corporate earnings — arriving in earnest in mid-July — confirms or undercuts the margin story for mega-cap technology.

Defensives holding and credit spreads staying tight are, on balance, reassuring signals that the week's damage was positional rather than fundamental. The growth-revival consensus was crowded; it is now being cleared. But tech's resilience — or fragility — over the next five sessions will determine whether this week reads, in hindsight, as an orderly clearing event, or as the opening chapter of something that requires a different playbook.
