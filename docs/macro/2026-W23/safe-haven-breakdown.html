Gold -5%, silver -10%: when the safe haven breaks, what is actually hedging the book?

Standard 400-oz 'four nines' gold bars, the benchmark form held by central banks and bullion vaults — Andrzej Barabasz / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Gold -8% and silver -14% over 20 days: a blowout jobs report sent DXY above 100 and exposed the gap between safe-haven mythology and portfolio reality.

The two assets that most portfolios reach for during turbulence both declined sharply last week — and not in a mean-reverting, single-session way. GLD, the gold ETF, is down 5.0% over five sessions and 8.2% over twenty. SLV, the silver ETF, shed 9.9% for the week and 14.0% over the same twenty-day window. Technology — which portfolios are supposed to hedge against — fell 5.6% in the same five sessions. The hedge did not hedge.

Understanding why is straightforward. What to do about it is harder.

The jobs print that closed the door

On Friday, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported 172,000 US jobs added in May — more than double the 85,000 consensus forecast. The dollar index crossed 100 for the first time since earlier in the year, and gold fell 3% on the day to $4,336 per troy ounce, its lowest London auction price since January. The ten-year Treasury yield climbed six basis points to 4.53%. Money markets moved to assign 67% probability to a Federal Reserve rate hike by December.

Gold's sensitivity to this outcome is well understood: no yield, so higher real rates raise the opportunity cost; a stronger dollar makes it costlier for non-dollar buyers. What is harder to explain is why the broader twenty-day decline is so large. The thirty-day gold-DXY correlation had already weakened to -0.25 by early June against a historical baseline of -0.45 — the mechanical dollar transmission was diminished. Dollar strength alone does not fully account for an 8% drawdown over twenty days.

The more complete explanation includes positioning. March 2026 saw North American gold ETFs record $13 billion in net outflows — the largest monthly redemption on record, driven partly by CME margin hikes and leveraged fund liquidations. When commodity positions start unwinding at scale, gold's deep liquidity makes it the first seller. A second, smaller version of that dynamic appears to have run through the book this week.

Silver's double exposure

Silver's decline is nearly double gold's on both time frames, and the spread is diagnostic. Silver carries an exposure that gold does not: it is simultaneously a monetary metal and an industrial input used in solar manufacturing, electronics, and electrical applications. In an environment where dollar strength signals higher-for-longer rates and compressed growth expectations, silver is hit from both directions — safe-haven demand retreats and industrial demand expectations pull back together.

The Silver Institute's 2026 World Silver Survey projected a sixth consecutive annual supply deficit of 46.3 million ounces. That arithmetic provides no short-term price support when macro positioning dominates. Structural supply tightness matters at quarterly or annual horizons; positioning unwinds happen in sessions. The gold-silver ratio currently sits near 62-to-1 — not dramatically wider than recent levels — which suggests the week's spread between the two metals reflects shared macro pressure rather than a divergence in their longer-term fundamentals.

What this week exposed

The harder question is portfolio-level. In a week where technology fell 5.6% and gold fell 5%, the alignment of losses is the problem — both assets declined for the same underlying reason, a real-rate repricing event. That is precisely the scenario in which precious metals are supposed to provide offset, and precisely the scenario in which they did not.

What held up was short-duration investment-grade credit, where spreads were stable because the week's move was driven by rate duration rather than default fear. Energy and Healthcare — which have led sector returns for two consecutive months — again outperformed, generating returns whose risk profile is largely insensitive to the dollar-rate dynamic. These are not hedges in the traditional sense; they are cash-flow-generating positions that happened not to be in the path of this week's repricing.

The structural question this selloff forces into the open: gold's decade-long bull thesis was built substantially on negative real rates. Rates were deeply negative through 2020-2022, the precise condition that powered gold's prior multi-year rally. From its January 2026 peak near $5,600, gold has now retraced roughly 22%. If the ten-year drifts toward 4.75% and the Fed does hike by December, the real-rate regime that justified heavy precious metals allocations has materially changed — and a position sized for a hedge that no longer functions as one is simply exposure without the intended offset.

The near-term diagnostic is June CPI, due mid-month. If inflation continues above the Fed's 2% target, December rate-hike probability moves higher and the conditions generating this selloff persist. The gold-silver ratio is worth tracking separately: if it widens sharply from the current 62-to-1, that signals markets pricing not just monetary tightening but industrial demand deterioration — a growth-fear layer that would make the metals' decline harder, and slower, to reverse.

For the week ending June 7, the data point is unavoidable. Gold and silver collapsed in the same sessions that tech fell hard, and the three instruments most commonly cited as portfolio hedges were, for five days at least, the same trade.
