China demand skepticism is back: read the base metals and agricultural data

The Guixi Smelter of Jiangxi Copper Corporation in Jiangxi province — the world's largest single copper smelting facility and a barometer of Chinese industrial metals throughput. — Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA 4.0
In one sentence
Base metals reversed a 20-day uptrend, agriculture extended its slide, and silver's near-double drop versus gold flags industrial demand as part of the story.

Two commodity ETFs gave the same reading this week, from different directions. The Invesco DB Base Metals Fund (DBB) fell 1.62% over five sessions, snapping a positive 20-day trend that had accumulated +1.87%. The Invesco DB Agriculture Fund (DBA) extended a decline that is already four weeks old: -3.12% for the week, -5.04% over twenty days. Short-term moves in commodity futures ETFs are often reversible; a persistent drop in agriculture combined with a reversal in base metals is a more deliberate pattern. The natural first question is whether China is slowing down, because China is the marginal buyer that determines the direction of both markets.

The macro backdrop supports the question. China's official National Bureau of Statistics manufacturing PMI for May came in at exactly 50.0 — the technical boundary between expansion and contraction — down 0.3 points from April. The new orders sub-index slipped to 49.9, crossing into contraction territory. That is not a collapse reading, but it is not the demand engine the base metals complex needs to hold a rally. A private survey — the RatingDog PMI — came in at a more reassuring 51.8, above forecasts, suggesting activity is not uniformly weak; but even there, employment contracted marginally and new export business declined.

The silver read

The most textured signal of the week comes not from DBB but from the spread between silver and gold. The iShares Silver Trust (SLV) fell 9.89% over five days. The SPDR Gold Shares ETF (GLD) fell 5.0% over the same period. Both declines contain a monetary element — risk-off repositioning partly triggered by a stronger-than-forecast US jobs print — but the magnitude gap between them is too large to be explained by monetary sentiment alone.

Silver is a split-personality asset. Roughly 60% of annual silver demand is industrial: solar panels, electric vehicles, semiconductors, AI data-center infrastructure. Gold's industrial demand sits close to 5%. When silver drops nearly twice as sharply as gold in the same week, the spread reflects not just haven demand being priced down but the industrial component being discounted separately. The gold-silver ratio functions as a real-time proxy for industrial demand sentiment precisely because the two metals share monetary repricing while diverging on the cycle. Right now, that proxy is pointing toward softness.

Oil complicates the picture only superficially. The United States Oil Fund (USO) rose 3.04% over five days, but the context is supply-side: crude's 2026 rally has been driven by a Strait of Hormuz disruption that cut OPEC+ output by roughly 8 million barrels per day in Q2. A supply-constrained oil price rising alongside softer industrial metals is not a contradiction — it is a disaggregation. The two signals are answering different questions.

The thesis that remains open is whether DBB's reversal and DBA's persistent decline reflect a genuine Chinese demand slowdown or an inventory normalization after earlier front-running of tariff and supply-chain concerns. Global visible copper inventory has already risen by roughly 540,000 metric tonnes since January to nearly 1.5 million tonnes, which is consistent with either scenario. China's May trade data, expected within the next two weeks, will be the cleaner test: actual import volumes will say whether the PMI softness in new orders has translated into physical buying hesitation.
