Ninety-one basis points of normality: what a positive curve means at 4.5%

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C. — the institution whose rate path defines where the curve settles. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The yield curve has its most positive slope in two years, but at 4.54% on the 10-year and 5% on the long bond, 'normal' is far from cheap.

For most of 2023 and into 2024, the most-watched diagnostic in fixed income showed the same thing: short-term rates above long-term rates, a configuration that sent a persistent, unresolved signal about where the economy was headed. That configuration is now gone. As of June 5, the 3-month Treasury bill yields 3.625%, the 5-year note 4.28%, the benchmark 10-year 4.54%, and the 30-year bond 4.999%. The 3M-10Y spread stands at +91.1 basis points — up from +86.5bp a week ago — and for the first time in the better part of two years, the full term structure slopes cleanly upward from bill to bond.

The longest inversion in U.S. history is over. What that means for capital markets is more complicated than the headline suggests.

In one sentence: The 3M-10Y spread is the most normal it's looked in two years — but at 4.54% on the 10-year and 5.00% on the 30-year, normality is not cheap.

The NIM dividend

The most direct beneficiaries of a positively sloped curve are traditional lenders. Banks fund themselves at the short end — deposits, overnight borrowing, commercial paper — and deploy capital at longer tenors through mortgages, corporate loans, and bond portfolios. When the curve inverts, that model is under mechanical strain: the cost of funding approaches or exceeds the yield on assets. When the curve reverts to positive, the spread opens up and net interest margin expands.

That repricing is now underway. Industry-wide NIM for FDIC-insured institutions reached 3.39% in Q4 2025 — the highest reading since 2019 — after spending much of 2023-24 compressed by the very inversion that has since resolved. Banks reporting in 2026 have continued the trend: one mid-sized regional reported NIM expanding 18 basis points quarter-over-quarter and noted that its margin had risen 106 basis points from its Q1 2024 trough.

Equity markets are pricing that structural tailwind. XLF — the financial select sector ETF — gained 1.40% in the five sessions ending June 6, and its 20-day return of +1.45% suggests the move is not simply week-end noise. In a week when broad equities were under pressure and duration anxiety was resurfacing, financials held their ground. That consistency across time windows says something about the structural trend rather than a tactical rotation trade.

For fixed-income carry players, the restoration of positive slope also improves the economics of holding intermediate maturities. Rolling long positions in 2-year or 5-year paper no longer carries the negative carry that defined the inversion period. The carry-to-volatility ratio at intermediate tenors is recovering — which supports positioning in that part of the curve.

Term premium, not growth

Here the narrative requires precision. A positive curve at 4.54% on the 10-year is a different animal from a positive curve at 2.5%. The absolute level of financing costs has not eased. What has changed is the shape of the pricing — and the shape deserves interpretation before it gets read as a green light for growth.

The 30-year bond at 4.999% is not primarily a signal about long-run economic expectations. It reflects a substantial term premium — the extra compensation investors demand for holding duration rather than rolling short-term paper. The New York Fed's ACM model, which decomposes the 10-year yield into expected short rates and term premium, shows a term premium that has moved decisively out of the negative territory it occupied for most of the 2010s. The drivers are well-documented: persistent inflation uncertainty, an elevated and compounding federal deficit driving Treasury supply, and the withdrawal of the demand-smoothing once provided by quantitative easing.

In practical terms, this means the market is not rewarding optimism about growth when it prices the long bond at 5%. It is demanding compensation for duration risk — the risk that inflation does not fully subside, that supply keeps pressing, that the Fed has less capacity to intervene in bond markets than in the prior decade. A positive curve in that environment signals stable short-rate expectations, not accommodative financing conditions. For corporate borrowers approaching refinancing walls in 2026 and 2027, the distinction matters considerably.

The real estate riddle

Which makes the week's most conspicuous data point worth unpacking. XLRE — the real estate select sector ETF — gained 1.61% in the five sessions ending June 6. In a week when the 10-year sat at 4.54% and the 30-year touched 5%, equity real estate outperformed broad markets, moving in apparent defiance of its own rate sensitivity.

Two explanations compete. The first: the market may be pricing a rate cut that is not currently on the consensus schedule. Rate-sensitive sectors respond to rate expectations more than to current rate levels. If residual futures positioning still assigns meaningful probability to the Fed resuming easing aggressively in the back half of 2026 — as it did before the stronger-than-expected jobs print reinforced hold expectations — REIT valuations can lift even as spot yields hold elevated. The second explanation is more mechanical: short covering. XLRE entered the week as a heavily shorted sector given the rate backdrop. An unexpected catalyst above key technical levels forces short-side exits, amplifying price moves without any change in underlying fundamentals.

Neither explanation is reassuring. If it is rate-cut pricing, XLRE is ahead of a cut that the Fed has not signaled. If it is short covering, the move carries no durable signal at all. Names with material refinancing walls in the next eighteen months face a 5% long bond regardless of what the ETF did in five sessions. The week's real estate outperformance demands scrutiny rather than extrapolation.

What resolves it

The next material inputs arrive in sequence. June CPI, due mid-month, will either reinforce or undercut the Fed's hold posture — a soft print gives rate-cut pricing in XLRE some empirical support; a hot one removes it. The June FOMC meeting follows, with updated dot-plot guidance that may clarify how many cuts, if any, the committee sees for 2026. And Q2 bank earnings in mid-July will provide the first clean read on whether NIM expansion is compounding into per-share earnings growth or being absorbed by provisions and credit costs.

The 3M-10Y spread at +91bp is the most normal-looking number on the rates sheet in two years. The task now is to avoid mistaking normality of shape for normality of conditions. At 4.54% on the benchmark and 5.00% on the long bond, the curve has healed — but the level at which it has healed leaves financing expensive, duration risky, and any sector pricing relief ahead of the data on uncertain ground.
