XLE's 10% 20-day drawdown: capitulation or value trap?

An offshore drilling platform in the North Sea — the global energy sector is pricing a supply reversal as the Strait of Hormuz reopens after a 110-day closure. — Erik Christensen / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
XLE's 10% 20-day drop is a war-trade unwind, not a demand panic — but with Hormuz clearing and OPEC+ adding barrels, the floor is not yet in.

Energy was the best-performing sector in the S&P 500 through the first quarter of 2026. By the third week of June, it had become the worst — by an uncomfortable distance. XLE, the benchmark energy ETF, is down 5.86% over five days and 10.08% over twenty, a drawdown that leaves the next-worst sector, XLC, more than four percentage points behind. USO, which tracks crude oil futures, has shed 20.38% over the same twenty-day window, closing Friday at $114.87. The numbers are dramatic enough to invite a capitulation call. The history of such calls in energy argues for patience.

Energy was the trade of the year — until it wasn't. XLE gave back a third of its 2026 gains in a fortnight once the Hormuz deal was signed.

A war trade unwinding

The story of XLE in 2026 is, in almost every respect, the story of the Strait of Hormuz. When the United States and Israel struck Iran on February 28, the waterway through which roughly 20% of the world's oil and LNG had passed effectively closed. The supply shock was acute — at its peak, analysts estimated more than 11 million barrels per day had been pulled from global markets, and WTI crude surged past $100 per barrel. XLE rode that supply shock to a 32% year-to-date gain by early Q2, a run that made it the standout performer against an S&P 500 that had mustered barely a quarter of that return.

On June 14, a ceasefire framework began to take shape. By June 17, the deal was signed; by June 18, the Strait reopened under a 60-day interim arrangement that lifted the U.S. naval blockade and committed both parties to nuclear talks. Crude dropped more than 4% on the news, and then kept falling as markets worked through the implications. The selloff in XLE is, at its core, the market returning what the war gave.

The supply picture is not simple, though. The deep-water channel through the Strait still has an estimated 80 mines requiring clearance — a process analysts peg at 40 to 50 days minimum. A backlog of stranded tanker traffic means shipping schedules will take weeks, perhaps months, to re-synchronise. The EIA projects full inventory normalisation may not arrive until late 2026 or early 2027. OPEC+ has added complexity of its own: in May, seven member countries agreed to lift output by 188,000 barrels per day for June — Saudi Arabia and Russia each absorbing 62,000 bpd of that increase — before the peace deal had fully materialised. The question for oil prices is not whether supply is coming back. It is how fast, and at what rate OPEC+ will continue to add barrels into a recovering market.

The demand picture complicates things further. China's crude imports fell to 7.8 million barrels per day in May — the lowest reading since October 2017 and nearly four million barrels per day below the 2025 average of 11.6 mb/d. Part of that collapse is attributable to the war itself: Middle Eastern supply routes to Chinese refiners were severed, and state-owned processors cut run rates to record lows in response. But structural forces are also at work. China's accelerating transition to electric and hybrid vehicles has depressed gasoline demand, and GL Consulting forecasts a 5% drop in refining activity for the year. Whether Chinese import volumes recover as Hormuz normalises — or whether the structural drag dominates — will matter enormously for where oil settles.

The gas exception

Natural gas has not followed crude lower. UNG, which tracks Henry Hub futures, is up 5.20% over five days and 2.18% over twenty — a divergence within the energy complex that cuts both ways analytically.

The optimistic read: if this were a broad macro demand collapse, natural gas would not be holding up. Gas is rising because AI-driven electricity demand is pulling hard on power-sector consumption, particularly in the United States, where data-centre load growth is running well ahead of forecasts. That would place crude's weakness in the supply column — a finite, reversible dynamic rather than a signal of collapsing global activity.

The pessimistic read: gas and oil are not symmetric markets. Henry Hub prices are driven by domestic U.S. supply-demand balances and LNG export volumes, and those markets never faced a Hormuz-style supply shock in the first place. The gas rally may be telling a gas-specific story about power demand while oil tells an oil-specific story about supply returning. Both can be true simultaneously. The divergence does not invalidate the demand-slowdown thesis for crude; it just narrows it to crude specifically.

For XLE, the composition matters. The ETF is overwhelmingly weighted toward integrated majors and upstream producers — ExxonMobil, Chevron, ConocoPhillips — whose earnings are far more sensitive to oil than to gas. A gas rally that is not accompanied by an oil recovery offers limited direct relief.

Capitulation, or another leg lower?

The statistical case for a floor is not nothing. XLE's RSI has entered oversold territory. The 20-day drawdown of more than 10% makes it the only sector in that category by a wide margin, a dispersion that historically has preceded at least tactical recoveries. And at $76 to $77 per barrel — where WTI is trading as of this week — integrated majors remain profitable. Most large-cap energy producers break even somewhere between $50 and $65 per barrel; the current price does not threaten their balance sheets.

But the capitulation framing has a specific problem in this instance. XLE was not cheap before the drawdown — it was expensive on the back of a supply shock. If WTI settles toward $65 to $70 as Hormuz normalises and OPEC+ continues adding barrels, earnings estimates for XLE constituents will need to compress from their war-era levels. J.P. Morgan's base case for 2026 Brent is approximately $60 per barrel on an annual average, implying a meaningful second-half drag if realised. Mean-reversion arguments based on XLE's gap to the broader market are only persuasive if the underlying commodity has found its equilibrium — and the supply picture is still being written.

The cross-market signals are worth noting. High-yield credit has outperformed investment-grade on the week, but energy names buried within the HY index represent a growing exposure; if oil drifts further, the credit picture in that pocket could deteriorate. The dollar's quiet strength this month has not helped commodity prices denominated in dollars. And the 30-year Treasury at 4.90% is telling a story about term premium and fiscal risk rather than energy demand — but it does constrain the valuation multiples at which risk assets, including beaten-down sector ETFs, can find buyers.

The natural supply catalyst that would argue for turning constructive on XLE is a clear signal that the Hormuz normalisation is proceeding faster than priced — tanker data showing resumed flows, inventory draws in the Gulf, or a pullback in OPEC+ output increases as prices soften. Absent that, the 10% drawdown reflects a rational repricing of what was essentially a geopolitical premium. The premium is leaving the price. The question is whether it is leaving too fast.

The next EIA weekly petroleum status report will offer the first meaningful data on whether U.S. inventories are building in response to the anticipated supply normalisation. Chinese customs data for June crude imports, due in mid-July, will be the other number to watch. Until one of those prints provides a cleaner read on where oil supply and demand are actually settling, the energy sector is trading on narrative — and the narrative just changed.
