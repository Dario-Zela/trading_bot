Hardware leads, content lags: the XLK-XLC split defines the week's equity narrative

Server racks in a commercial data centre — the capex-cycle infrastructure that XLK's semiconductor and cloud holdings are built to supply. — Carl Lender / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
XLK's 14-point lead over XLC in 20 days is the market's verdict: AI infrastructure rewarded, attention-economy platforms repriced lower.

The clearest signal from this week's equity tape is not the level of the index — it is the gap inside it. Technology hardware and software (XLK) added 4.49% in five sessions and is up 8.07% over twenty days. Communication services (XLC) lost 2.38% on the week and has shed 5.73% over the same window. That is roughly a 14-percentage-point spread between two sectors that occupy adjacent tiles on any sector-rotation chart, often lumped together under the loose label of "tech." The market is not making that mistake this week.

The picks-and-shovels trade, legible again

XLK's top three holdings — NVIDIA (15.5% of the fund), Apple (13.6%), and Microsoft (10.1%) — describe an index weighted toward the physical and software infrastructure of artificial intelligence rather than toward the applications that sit on top of it. Semiconductors and semiconductor equipment account for 42% of the fund's exposure; software for 27%; hardware storage for 17%. The composition is, in effect, a leveraged bet on the capex cycle of the people who are building AI rather than the people who are deploying it.

The fundamental backdrop supports the move. The four largest hyperscalers — Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet, and Meta — are projected to spend between $630 billion and $725 billion on AI infrastructure in 2026, a figure that has been revised upward multiple times since January. NVIDIA, the most direct beneficiary, reported $81.6 billion in revenue for its fiscal first quarter of 2027, an 85% gain year-over-year, and guided to $91 billion for the current period. That is not a cyclical number; it is a multi-year contract being priced into a public equity.

The market phrase "picks and shovels" — selling to every miner regardless of who strikes gold — has been a fixture of AI commentary for two years. What this week's price action adds is granularity: not all picks are the same. Cloud infrastructure, enterprise software licences, and the chips that run both are being rewarded. Distribution networks, attention platforms, and content libraries are not.

What is pressing on communication services

XLC's composition is the obverse of XLK's. The fund is heavily concentrated in two names — Meta and Alphabet — whose revenues are tied primarily to digital advertising. That structure means XLC tracks consumer sentiment and corporate ad budgets almost as faithfully as it tracks any technology trend.

The backdrop here is less comfortable. University of Michigan consumer sentiment hit 53.3 in March — well below the threshold that advertising planners use as a recessionary signal — and while subsequent readings recovered modestly, brands have been cautious about committing to upside. Meta posted strong headline numbers for Q1 2026, $56.3 billion in revenue on 33% year-on-year growth, but the market is also discounting a set of regulatory overhangs that have no equivalent on the hardware side of the ledger.

For Alphabet, the DOJ's September 2025 antitrust remedy — banning exclusive distribution contracts and mandating limited search-data sharing — introduced a structural uncertainty that has not fully cleared, even after the court rejected structural breakup. For Meta, the FTC's monopolization case was dismissed in late 2025, but the commission's subsequent appeal means the question remains open. Neither case directly impairs near-term cash flows. Both create a discount the XLK side of the market does not carry.

Streaming and content are a smaller part of XLC but reinforce the drag. The rationalisation cycle — studios trimming library spending, platform price increases moderating subscriber growth — has not reversed. The broader dynamic points the same direction: businesses that depend on consumer discretionary attention are being repriced relative to businesses that supply the infrastructure on which that attention runs.

XLI as corroboration

The argument would be less compelling in isolation. What makes the XLK/XLC divergence a thesis rather than a coincidence is the behaviour of XLI, the Industrials Select Sector SPDR, which gained 3.29% this week and 5.96% over twenty days.

XLI holds the physical-world layer of the AI buildout — GE Aerospace, Honeywell, Eaton, Caterpillar, and the grid-infrastructure suppliers confronting the data-centre power problem. Data centres could triple US power demand by 2030; the electrification, cooling, and logistics work required to support that growth is landing in XLI order books now. The fund attracted nearly $2 billion in net inflows this year and is up roughly 12% year-to-date.

The joint performance of XLK and XLI carries meaning that neither fund's return would carry alone. It says the market is rewarding capital-expenditure beneficiaries at every layer of the stack — silicon, software, and infrastructure steel — while repricing businesses dependent on discretionary consumer attention and ad-cycle timing. That is a precise bet, not a broad risk-on call. The S&P 500's headline gain obscures it; the sector breakdown reveals it.

What to watch

The next inflection points are specific. NVIDIA's fiscal second-quarter results, due in August, will either confirm or complicate the revenue cadence that justifies XLK's 20-day gain. The hyperscalers' mid-year capex updates — embedded in Q2 earnings calls from Amazon, Microsoft, and Alphabet in late July — will determine whether buildout estimates are revised upward again or begin to moderate. Any reduction in forward capex guidance would hit XLK directly and remove the principal argument for XLI's elevation.

On the XLC side, June advertising-market data and any development from Meta on its EU regulatory posture are the near-term reads. The FTC appeal is unlikely to surface meaningful information before autumn, but the gap between XLC's fundamental growth rate — still solid, at Meta's 33% revenue expansion — and its multiple compression suggests the market is pricing something beyond the earnings line. If the antitrust calendar produces clarity rather than escalation, that discount could narrow. For now, the spread holds, and the split between hardware and attention is the defining sector story of the month.
