CREDIT · HY VS IG · By T. Larch · 2026-06-21 · 5 min read

HY beats IG on the week, but energy names are the buried exposure

An offshore oil platform in the North Sea — energy names constitute nearly 12% of major high-yield indices, making them the sector most exposed to the current oil-price reversal. — Erik Christensen / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
HY edged IG by +0.097pp this week, but energy's 11.68% share of HYG means a 20% crude-price drop will reach credit balance sheets before autumn borrowing-base reviews arrive.

HY edged investment-grade by +0.097 percentage points this week — textbook risk-on. Below the index calm, energy's 11.68% slice of HYG is sitting on a 20% oil-price drawdown that credit markets have not yet priced.

HYG returned +0.088% on the week. LQD returned -0.009%. The differential — +0.097 percentage points in favour of high yield — is the kind of number that signals mild risk appetite, not stress. Spreads are orderly. Flows are not alarming. The index is doing what a healthy credit market does in a week with no major data surprises.

This is not a normal week for oil.

USO, the front-month crude futures ETF, is down 20.38% over the past 20 trading days. XLE, the equity proxy for the energy sector, has shed 10.08% over the same window. Behind those numbers is a near-complete reversal of the geopolitical premium that drove Brent crude above $110 a barrel during the height of the Strait of Hormuz crisis earlier this year. With peace negotiations between the US and Iran concluding and the Strait reopening to traffic, that premium has unwound sharply. Brent was trading at $111.91 a month ago. It touched $81.55 on June 16.

The credit index has not moved accordingly. That is the interesting part.

The 11 percent problem

Energy accounts for 11.68% of the iShares iBoxx $ High Yield Corporate Bond ETF — the fourth-largest sector weighting in the fund, sitting behind consumer cyclicals, communications, and consumer non-cyclicals. At first glance that exposure is manageable. At second glance, it is the segment most directly exposed to a 30-point swing in the price of crude oil.

The equity market has already begun to price the deterioration. XLE's 10% drawdown over 20 days is a reasonable first-pass estimate of what the market thinks the oil reversal costs the sector's equity cushion. Investment-grade energy names — Exxon, Chevron, ConocoPhillips — have balance sheets and diversified revenue bases that cushion the blow. Single-B and CCC-rated exploration-and-production names in the HY universe do not.

Credit markets have not followed equities lower. This is structurally predictable, not mysterious. Equity re-prices in real time; credit re-prices on events — covenant breaches, revolver redeterminations, missed coupons. Those events have a lag built into the calendar.

Oil to credit: the transmission mechanism

A 20% decline in crude over 20 days does not appear immediately in a bond issuer's financial statements. It arrives in the next earnings quarter, in the next borrowing base review, in the next hedging programme roll. The mechanism is well understood; it simply does not move at the speed of a futures contract.

For exploration-and-production companies, the most immediate pressure point is the reserve-based lending facility. E&P revolvers are collateralised by reserves, and those reserves are valued on a price deck that tracks the forward strip. When Brent was at $110, lenders were already conservative — the strip implied mean reversion. Now that mean reversion has partially arrived. The question is how quickly lenders will push to redetermine borrowing base levels downward at the next formal review. The spring redetermination cycle in most RBL agreements closes in April and May. The next test is autumn 2026 — by which point the strip environment will have clarified considerably.

Hedging programmes are the second transmission channel. Companies that rolled or added hedges during the spike — locking in production revenues at $100 per barrel or above — face a different set of problems now that crude has retreated. Firms that hedged aggressively at the peak have downside protection but gave up the upside during the spike; firms that were slow to hedge are now fully exposed at the lower price. Neither position is uncomplicated, and the losses on hedge books will not be visible until quarterly filings.

The third mechanism is financial covenants. Leverage ratios based on trailing twelve-month EBITDA will not catch a revenue decline for at least two reporting periods. But the internal narrative around those ratios — the analysts' forward estimates, the lenders' qualitative assessments — is already shifting.

Watching, not alarmed — yet

None of this is a call for immediate concern. The HY default rate remains contained. At $75–85 per barrel, most well-capitalised E&P operators are still generating positive free cash flow, and the index-level spread environment at roughly 266 basis points does not signal acute distress. The universe of single-B and CCC-rated energy issuers is also meaningfully smaller than it was in 2015–16, when crude's collapse below $30 triggered a wave of restructurings that dominated the default rate for nearly two years.

But the pattern is worth naming plainly. Equity markets are already pricing E&P deterioration; credit has not followed. A 20% oil move in 20 days is the kind of shock that tends to be absorbed quietly at first and then re-priced in a cluster — when revolver redeterminations happen, when earnings disappoint, when a deal is pulled from a roadshow. Index-level HY returning +0.088% this week is not evidence the energy complex is untroubled. It is evidence the credit market is still running on yesterday's price deck.

The specific catalyst to watch is the autumn 2026 borrowing base redetermination cycle, which begins in earnest in September. By that point, E&P lenders will be working from six months of strip price data significantly below the Hormuz-crisis peak. If the oil strip has stabilised above $75, the review cycle will be painful but manageable for most borrowers. If crude continues to track the supply glut dynamics developing from the OPEC+ production restoration — the same structural force that is pulling down the geopolitical premium — the conversation about revolver availability changes character rather quickly.

HY beat IG this week. The qualifier remains: yet.
