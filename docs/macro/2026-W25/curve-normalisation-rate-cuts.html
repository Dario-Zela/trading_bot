The curve is positive again: what 79 basis points tells the Fed about rate-cut timing

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C., headquarters of the Federal Reserve — AgnosticPreachersKid / Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA 3.0
In one sentence
The 3M-10Y spread has turned positive at +79 bps, but the 30Y's refusal to rally despite a 20% oil drop points to a bear steepener driven by term premium, not growth.

The U.S. Treasury yield curve is no longer inverted. As of June 18, the spread between the 3-month bill and the 10-year note sat at +79.3 basis points — positive territory, and growing. From the 3-month at 3.658% to the 30-year at 4.901%, the curve is monotonically upward-sloping with no kink, no inversion, no ambiguity. After more than two years of inversion — the longest such episode in the modern record — the chapter is closed.

The question is what replaced it.

A positively-sloped curve can mean almost opposite things depending on how it got there. Markets interpreting this as an all-clear signal for risk assets are reading one version of the story. The bond market's own long end is quietly telling a different one.

Two Flavors of Steepening

Textbook yield curve analysis distinguishes between two mechanisms of steepening. In a bull steepener, the short end falls faster than the long end — typically because the Federal Reserve is cutting rates aggressively, markets are pricing in a recovery, and front-end yields collapse toward the new policy rate. Post-2009 and post-2020 looked like this: the Fed moved hundreds of basis points in months, the front end cratered, and the spread widened dramatically.

A bear steepener works differently. The long end rises — or refuses to fall — while the short end remains anchored. The spread widens, but not because investors are growing confident that easier policy is coming. It widens because the market is demanding more compensation to hold long-duration government debt, usually driven by fiscal anxiety, concerns about Treasury supply, or rising inflation expectations at the margin.

The distinction is not semantic. A bull steepener tells the Fed that markets believe policy is too tight and growth needs support. A bear steepener tells the Fed that the government's borrowing program is causing the bond market to reprice risk — and that rate cuts may not do much to fix it.

The Long End Is Not Cooperating

The evidence points toward the bear version. The simplest test: oil has fallen roughly 20% over the past 20 trading days as U.S.-Iran peace negotiations advanced and the Strait of Hormuz moved toward reopening. That is a supply-side disinflationary shock of the first order — the kind that, in a normal inflation-expectations-driven regime, pulls long Treasury yields down alongside it. Inflation fears recede; real yields adjust; the 30-year rallies.

The 30-year has not rallied. At 4.901%, it is essentially flat with where it stood when oil was trading above $100. If the long end were being priced purely by inflation expectations, it would have moved. It has not, which suggests something else is holding it up: term premium — the extra yield investors demand simply to lend to the U.S. government for three decades rather than rolling short-term bills.

That term premium reflects a very specific set of concerns: the projected trajectory of Treasury issuance, deficit expansion, and the question of who absorbs the supply at the margin. With headline deficits running at multi-year highs and Treasury issuance expected to remain elevated, the long end is being priced not for where inflation goes, but for how much new paper investors will be asked to absorb at what price.

One constituency that benefits from this configuration is the banking sector. XLF — the financial sector ETF — is up 1.81% over five days and 3.70% over twenty, reflecting straightforward arithmetic: a steeper curve expands net interest margins for institutions that borrow short and lend long. JPMorgan Chase updated its 2026 net interest income guidance to a record $104.5 billion on the same dynamic. Banks are not bidding the curve back down; they are beneficiaries of it staying wide.

What Warsh Should Read Into It

The Federal Reserve's June 17 meeting — the first under new Chair Kevin Warsh — held rates at 3.50%-3.75% but shifted the committee's posture markedly. Nine of nineteen policymakers now project at least one rate hike before year-end, a reversal from earlier expectations of multiple cuts. The 2-year yield advanced more than 16 basis points on June 18 alone, the largest Fed-day move since March 2008. The front end of the curve, in other words, is not falling — it is re-anchoring upward in response to a more hawkish committee.

This is crucial context for reading the +79 basis point spread. The steepening is not occurring because the front end is pricing in a dovish pivot. The 3-month bill at 3.658% tracks the existing policy rate; the front end is essentially inert. All the variation is in the long end — which, as argued above, is elevated for fiscal supply reasons, not because inflation expectations have re-accelerated.

That reading should matter for the FOMC. A bear steepener does not validate rate hikes designed to cool growth-driven inflation. It signals that the government's own borrowing is the price-setting force at the long end. Tightening further would not address term premium; it would compress growth without fixing the supply dynamics driving long yields. The curve's shape, in that light, is not a reason for Warsh to hike — but it is a reason to be cautious about reading the steepening as evidence of underlying vigor.

The next decisive data points arrive soon. The Federal Reserve's preferred inflation gauge — core PCE — is due before the next FOMC meeting; so is the Treasury's quarterly refunding announcement, which will give markets a clearer read on the supply side of the equation. If the long end finally responds to oil's deflationary signal and rallies, the bear steepener narrative weakens. If the 30-year holds above 4.80% through the summer regardless of incoming inflation prints, the term premium story will be difficult to dismiss.
