Two crises that didn't happen: why the commodity rout and the equity rally can both be right

NASA satellite view of the Strait of Hormuz and Musandam Peninsula, the critical chokepoint through which roughly 20% of global oil supply transits — closed since February, and now subject to a ceasefire agreement that is reversing the enti — MODIS Land Rapid Response Team, NASA GSFC / Public Domain
In one sentence
The oil crash and tech rally are the same trade — a geopolitical risk premium unwinding as the Strait of Hormuz reopens, with supply flooding a market that never lost its demand.

There is a version of this week's markets that looks like a contradiction. Crude oil has shed a fifth of its value in twenty days. Gold is down seven percent. Silver has lost thirteen. And yet the technology sector just posted its best five-day stretch in months, the yield curve is positively sloped, and high-yield credit — while not celebrating — has not blown out. Markets are supposed to reprice consistently. When commodity deflation this sharp arrives, equities are supposed to flinch. They are not flinching. The question the editorial board takes up this week is whether that makes sense, and if so, exactly where it stops making sense.

The short answer is that it does make sense — but it depends entirely on one thesis holding. Call it the supply-deflation thesis: the commodity selloff is not about demand collapsing; it is about supply surging after a geopolitical premium gets unwound. That thesis is internally coherent. It is also falsifiable in three specific places, and at least one of those places is already showing early stress.

In one sentence: The oil crash and tech rally are the same trade — a geopolitical risk premium unwinding and supply flooding back into a market that never lost its demand.

The Hinge Event

The immediate trigger for the oil collapse is not a demand model. It is a ceasefire. On June 17, President Trump and Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian signed a memorandum of understanding at the Palace of Versailles, alongside the G7 summit, formally ending hostilities that had, since late February, closed the Strait of Hormuz to normal commercial traffic. The following day, Pakistan — primary mediator of the talks — announced that the American blockade of Iranian ports would cease immediately and that Tehran had committed to reopening the strait promptly.

The Strait carries roughly a fifth of global oil supply under normal conditions. Its effective closure since the February 28 military escalation had produced an extraordinary supply shock — and an equally extraordinary geopolitical risk premium in oil prices. Brent averaged $107 per barrel in May, according to the EIA's June Short-Term Energy Outlook, even as the underlying demand picture deteriorated. The ceasefire is the market reversing that premium.


The Strait of Hormuz, photographed by NASA's Terra satellite. Its effective closure since February drove oil above $100; the ceasefire is unwinding that premium. Credit: MODIS Land Rapid Response Team, NASA GSFC

Layered on top of the geopolitical unwind is a structural supply problem that predates the Iran conflict. OPEC+ has implemented four consecutive monthly production increases since April, totaling more than 600,000 barrels per day in cumulative additions. The bloc simultaneously cut its 2026 demand growth forecast — twice — arriving at 970,000 barrels per day as of June 11, down sharply from earlier projections. Non-OPEC supply from the U.S. Permian Basin, Brazil, and Guyana has not slowed. The EIA now projects global oil demand to decrease by 1.1 million barrels per day on average in 2026. The geopolitical premium was the only thing keeping oil at elevated levels. Remove it, and the structural glut reasserts immediately.

The Thesis That Connects Oil to Technology

If the oil decline is a supply story, not a demand story, then falling oil prices carry a very different message for equities than they would in a demand-collapse scenario. In a demand collapse — the 2015-2016 pattern, or March 2020 — falling oil signals that the global economy is contracting, and equities should reprice lower across the board. In a supply-glut scenario, lower energy prices act as a tax cut for the rest of the economy. Input costs fall. Consumer real income rises. Inflationary pressure eases.


The Whiting Refinery, Indiana. North American refining capacity has not pulled back, adding to the global supply overhang now that the Hormuz blockade is lifting. Credit: Pi.1415926535 / CC BY-SA 4.0

The beneficiaries are exactly where the week's price action shows up. XLK — the technology sector ETF, weighted heavily toward semiconductors at 38.64% of the fund, with software adding another third — gained 4.49% over five days and 8.07% over twenty. Year-to-date, XLK is up more than 33%, with the one-year return now exceeding 61%.

The link between cheap energy and semiconductor outperformance is not incidental. The AI infrastructure buildout is energy-intensive in a way that earlier technology cycles were not. Data center power consumption is the primary operating cost constraint on hyperscaler capital expenditure plans. Lower electricity input costs — driven by lower natural gas prices, which correlate with oil — directly relieve that constraint. The capex cycle does not need rate cuts to continue. It needs power and compute. Both are getting cheaper.

Gold and silver are telling the same story from the other direction. Gold has shed 7.25% over twenty days; silver 13%. The precious metals bull case in 2025 and early 2026 rested on two pillars: geopolitical risk premia and inflation hedging. Both pillars are being kicked out simultaneously. The ceasefire removes the geopolitical bid. The oil-driven disinflation impulse reduces the inflation hedge rationale. And Goldman Sachs removed its remaining 2026 rate-cut call entirely in June, pushing the first expected cut to mid-2027 at the earliest — which raises the opportunity cost of holding zero-yield assets like gold. The selloff is not a crisis; it is a coherent repricing.

Three Places the Thesis Breaks

The supply-deflation thesis is coherent. It is not invulnerable. This editorial board identifies three specific fault lines.

First: energy high-yield credit. The ICE BofA US High Yield Index OAS stood at approximately 271 basis points as of June 12 — historically tight, consistent with broad credit health. But that aggregate masks the energy sub-sector. Levered exploration-and-production companies structured their debt at oil price assumptions set during the conflict-era elevated pricing. If Brent settles sustainably in the $65-70 range — which supply dynamics and the forward curve now suggest — portions of the high-yield energy complex face covenant stress. Credit markets lag. The aggregate spread looks fine until it doesn't. Watch for divergence between broad HY OAS and the energy sub-index over the next four to six weeks.

Second: the 30-year yield. The 3-month to 10-year spread stands at approximately +79 basis points — a positively sloped curve consistent with growth expectations. But the steepening is not coming from the short end falling (the Fed is on hold). It is coming from the long end rising. That is a bear steepener, not a bull steepener, and the 30-year has not rallied despite the disinflation impulse from oil. It remains near 4.90%. Elevated term premium on the long end reflects fiscal concerns that exist independently of energy prices: U.S. debt issuance, deficit trajectory, and the broader "higher-for-longer" structural argument. A bear steepener is compatible with "growth OK" in the near term, but if the 30-year breaks through 5.00%, the multiple compression effect on long-duration equity becomes difficult to ignore.

Third: healthcare as a policy variable. XLV's sharp reversal this week is a reminder that not every sector movement fits the macro frame. Healthcare has underperformed materially in 2026 — down roughly 7% year-to-date against a rising broad market — and the reversal appears driven by a discrete policy catalyst rather than a macro rotation. The Inflation Reduction Act's Medicare drug price negotiation provisions are moving from legislative text to actual price implementation in 2026, directly hitting pharmaceutical margins. Managed care names have faced a separate round of reimbursement uncertainty. These are not cyclical signals. They are regulatory events, and they are capable of interrupting even a coherent macro thesis at the sector level.

What the Curve is Actually Saying

The yield curve's +79 basis point spread is often summarized as "growth positive" and left there. That framing is too simple this week. A positively sloped curve in which the steepening is driven by the long end rising — not the short end falling — is consistent with at least two very different readings: genuine growth optimism that pushes term rates up, or fiscal anxiety that demands a premium for duration without actually implying that growth is strong.

The 30-year's refusal to rally on dis-inflationary news strongly suggests the second element is present. The ceasefire, the supply glut, the falling gold price — all of these are disinflation signals. In a world where the long end moved purely on inflation expectations, those signals would push the 30-year yield down. They are not. That means fiscal term premium is doing independent work at the long end of the curve, unrelated to the energy price move.

The practical implication: the supply-deflation thesis supports equities so long as the dis-inflationary impulse remains contained to the commodity complex and does not tip into goods-sector deflation that threatens corporate revenue. If goods prices begin falling outright — not just energy, but core commodities flowing through to manufactured goods — the earnings picture changes, and the tech multiple, already at a P/E above 33x on XLK, comes under pressure from the denominator side even as it benefits from the input-cost side.

What to Watch

The thesis lives or dies on one question in the near term: does Hormuz actually reopen, and on what timeline? Pakistan announced the American blockade would cease "immediately" on June 18. Political agreements and flowing barrels are different things. Tanker booking data, vessel tracking through the strait, and freight rate movements in the Persian Gulf over the next two to three weeks will confirm or complicate the thesis before any macro data point does.

Beyond that: watch the ICE BofA energy high-yield sub-index for the first sign of spread divergence. Watch the 30-year yield for a test of 5.00%. Watch for any follow-through on the healthcare policy catalyst — if drug pricing headlines reappear, the managed care selloff will deepen. And watch the dollar, which has been strengthening quietly — a continuing dollar rally would complicate the commodity unwind in emerging markets and create cross-asset drag the equity desk has not yet priced.

The supply-deflation trade is not wrong. But it is not self-executing. It requires the Strait to reopen, the credit market to stay calm, the long bond to stop rising, and a policy calendar to produce no more abrupt sector-level shocks. That is a lot of conditions to hold simultaneously.
