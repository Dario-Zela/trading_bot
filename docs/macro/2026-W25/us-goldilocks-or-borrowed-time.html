U.S. goldilocks, or borrowed time? The case for and against

The New York Stock Exchange on Broad Street, Manhattan — the U.S. equity market is signalling broad growth confidence, though the details are less uniform than the headline numbers suggest. — Ken Lund / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Steepening curve and tech-industrials breadth make the U.S. look near-ideal, but oil's 20% drop and healthcare's policy reversal are the fault lines Q3 data will stress-test.

The U.S. equity market has spent the past three weeks offering the kind of setup that makes it tempting to stop asking hard questions. The Treasury yield curve is positively sloped for the first time in years, the dollar is ascending, technology and industrial stocks are logging 20-day gains that put most developed markets to shame, and credit — for now — is not flinching. On the surface, the conditions look close to optimal: growth confidence is intact, financial conditions are normalising, and the sectors that typically lead a capex upcycle are doing exactly that.

The harder question is whether the signal is clean or borrowed.

The curve, the banks, and the growth bet

The three-month to ten-year spread hit +79.3 basis points this week — a level that would have seemed aspirational two years ago when the curve was still deeply inverted. The normalisation matters because it directly restores bank net interest margins: XLF has added 3.7% over the past 20 sessions, and while one month does not make a thesis, the correlation with curve steepening is tight and mechanical.

More telling is the breadth behind the equity rally. The technology sector (XLK) is up 8.1% over 20 days — a number large enough to move the index on its own — but industrials (XLI, +6.0%) are following, and that combination is significant. Technology gains that run ahead of the real economy are frequently mean-reversion setups. Technology gains accompanied by double-digit industrials earnings growth and a heavy pace of capex expansion — much of it driven by data-centre construction and AI infrastructure — look more like a genuine cycle. Goldman Sachs raised its full-year S&P 500 EPS estimate to $340, implying 24% annual growth, with industrials earning more than their usual share of the credit.

The high-yield market is, for the moment, holding. The ICE BofA HY option-adjusted spread sits around 278 basis points — low by historical standards and not showing the stress one would expect if risk appetite were genuinely deteriorating.

Where the fault lines run

Oil is the most conspicuous anomaly. Brent crude has fallen roughly 19% from its 2026 peak over the past month — its steepest monthly decline since the pandemic — driven primarily by optimism around U.S.-Iran ceasefire talks and the prospect of Strait of Hormuz shipping lanes reopening. The energy sector (XLE) has reflected that directly, losing 5.9% in five days.

The ambiguity is structural. A 20% decline in crude over a month has historically preceded one of two outcomes: a demand slowdown, or a supply-driven repricing that stresses energy credit before anyone notices the coupons. The current move appears largely supply-expectation-driven — UBS noted there is still "little evidence" of improved vessel traffic or cargo flows inside the Gulf. If the ceasefire frays, oil snaps back. If it holds and crude stays low, the pressure migrates to high-yield energy issuers whose debt was priced against oil assumptions considerably above current spot. Neither path is costless.

Healthcare is the quieter fault line. XLV has given back 3% in five days — a reversal too sharp to be sector rotation alone. The sector carries roughly 13% of S&P 500 index weight, so its direction matters at the index level. The policy backdrop explains much of the move: the Inflation Reduction Act's drug-pricing negotiation provisions are advancing, managed-care margins are under pressure, and any regulatory headline touching UnitedHealth, Eli Lilly, or a Medicare programme can reprice the entire ETF within a session. A sector that had been treated as a defensive backstop is now carrying policy risk that accumulates quietly until it doesn't.

The dollar is the third variable. DXY has gained 1.8% over 20 days and sits at 100.85 — a level that signals U.S. growth outperformance relative to the rest of the developed world, but that also compresses multinational earnings on translation. Apple generates roughly 60% of revenue overseas; Microsoft, around 50%. A sustained 10% DXY appreciation shaves an estimated two to four percentage points off S&P 500 EPS. At current valuations, that is not a number investors will absorb quietly.

Goldilocks or a borrowed quarter?

Both stories are simultaneously defensible, and the resolution depends on timing. The bull case is well-supported at the data level: the curve is sending the right signal, capex is flowing, credit is not alarmed. The bear case is a collection of lagging indicators — oil's decline will show up in energy-sector revenues in Q3 earnings; healthcare's policy exposure will crystallise around the next regulatory announcement; dollar strength will surface in megacap earnings translation when technology reports in July.

The most likely catalysts to resolve the ambiguity arrive in two tranches. The July earnings season — beginning mid-month with the major banks — will test whether the curve's NIM gift is showing up in actual results, and whether technology and industrial guidance is being revised upward or merely sustained. The second is geopolitical: any deterioration in Iran ceasefire talks that pulls crude back above $80 would simultaneously re-stress energy credit and revise the soft-landing narrative underwriting current valuations.

Until one of those data points arrives, the surface is calm. That is not a reason to panic; it is a reason to be precise about which parts of the setup are genuine inflections and which are held together by an assumptions chain that the next quarter will test.
