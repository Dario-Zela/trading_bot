RISK · PRECIOUS METALS · By S. Vance · 2026-06-21 · 3 min read

Gold and silver's diverging selloff signals more than an inflation unwind

Gold bullion bars: the metal's decline is the smaller story — silver's deeper slide carries the industrial warning — Pixabay / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
Gold's 7% drop is an inflation-hedge unwind; silver's 13% drop is that plus an industrial demand signal the equity market hasn't fully heard.

Gold and silver have both been falling — gold by roughly 7 percent over the past twenty trading days, silver by more than 13 — but treating them as a single story misses the more important signal. The two moves share a driver and diverge on a second, and it is the divergence that carries the warning.

The shared driver is the inflation unwind. Gold's role as a purchasing-power hedge attracted a substantial bid over the past two years. That bid is now reversing. Oil is down more than 20 percent over the same window, broad commodity disinflation is recalibrating the macro backdrop, and the May payrolls report — 172,000 jobs added against an 85,000 consensus — all but eliminated near-term rate-cut expectations and briefly placed a December hike in play. Gold produces no yield; when real rates stop falling, gold's opportunity cost rises. The metal fell $146 in a single session on June 5. That is the inflation story playing out as it should.

Silver felt the same repricing and then some. Its additional six-plus percentage points of underperformance relative to gold over twenty days — and its continued slide this week while gold has stabilised — reflect a second, separate dynamic: industrial demand, and the loss of it.

"Silver is the only major commodity that is simultaneously a monetary asset and a major industrial input — and right now, both sides of that equation are turning against it."

The canary with a different song

Roughly half of silver demand is industrial. The metal runs through photovoltaic cells, semiconductors, EV battery contacts, and high-frequency electronics. China is the dominant consumer across virtually all of these end-uses. Chinese manufacturing PMI sat at exactly 50.0 in May — the threshold between expansion and contraction — with export orders weakening and factory employment falling. Solar installations, which set a record 315 gigawatts in 2025, are expected to see their first annual decline in two decades. Chinese panel makers — Longi Green Energy, Jinko Solar, Shanghai Aiko — are actively moving toward copper-based cells specifically to reduce their silver exposure, with mass production of copper-contact modules already underway.

The gold-silver ratio, which contracted to around 50 in January when silver was hitting an all-time high near $121 per ounce, has since widened to approximately 63–65. Historically, a widening ratio tracks declining manufacturing activity with reasonable consistency: a ratio pushing above 80 has preceded two of the last three recessions and the sharp PMI contractions of 2015 and 2019. The current reading is more moderate, but the direction of travel since January is unambiguous.

Base metals — copper, aluminum, zinc, as captured by DBB — are down roughly 2 percent over twenty days. That directionally confirms the industrial demand narrative but understates it considerably. Silver at -13 percent is the more sensitive instrument. It carries sharper exposure to Chinese demand cycles than copper or aluminum, with less liquidity friction dampening the signal.

The risk flag is not the fall in gold. A correcting gold price after sustained central bank accumulation and geopolitical hedging is behaving predictably. The risk is that the widening gold-silver ratio is pointing at a deterioration in industrial demand that base metals are only partially capturing — and that equity markets, particularly in industrials and materials, have so far largely not repriced.

The next Chinese manufacturing PMI release, due in early July, is the data point to watch. If it prints below 50, the ratio's message will have been confirmed before most equity desks have moved.
