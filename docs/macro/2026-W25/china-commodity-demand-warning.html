The commodity complex is sending a China demand warning markets are slow to price

The Guixi Smelter in Jiangxi province — operated by Jiangxi Copper, it is the world's largest copper refining complex, processing over one million tonnes annually. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Oil −20%, silver −13%, base metals flat despite an equity rally: the 20-day commodity selloff is broader than OPEC+ can explain, and historically this kind of breadth has led China PMI by 4–8 weeks.

Oil down 20%, base metals flat-to-negative, silver down 13%, agriculture off nearly 5% — read together over the same 20-day window, the commodity complex is pricing a China demand slowdown that equity and credit markets have yet to acknowledge.

The movement in oil is easy enough to explain on its own terms. OPEC+ is fracturing in ways that would have been unthinkable two years ago. The UAE formally exited the cartel in May, announced plans to lift output toward 5 million barrels per day, and handed every remaining member a market-share calculation that puts the old quota discipline under terminal pressure. USO's 20-day decline of 20.38% has a credible supply-side alibi.

But oil is not the only commodity that's selling off. And when you look at what else is moving — and why — the supply story starts to feel insufficient.

When Breadth Is the Message

Silver is down 13.41% over the same 20-day window. Base metals, measured by DBB, have shed 1.98% even as equities have rallied. Agriculture (DBA) is off 4.79%. These are not correlated by a single supply event. They are correlated by a single buyer.

China accounts for roughly 16% of global oil demand, according to Worldometer consumption data — a share that has been plateauing as EV adoption displaces liquid fuels. But China's footprint in other commodity markets is, if anything, more decisive. It manufactures more than 80% of the world's solar panels, consuming silver paste at a rate that has more than doubled since 2021. Silver's share of total solar module production cost has climbed from roughly 3% in 2023 to an estimated 17-29% by 2025-26, according to the Silver Institute, making it a sensitive real-time indicator of Chinese panel output. China also manufactures approximately 60% of the world's electric vehicles, each of which carries silver in its electronics and copper in its motor and wiring.

Base metals tell a similar story. Copper demand in China softened sharply from Q3 2025 onward, driven partly by the prolonged contraction in property investment — S&P Global data pegs that decline at around 11.1% year-on-year even as industrial output nominally grew. The divergence between headline output figures and the underlying demand signals embedded in commodity prices is a standing tension in China's macro data. Markets that trade physical flows, rather than survey responses, tend to resolve it first.

Agricultural commodities are the outlier that completes the picture. Feed grain imports are a function of Chinese livestock and food processing demand; DBA's 4.79% decline is unlikely to be driven by supply shocks in Brazil or the US Plains alone at a time when those markets have been broadly stable. The more parsimonious explanation is softening import appetite.

The Supply-Side Alibi and Its Limits

The OPEC+ breakdown is real and consequential for oil prices. The UAE's exit removed the cartel's third-largest producer from any collective constraint. Iraq and Kazakhstan had been chronic overproducers even within the old framework. The group's May decision to add 188,000 barrels per day from voluntary adjustments came against a backdrop where global oil demand is now forecast to decline by 1.1 million barrels per day over the course of 2026, according to IEA projections. The combination of supply acceleration and demand deceleration has structural logic; the oil price decline is not purely speculative.

But the alibi runs out at the energy sector's border. OPEC+ decisions do not move silver. Cartel dynamics do not explain why base metal prices have failed to bounce despite an equity rally that, in prior risk-on episodes, would have dragged industrial metals higher alongside it. The correlation breakdown between equities and base metals is itself a signal: the equity rally is being driven by factors — AI capital expenditure, large-cap tech earnings, domestic services activity — that are decoupled from Chinese industrial throughput.

Oxford Economics flagged the structural divergence in its 2026 commodities outlook, noting that metals were expected to underperform while precious metals found support from other factors. What they did not fully model was the possibility that the weakness in industrial commodities and the weakness in silver would arrive simultaneously — which is what the 20-day price action shows.

What Credit and the Dollar Are Not Saying

The absence of a comparable signal in other asset classes deserves scrutiny rather than reassurance. The dollar has been strengthening — the DXY's quiet march higher over recent weeks reflects a growth-divergence trade, with US activity still outpacing Europe and, apparently, China. High-yield credit spreads have not blown out; energy names are carrying elevated embedded exposure, but the broader HY index has absorbed the commodity weakness without a dislocation.

Both observations are consistent with two interpretations. The first: commodity markets are wrong, and the softness in oil, silver, base metals, and agriculture reflects technical positioning, seasonal patterns, and the OPEC+ supply shock rather than a genuine Chinese demand signal. The second: commodity markets — which price physical flows directly — are simply faster than credit and FX, which price financial claims and policy expectations.

In prior cycles, the latter has often been true. The commodity complex has historically led Chinese PMI readings by four to eight weeks, with the direction of industrial metals and energy providing early visibility into what purchasing managers will report in the next monthly survey. The RatingDog China General Manufacturing PMI eased to 51.8 in May from a multi-year high of 52.2 in April. Still in expansion territory, but deceleration was already visible at the margin. New export orders dipped.

The question is whether the June data — due in early July — will confirm the commodity signal or refute it. A Caixin Manufacturing PMI reading that holds above 51.5 with stable new orders would complicate the demand-slowdown thesis. A reading that dips toward 50, or shows a sharp contraction in the input prices and new orders subcomponents, would validate what oil, silver, copper, and grain prices have been saying for three weeks.

Credit spreads and the dollar are not telling the commodity complex it's wrong. They are, more likely, simply behind.
