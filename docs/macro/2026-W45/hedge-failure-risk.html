The tail risk is a bond market that stops hedging equities

In one sentence
Tech's 17% slide failed to pull the 10-year below 4.9% while credit stayed calm; three levels would show whether the lagging credit market is about to follow.

A balanced portfolio rests on one assumption: when stocks fall, government bonds rise, and the second cushions the first. This week that assumption failed in plain view. Technology shares have lost 17.12% over 20 days, as measured by the XLK sector fund, and the 10-year Treasury yield is still 4.86%. It is not far from where it began. Credit markets, which usually flinch first when something is wrong, barely moved.

That combination is the risk. A drawdown of that size should have sent money into Treasuries and pulled yields down. It did not. If credit now begins to move, the delay between the equity rout and the credit reaction is the warning.

A hedge that did not hedge

The 10-year yield has not fallen below 4.9%, and the 30-year sits at 5.26%. In a classic risk-off episode, a tech sell-off of this scale would drag long yields down by tens of basis points as investors reached for duration. The bond market declined the role, as examined in why duration did not catch a bid. The curve is also steep, and the long end is doing the work, a pattern covered in the steep-curve piece.

The likeliest explanation is that bond buyers are pricing something other than growth fear. Term premium, supply and inflation risk can all push long yields up while equities fall, and when they do, stocks and bonds fall together. The tech and oil unwinds this week, which moved together without a bond rescue, fit that reading. The evidence for it is the outcome itself: a large equity drawdown with no meaningful yield relief.

There is a second tell in the metals. Gold rose 4.7% on the week and silver 5.1%. Demand for those assets suggests some investors are looking for protection elsewhere because Treasuries are not supplying it.

Credit is calm, for now

The reassuring piece of the picture is credit. High yield trailed investment grade by 0.51 points over five days, a gap measured by HYG against LQD. That is a mild quality tilt rather than stress, as the credit note argues. Companies that borrow at junk spreads are not being priced as if defaults are coming.

Calm credit is not the same as safe credit. Credit often lags equities, because spreads reprice as funding costs bite and as earnings forecasts follow the share-price signal. A stock-bond hedge that fails while credit stays quiet describes the early stage of a repricing, not its absence. The risk is that the sequence completes: equities first, rates second, credit last.

The equity side is concentrated. The 17% slide in technology is the story under the index, covered in the sector piece, and defensives have led the month, as the US regional note shows. A market rotating into defensive sectors rather than fleeing risk broadly is one that has not yet fully repriced.

Three trip-wires

The case for concern rests on a few measurable lines.

  • The 30-year above 5.3%. It closed the week at 5.26%, a few basis points away. A break would show that long-end selling is continuing even as equities weaken, which is the clearest sign the hedge is broken rather than merely late.
  • The HYG-LQD five-day gap widening past one point. At 0.51 points the gap is roughly half that. A move past one point would mean high yield is no longer lagging mildly but being sold, and the lag between equities and credit would have closed.
  • XLK extending below this week's lows. Further technology losses with no relief in yields would turn a rotation into a test of whether anything in a balanced portfolio offsets equity risk.

No single line is decisive. Two of the three would be difficult to dismiss as noise. All three together would describe a market in which stocks, long bonds and credit are all repricing against the same shock, with no diversification between them.

What would ease the concern

The benign reading is available. The dollar is range-bound over 20 days, per the FX note, and the energy slide is a reversal rather than a collapse. Emerging markets face a weaker oil price meeting a firmer dollar, which is a squeeze but not a systemic event. Communication services bounced 4.9%, as the sector report notes. On this view, the rates market is simply deciding that term premium matters more than equity weakness, and credit is right to stay calm.

The test is the 10-year. If a 17% tech drawdown cannot pull the yield below 4.9%, and a deeper one still cannot, balanced portfolios have lost their shock absorber. The next checks are the 30-year against 5.3%, the HYG-LQD gap against one point, and whether XLK holds this week's lows when markets reopen.
