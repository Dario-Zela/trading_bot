Technology's 17% monthly drawdown is the story under the index

In one sentence
Technology fell 17.1% in twenty days while health care, staples, real estate and financials rose, which points to rotation rather than a broad selloff.

The technology sector has had the kind of month that usually dominates a market narrative. The Technology Select Sector SPDR (XLK), the standard exchange-traded proxy for the sector, fell 8.23% over the last five trading days and 17.12% over twenty. Measured against the other sector funds, it is the weakest on both frames, with one exception: energy fell harder in the single week, a move covered separately in these pages.

The more useful fact sits elsewhere on the scoreboard. Over the same twenty days, health care, consumer staples, real estate and financials all show positive returns. That is not what an across-the-board selloff looks like. It looks like money changing seats.

What held up

The clearest evidence is at the top of the 20-day table. Health care (XLV) is up 8.19% and consumer staples (XLP) is up 7.97%. Those are the two sectors an investor reaches for when the aim is to own earnings that do not depend on the growth cycle or on the discount rate applied to distant cash flows. Real estate and financials are also positive over the window, though by smaller margins than the two leaders.

The cyclical middle of the market has not been spared, but its losses are an order of magnitude smaller than technology's. Industrials (XLI) slipped 2.83% on the week and 3.89% over twenty days. That is a soft patch, not a rout. If investors were pricing a recession, industrials would be falling alongside tech, and they are not.

The pattern fits the broader regional picture, in which defensives are leading the month as growth stocks give way, and the oil unwind that took energy down in the same week. The rotation also helps explain why the index itself looks less alarming than its largest sector. Technology carries a heavy weight in the major US benchmarks, so a 17% drawdown there is partly offset by gains in sectors that make up the rest of the index. The headline number understates how much is moving underneath it.

What stabilization would look like

A bottom in XLK would not announce itself with a single strong day. Communication services bounced 4.9% this week and is still down on the month, a reminder that a rebound inside a downtrend is not the same as a turn. Three signs would carry more weight.

First, a week in which technology's decline is smaller than the market's, rather than a sharp one-day recovery. Second, a narrowing of the gap between XLK and the defensive leaders on the 20-day table, driven by technology rising rather than health care and staples falling. Third, evidence that the bond market is again willing to offset equity risk. So far it has not: duration failed to catch a bid during the equity decline, and the curve, at 86 basis points steep, is being shaped mostly by the long end. A tech recovery that arrives without a working bond hedge would be a more fragile one.

Who benefits

In the near term, the beneficiaries are the funds and sectors already named: health care and staples above all, with real estate and financials sharing in the rotation. For investors running broad index exposure, the rotation has acted as a cushion. For concentrated growth portfolios, it has not.

The caution is that defensive leadership of this size tends to be crowded quickly. A sector that has gained roughly 8% in a month has less room to absorb a reversal in sentiment than one that has been ignored. If technology stabilizes, the same flows could run the other way, and the 20-day leaders would be the ones to give back ground first.

The next test is the coming week's sector tape. If XLK's five-day return turns flat or positive while health care and staples hold their gains, the rotation will look like a pause. If technology keeps falling while the defensives stall, the selloff will have moved from a sector story to an index story.
