Why duration did not catch a bid from the equity rout

In one sentence
Tech fell 8.2% in five days and oil funds fell 12.1%, yet the 10-year yield sits near 4.86%, leaving the usual bond hedge untested; a close under 4.75% by 13 November would repair it.

The usual arrangement in a portfolio is that stocks and Treasuries pull in opposite directions. When equities sell off, investors tend to buy government bonds, yields fall, and the bonds cushion the loss. This week that arrangement did not hold. The Technology Select Sector SPDR (XLK) lost 8.2% over five days, and the 10-year Treasury yield, which would normally slide in that setting, finished near 4.86%.

What the hedge is supposed to do

A bond hedge works through two channels. The first is flight to safety: money leaves risky assets and lands in Treasuries, pushing prices up and yields down. The second is growth: a sharp equity decline signals weaker activity and lower expected policy rates, which also lowers yields. A tech drawdown of this size would usually trigger both.

Neither appears in the price. A 10-year yield at 4.86% after an 8% fall in the market's largest sector means holders of long Treasuries got no offset. The stock and bond sides of a balanced portfolio fell in step, or at best the bonds sat still.

Why the bid may be missing

The cross-asset tape offers a few clues, though none is conclusive.

The first is oil. Energy funds fell 12.1% over the same five days. Falling crude is a disinflationary impulse, and in theory it should pull long-dated yields down. The long end has not priced it. One reading is that the move is too recent. Another is that investors are demanding extra compensation for holding duration, regardless of what inflation is doing. The curve, about 86 basis points steep, suggests the second reading deserves weight: the long end is carrying its own premium rather than following the front end.

The second is credit. High-yield bond ETF HYG slipped 0.26% while its investment-grade counterpart LQD gained 0.25%. That is a modest tilt toward quality, not a panic. Credit markets are behaving as if the equity move is a rotation inside a market, not a threat to the economy. If so, the flight-to-safety channel never switches on, and the hedge has nothing to respond to.

This would also explain why the stock-bond link looks broken without a crisis to blame. A selloff concentrated in one sector, alongside a commodity unwind, is not the kind of event that sends money into Treasuries.

A level to test it

The claim can be checked against a number. If the hedge is merely delayed, with oil's disinflation and the equity weakness feeding through, the 10-year should close below 4.75% by Friday's close on 13 November. A close at or above 4.86% would say the long end is ignoring both signals, and that the duration premium is the dominant force. Anything between the two levels is ambiguous and should not be read as repair.

A failure of that test would matter beyond one week. A portfolio built on the assumption that bonds rally when stocks fall would need to treat that assumption as conditional on the source of the shock. Until the 10-year gives a clearer answer, the ballast should be considered unproven.
