A week of two unwinds: tech and oil fall together, and the bond market does not rescue either

In one sentence
Technology fell 8.2% and oil funds 12.1% in five days, yet the 10-year still yields 4.86% and the dollar firmed, so this is repricing rather than a flight to safety.

Two of the market's biggest themes unwound in the same five days. Technology shares fell 8.2%. Oil funds fell 12.1%. The usual script for a sell-off like this has money fleeing into government bonds, pushing yields down and cushioning the damage. That did not happen. The 10-year Treasury still yields 4.86%, the dollar edged higher, and the bond market declined to act as the shock absorber. This was a repricing of crowded positions, not a retreat to safety, and the distinction matters for what comes next.

Two leaders, one week

The technology sector fund, XLK, is down 8.2% over five days and 17.1% over twenty. The second figure is the more telling one: the week's fall came on top of a month-long slide, not out of a clear sky. The energy sector fund, XLE, lost 12.0% in the same five days, and the United States Oil Fund, USO, lost 12.1%. The near match between the equity sector and the commodity it tracks says the energy move was driven by crude itself rather than by anything company-specific.

The sell-off was concentrated rather than general. Communication services rebounded 4.9% on the week, though it remains down on the month. Over twenty days, health care (XLV) is up 8.2% and consumer staples (XLP) up 8.0%. Capital did not leave equities wholesale. It left the two most extended trades and went toward businesses with steadier earnings. Our sector desk examines the technology side in Technology's 17% monthly drawdown and the energy side in Energy gives back 12% in a week.

Gold fits the haven story on its face: GLD rose 4.7% and SLV 5.1%. But precious metals have lately traded as much on real-rate and currency expectations as on fear, and with the rest of the cross-asset tape failing to confirm a flight to safety, gold alone cannot carry that interpretation.

The hedge that did not hedge

The cleanest test of a risk-off week is the Treasury market. In a genuine scramble for safety, the 10-year yield falls as buyers pile in. Here it sits at 4.86%, with the 30-year at 5.26% and the three-month bill at 4.00%. The gap between the three-month and 10-year is 86 basis points, and almost all of the curve's steepness comes from the long end.

That is the signature of a market demanding compensation for holding long-dated debt, the term premium, rather than one expecting rate cuts to rescue growth. A flight to safety would flatten that premium. Instead it is intact. The long end is doing the work, as our rates desk lays out in The curve is 86 basis points steep and in Why duration did not catch a bid.

The dollar points the same way. It rose modestly on the week, but the twenty-day picture is flat, which is not the sustained surge that accompanies panic. The dollar edges higher examines the range. Credit is similarly calm: high yield lags investment grade by about half a point, a mild quality tilt rather than a scramble for the exit (Credit calm, quality tilt).

Taken together, the signals describe an orderly rotation. Equities fell without a corresponding rally in bonds, the dollar firmed without surging, and credit spreads showed discomfort at the margin rather than distress. Stress that was systemic would have shown up in at least one of those places.

Why this is repricing, not panic

The interpretive claim here is specific, so the evidence for it should be too. Three observations support it.

First, the dispersion. If the week had been a broad de-risking, defensives would have fallen alongside everything else, or at least not gained. Health care and staples are up roughly 8% over twenty days, which means investors are choosing what to own, not merely selling.

Second, the rates market. Long yields held near multi-year highs while stocks fell. When bonds fail to rally into an equity decline, the likeliest explanation is that the same forces are pressing on both: a higher required return on long-dated cash flows. That hits technology, whose value rests on earnings far in the future, and it limits how far duration can rally even when fear rises.

Third, oil. Crude's fall is a separate story from technology's, with its own supply and demand drivers, and its effects are felt elsewhere. A weaker oil price against a firmer dollar is a squeeze on commodity exporters, a theme taken up in Emerging markets and commodity exporters. That the two unwinds coincided suggests crowded positioning in both, not a shared fundamental cause. The domestic picture, with defensives leading the month, is in US: defensives lead.

The cost of this reading is that it offers no easy floor. A panic ends when forced sellers run out. A repricing ends when the new price is accepted, and that depends on rates.

What would settle it

The thesis is that the next move turns on whether the 10-year can hold near 4.86% while equity leadership resets. If it does, the term premium keeps a lid on any relief rally in growth stocks, and rotation toward defensives has further to run. If yields drift higher, the pressure on long-duration equities returns, and the risk flagged in The tail risk is a bond market that stops hedging equities moves from scenario to condition.

The falsifier is equally concrete. If the 10-year falls below 4.6% while XLK recovers its week's loss of 8.2%, the better description of these five days is a positioning flush: leveraged holders forced out, prices overshooting, and normal relationships restored. In that case the bond market will have eventually done its job, only late.

Neither condition is met today. The 10-year is 26 basis points above the line that would signal a flush, and technology is still more than 17% below where it stood a month ago. Until one of those figures moves decisively, the evidence favours the less comfortable explanation: a market that is repricing the cost of long-dated money, with no safe harbour built into the price.
