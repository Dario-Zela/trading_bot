US: defensives lead the month as growth stocks give way

In one sentence
US sector leadership has shifted from technology (-17.12% in 20 days) to health care, staples and real estate while the curve stays steep, pointing to a sector story over recession.

US equities spent the past month trading one idea: get out of technology and into things that pay you to wait. Over the last 20 trading days the technology sector fund XLK has fallen 17.12%. Over the same stretch the health care fund XLV rose 8.19%, consumer staples XLP rose 7.97% and real estate XLRE rose 5.74%. That is a rotation, and the more useful question is what it says about the economy. The bond market offers a partial answer.

A rotation, not a retreat

The pattern is lopsided. The three sectors that gained are the ones investors usually reach for when they want steadier earnings and dividends, and they are not obvious beneficiaries of an economy in good health. Technology, the sector that carried the index for much of the cycle, absorbed most of the damage. Equity weakness that is concentrated in one sector looks different from equity weakness that is spread across all of them.

A broad recession scare would normally hit cyclicals as hard as growth names, and it would pull money into Treasuries. This move has been narrower. Money has left one corner of the market and gone into other corners of the same market. Investors have been repricing what they will pay for technology earnings. Most of the selling does not read as a verdict on the economy.

That is an interpretation drawn from the price pattern, and the data can still prove it wrong. But the evidence points one way: the funds that gained are listed equities, and the funds that fell are one sector. Neither looks like a flight from risk assets as a class.

What the bond market is saying

The rate backdrop supports that reading, though it does not settle it. The 10-year Treasury yield sits at 4.86%. The spread between the 10-year and the 3-month bill is +0.86 percentage points, a clearly positive, steep curve. A flat or inverted curve has historically been the market's way of signalling recession worries, and this curve is neither.

The steepness cuts two ways. It is not the shape of a market bracing for imminent contraction. But a 10-year yield near 4.86% is also a high discount rate for companies whose value rests on earnings far in the future, which is the usual argument for why long-duration growth stocks suffer when long yields stay high. That mechanism fits technology's slide better than a growth scare would. Our rates desk has looked at where the steepness is coming from in the long end of the curve and at why bonds did not rally as stocks fell. The absence of a Treasury bid is itself informative. In a classic risk-off episode, yields fall as equities do. Here they have not.

The sector picture also fits the wider week, in which technology and oil fell together without a bond-market cushion. The details of technology's drawdown are covered in our sectors coverage.

What would confirm or contradict the reading

The thesis is that this is a sector story, not a macro one. It is testable. The coming week's US data can be sorted by what each outcome would imply.

Inflation. The October consumer price report, expected on the usual mid-month schedule, is the main test of the rate leg. A hot core reading would keep long yields elevated and extend the pressure on long-duration growth stocks, which supports the rotation story. A soft one would loosen that pressure and could let technology stabilise.

Consumer spending. Retail sales are the cleanest check on whether the economy is weakening. If sales hold up, the case that this is not a broad recession signal gains weight. A sharp miss, especially outside discretionary categories, would suggest the defensive bid is about growth after all.

Labour market. The weekly jobless claims figures are the quickest read on labour conditions. A sustained climb would contradict the sector-only reading, because layoffs are what turn a valuation reset into an earnings problem.

Credit and breadth. Not a data release, but worth watching alongside them: whether the weakness spreads beyond technology, and whether high-yield spreads widen. So far credit has shown only a mild quality tilt.

The risk to the reading

The sharpest challenge to a benign interpretation is the one the bond market has so far declined to supply. If Treasuries stopped acting as a hedge against equity losses, a sector drawdown would be harder to contain, a risk examined in our risk coverage. For now, the evidence in hand is a steep curve, a high long yield and a rotation within equities. The week's data will show whether that combination holds or whether the economy has started to move the market.
