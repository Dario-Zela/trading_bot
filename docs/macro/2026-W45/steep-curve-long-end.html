The curve is 86 basis points steep, and the long end is doing the work

In one sentence
With the 3M at 4.00% and the 30Y at 5.26%, a +0.86-point 3M-10Y spread shows investors demanding term compensation even as equities fall.

The usual script for a bad week in equities has the bond market playing the safe harbour. Investors sell shares, buy Treasuries, and yields fall. This week the first half happened without the second. Stocks fell hard, and the Treasury curve ended the week steeper rather than flatter, with the longest maturities carrying most of the slope.

As of 6 November, the three-month bill yielded 4.00%, the 5-year note 4.65%, the 10-year 4.86% and the 30-year bond 5.26%. The 3M-10Y spread stood at +0.86 points. A curve that slopes upward by that much is, on its face, the shape of an economy expected to keep growing. Paired with a falling equity market, it is something else: a sign that investors are asking to be paid more for lending long.

What the shape says

The curve is not uniformly steep. Most of the climb comes beyond the five-year point. The 5Y-10Y gap is 21 basis points. From the 10-year to the 30-year it is 40 more, and the 5Y-30Y slope comes to 61. Between the three-month bill and the 5-year note the gap is 65 basis points, so the front half of the curve carries slightly more slope than the stretch from 5 years to 30 years, but it covers a much shorter span of maturities. In terms of steepness per year of maturity, the long end is working harder.

That matters for the diagnosis. A growth scare typically pulls yields down across the curve, and the front end most of all if investors expect rate cuts. Here the 3M bill still sits at 4.00%, and the 30-year is above 5.25%. If investors were mainly fearful of a slowdown, the long bond would be the asset they bought. It was not bought in size, a point developed in Why duration did not catch a bid from the equity rout.

Three explanations fit the numbers, and they are not mutually exclusive. The first is supply: more long-dated issuance than buyers want at current prices. The second is inflation risk, with investors unwilling to lock in a fixed return without a cushion. The third is term premium, the extra yield demanded simply for bearing the uncertainty of holding a long bond. The week's data cannot separate these. What it does show is that the market is treating the long end as a source of risk, not a refuge.

Other markets agree

The cross-asset evidence points the same way. Gold rose 4.7% on the week, even with nominal yields this high. Gold pays nothing, so a rally against a 5%-plus long bond suggests buyers are hedging something that a Treasury does not protect against, whether that is inflation, fiscal strain or a loss of confidence in the usual hedges. The dollar, by contrast, edged higher but stayed range-bound over twenty days, which argues against a disorderly flight from US assets.

Credit has been calm. High yield lagged investment grade by about half a point, which reads as a mild quality tilt and not a funding squeeze. The equity damage was concentrated: technology's 17% monthly drawdown and energy's 12% weekly give-back were the main drivers, and the two unwound at once, as described in A week of two unwinds. A sector rotation, rather than a broad credit event, is consistent with a bond market that is repricing term compensation without seeing systemic stress.

The level to watch

The marker for the coming week is 5.25% on the 30-year. The bond closed the week at 5.26%, a single basis point above it. A close back below that line would suggest the week's move was an overshoot. A sustained break higher would confirm that the long end is repricing on its own terms, and would put pressure on the 10-year, now at 4.86%, to follow.

The slope itself is the second gauge. If the 5Y-30Y spread widens beyond 61 basis points while equities are still soft, it points to long-end selling driving the curve. If the curve flattens because the 30-year rallies, the market is reverting to the older pattern of bonds cushioning stocks. A flattening driven by a rise in short rates would be a different signal again, and a less comfortable one for equities.

The larger stake is what the long bond does when shares fall. The risk, laid out in The tail risk is a bond market that stops hedging equities, is that portfolios built on the assumption that Treasuries offset equity losses find the offset missing at the moment they need it. This week supplied a first instalment of that test. The auctions and long-end trading in the days ahead will show whether it was a one-week anomaly or the start of a pattern.
