What a 5.5% long bond does to everything else

In one sentence
With the 30-year at 5.48%, real estate and utilities still rose this week; whether that is complacency or pricing in advance depends on whether they hold above 5.4%.

The long end of the Treasury market is paying about 5.5%, and the stocks that usually suffer most from that have gone up. The 30-year yield stands at 5.48%. Over the past five days the Real Estate sector (XLRE) rose 0.8% and Utilities (XLU) rose 2.1%. Both sectors are also higher over 20 days, by 5.2% and 4.3% respectively.

That is an awkward result for a standard piece of market arithmetic. Real estate and utilities are called bond proxies because investors hold them for steady income, and because their cash flows are long-dated and their balance sheets carry a lot of debt. When a risk-free 30-year bond yields 5.5%, a stock offering a smaller payout has to justify the gap with growth, safety or both. Rising long yields usually push these sectors down first.

Ignored, or already priced?

There are two readings of the tape, and the week's data cannot fully separate them.

The first is that the equity market is ignoring the long end. On this view, the bounce is a rotation into defensive sectors, with money moving out of areas that had been sold and into those that hold up in a shaky tape. The gains would then be driven by flows and positioning, not by any judgment about discount rates. A 20-day gain of 5.2% in real estate, a sector with heavy debt, is hard to explain with fundamentals alone over so short a window.

The second is that the move has already been priced. Long yields did not jump this week; they have been drifting toward these levels, and the 20-day returns suggest these sectors absorbed the adjustment earlier and have since recovered. If so, 5.48% is not a new shock to them. It is the established backdrop, and further damage would need yields to move higher still.

The evidence leans slightly toward the second reading. The 20-day gains are larger than the 5-day gains for both sectors, except that the utility move has been steadier. A one-week squeeze would show up mostly in the shorter window. But neither reading can be confirmed from a single week of returns, and a rally in rate-sensitive sectors alongside rising yields is more often a pause than a resolution.

The test

The level that matters is 5.4%. If the 30-year holds above it, the defensive sectors will have to keep rising against a higher hurdle rate, and that is the harder case. Real estate would be the more exposed of the two, because its valuations lean directly on the rate used to discount long-dated rents. Utilities have more room to pass costs through regulated rates, but they also borrow heavily and compete for the same income-seeking buyers.

The companion pieces in this edition show where the pressure is building. The steep curve puts the stress in the long end, not at the front. Precious metals sold off with real yields in the same period, which suggests the move is being driven by term premium and not by growth fears. And the duration drag in credit points the same way.

What to watch is simple. A weekly close for the 30-year above 5.4%, with XLRE and XLU giving back this week's gains, would say the equity market had only been late to notice. If the sectors hold while yields stay high, the market is making a different bet: that income stocks can carry a lower spread to Treasuries than they used to. That is a position that tends to hold until the bond market objects.
