A dollar bid that gold did not welcome

In one sentence
The dollar index rose 1.1% to 103.41 as long yields climbed, while gold fell 4.6%, silver 5.8% and base metals 2.1%, with yields the likely shared driver.

The dollar had a good week, and on its own that would hardly be news. The ICE dollar index, DXY, closed at 103.41, up 1.09% over five days and 1.54% over 20. Long-dated Treasury yields rose over the same stretch, and a stronger currency paired with higher yields is the oldest pattern in foreign exchange: capital is paid more to sit in dollars, so it moves there. What made the week unusual is what happened next door. Gold fell, silver fell harder, and base metals slid with them.

The familiar half of the story

The rates-differential account is the simple one, and the data fit it. The Treasury curve is steep, with a 103-basis-point gap between short and long maturities and a long end at 5.48%. When the long end reprices upward while other major bond markets do not keep pace, the dollar tends to gain. That is a statement about relative yield, and it requires no change in the growth outlook. Higher compensation for holding long-dated US paper is, on its face, reason enough to hold more dollars.

The 20-day figure matters as much as the weekly one. A 1.5% gain over a month for the dollar index is not a spike. It looks like a drift with a recent acceleration, which is how rates-driven dollar moves usually look.

The part that does not fit the usual script

Gold is not supposed to enjoy a rising dollar, but it is not supposed to collapse into one either, at least not when the dollar's rise is this modest. The SPDR Gold Shares ETF, GLD, closed at 355.45, down 4.6% on the week and 7.0% over 20 days. Silver fell 5.8%. DBB, the base-metals fund, lost 2.1%.

A dollar gain of about 1% does not mechanically explain a gold drop of nearly 5%. The conventional sensitivity between the two is looser than that, and the gap points to a second force. The likeliest candidate is the same one lifting the dollar: yields. Gold pays nothing, so when long-dated yields climb, the opportunity cost of holding it climbs too. Another piece in this edition, on hedges failing together, argues that precious metals sold off with real yields rather than with risk appetite, and the pattern here is consistent with that reading. On this view the dollar is less the cause of the commodity selloff than a fellow passenger, a second consequence of the repricing at the long end.

The distinction matters for what comes next. If the dollar were the driver, a pause in its advance would relieve metals. If real yields are the driver, the dollar can stall and metals can still fall. The snapshot cannot separate the two, and a single week is a thin sample. Treating the dollar as the common factor across the complex is a reasonable shorthand, so long as it is understood as a shared exposure and not a proven mechanism.

Base metals add a wrinkle. Copper-heavy baskets respond to growth expectations and to the currencies of their largest buyers and sellers as much as to rates. A 2.1% decline is smaller than the precious-metals losses, which fits the idea that the shock was concentrated in assets most sensitive to the discount rate, with industrial demand a secondary channel.

Where the cross-currents sit

The data snapshot for this week contains no pair-level currency moves, so no claim is made here about how any specific exchange rate behaved. What can be said is where to look. Currencies tied to commodity exports are exposed to two forces pulling in opposite directions: the broad dollar firmness, which weighs on nearly everything quoted against it, and the fall in metals prices, which weighs on the terms of trade of producing countries. When both point the same way, the currency story is simple. When a currency's domestic rate path pulls the other way, the result is a cross-current, and those are the pairs where the dollar's strength is least likely to be a clean read.

Emerging markets are the more acute version of that exposure. The edition's regional piece on emerging markets facing a stronger dollar and softer commodities covers the squeeze in more detail: dollar-denominated debt becomes costlier to service as the currency rises, and commodity exporters lose revenue as prices fall.

The backdrop for all of it is the long end. The steep curve and 5.48% long bond are the source of the week's rates impulse, and the hurdle-rate effect of a 5.5% long bond is the channel through which it reaches everything else. The broader US picture is equities higher, long yields higher and the dollar firm, which is an unusual combination to hold at once.

What to watch

The test is whether the dollar and long yields keep moving together. If long yields stabilise near 5.5% and gold keeps falling, the dollar-and-yield explanation is incomplete and something else, such as positioning, is doing the work. If yields ease and metals recover while the dollar holds its gains, the common factor was the discount rate all along. Either outcome will show up first in the next few weeks of Treasury auctions and in whether DXY can hold above the 103 area it has just reclaimed.
