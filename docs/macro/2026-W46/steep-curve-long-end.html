RATES · CURVE SHAPE · By G. Mehlman · 2026-11-15 · 4 min read

A 103bp curve with a 5.48% long end

In one sentence
The curve is steep from 4.12% at 3M to 5.48% at 30Y, and LQD's duration-led loss points to term premium, not policy expectations, as the driver.

The Treasury curve ended the week of 13 November with a shape that is easy to describe and harder to explain. Three-month bills yielded 4.12%. Five-year notes yielded 4.98%, ten-year notes 5.15% and the 30-year bond 5.48%. The distance from the front end to the long end is 136 basis points, and the gap between the three-month bill and the 10-year note is 103.

The useful question is not whether the curve is steep but why. A curve can slope upward because investors expect the Federal Reserve to raise rates, or because they want more compensation for holding long-dated debt whatever the Fed does. The two stories call for different responses, and this week's data lean toward the second.

Where the slope sits

Most of the climb comes early. Between the 3M bill and the 5Y note, yields rise 86bp, which is about 83% of the 103bp from 3M to 10Y. The next step, from 5Y to 10Y, adds only 17bp. From 10Y to 30Y the curve gains another 33bp. The 5Y-to-30Y span is roughly 50bp.

The curve is therefore steep at the front, flatter through the middle, and then steeper again at the long end. That is a different picture from a steady, even incline.

A policy-expectations story would show up most strongly in the first five years, because that is the horizon over which the market prices the path of the policy rate. An 86bp rise by the 5Y point does reflect some of that. A bill yielding 4.12% sits well below the five-year yield, so the market is pricing a policy rate that ends up higher than the one a bill implies today, or at minimum one that does not fall.

The 17bp from 5Y to 10Y is small. If the market were expecting a prolonged tightening cycle, the belly would usually keep rising. Instead the increments narrow in the middle, then widen again past ten years. The 30Y yield at 5.48% is the point where policy-rate forecasts matter least and compensation for risk matters most.

What credit is saying

Investment-grade bonds offer the cleaner test. The iShares iBoxx Investment Grade Corporate Bond ETF (LQD) lost 1.65% over five days. A loss of that size is what a duration-led selloff looks like: LQD holds long-maturity, high-quality paper, and its price tracks Treasury yields closely. If the market were worrying about corporate defaults, the pain would be concentrated in lower-rated bonds. As the high-yield-versus-IG comparison shows, high yield outperformed IG over the period by 1.3 points, which is the reverse of a credit scare.

That pattern points to term premium. Investors are demanding more to hold interest-rate risk over long horizons, and the price action lands on the assets with the most duration. Credit spreads, which would widen in a growth scare, are not the source of stress. The editorial on duration versus credit makes the same case from the market-wide view.

Term premium cannot be observed directly. Estimates from the Fed's models are revised often, and a rise in long yields can mix expectations and premium in proportions that only later data reveal. The reading here is an inference from the curve's shape and from what sold off, not a measurement.

What would change the view

The term-premium reading rests on the long end carrying the load while credit stays calm. Several developments would weaken it.

  • A 10Y close back below 5.0%. That would take the 10Y within a few basis points of the 5Y's current level and suggest the selloff at longer maturities has been reversed. A move of that size would put the curve closer to a policy-driven shape.
  • The 5Y-to-10Y gap widening well beyond 17bp. Real steepening in the belly would indicate the market is repricing the policy path, not only demanding extra compensation at the long end.
  • Credit spreads widening alongside yields. If high yield stops outperforming IG, the story shifts from duration to default risk.
  • The 30Y moving materially through 5.5%. A decisive break would raise the hurdle rate for other assets, a theme taken up in what a 5.5% long bond does to everything else.

The consequences already reach beyond bonds. Precious metals sold off with real yields, not with risk, and equities rose while long yields climbed in the US.

The next catalysts are the coming Treasury auctions of longer-dated debt and the next round of inflation data. Weak demand at a 10Y or 30Y auction, with a yield pushing past recent highs, would strengthen the term-premium case. A firm auction with the 10Y yield slipping under 5.0% would point the other way.
