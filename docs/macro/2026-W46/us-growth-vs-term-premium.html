US: equities up, long yields higher, dollar firm

In one sentence
The US tape combined a broad equity bounce with a steeper curve and a stronger dollar, a mix that can read as growth or as term premium, but probably not both.

The US market this week did two things that do not usually go together. Equities bounced across the board, with every sector finishing higher. Meanwhile the bond market kept getting more expensive to borrow in: the 10-year Treasury yield sits at 5.15%, and the dollar index gained 1.1% to 103.41. A rally in shares and a rise in the price of long-term money can both be true for a week. Over a longer stretch, one of them usually has to give way.

A bounce with a cyclical tilt

The breadth was the first thing to stand out. No sector fell. Industrials, tracked by the XLI fund, rose 3.76% over five days, the second-best result among the sectors. Technology led the bounce, up 3.4% on the week, but it remains down 7.2% on the month, so much of its gain is repair rather than new ground. Energy equities also led even as crude oil fell, a divergence covered in Energy leads the bounce while crude falls.

Industrials sitting near the top of the table fits a straightforward story: investors are paying for cyclical growth. Factories, freight and capital spending are the businesses that benefit when the economy runs hot, and a market that expects that will bid them up first.

Financials point the same way. The XLF fund gained 2.66% over five days and 5.69% over 20. Banks tend to earn more when long-term rates sit well above short-term ones, because they borrow at the short end and lend at the long end. The 3M-10Y spread is +1.03 percentage points, a wide enough gap to support that reading.

What the bond market is saying

The same curve that flatters bank earnings is also a statement about risk. The 10-year at 5.15% and a long end near 5.48% mean investors are demanding a lot to hold government debt for decades. The desk's coverage of the long end, in A 103bp curve with a 5.48% long end, and of what that does elsewhere, in What a 5.5% long bond does to everything else, sets out the arithmetic. A higher risk-free yield raises the hurdle rate every other asset must clear.

There are two ways to explain a steep curve. In the first, the economy is strong, the central bank can keep short rates where they are, and long yields rise because growth and inflation are expected to stay firm. In the second, long yields rise because investors want more compensation for the uncertainty of holding duration, a term premium, regardless of how strong the economy is.

The first reading is friendly to industrials and banks. The second is not friendly to anything with long-dated cash flows, which is part of why technology remains well below where it began the month. Credit offers a clue. High yield outperformed investment grade by 1.3 points, which suggests the pain is concentrated in rate-sensitive, long-duration holdings. That is the argument of High yield outperformed IG by 1.3 points and the editorial line in The stress is in duration, not in credit.

The dollar as a tiebreaker

A 1.1% weekly gain in the dollar index is large for a currency gauge. It is consistent with either story: capital flowing to a fast-growing US economy, or capital demanding higher US yields. The reaction elsewhere is more informative. Gold, which does not pay interest and is sensitive to real yields, did not welcome the move, a pattern traced in A dollar bid that gold did not welcome and in Precious metals sold off with real yields, not with risk. Emerging markets, which borrow in dollars and sell commodities, face a harder combination, as Emerging markets face a stronger dollar and softer commodities describes.

The dollar's strength also fits the term-premium reading better than a clean growth story would. In a pure growth boom, commodity prices would more plausibly be firm, not soft.

What would settle it

The equity tape has so far absorbed the rate move without complaint, which is itself a form of evidence: buyers are treating higher yields as a sign of strength. That tolerance has limits, and the 5.15% level on the 10-year is where it will be tested. If the next round of growth and inflation data is strong, the cyclical reading holds, and the leadership of industrials and financials can persist. If yields keep rising on a soft or mixed data print, the explanation shifts toward term premium, and the sector that has most to lose is the one that just rebounded hardest. The next data releases and the next long-bond auctions will show which of the two is doing the work.
