CREDIT · IG VS HY · By T. Larch · 2026-11-15 · 4 min read

High yield outperformed IG by 1.3 points

In one sentence
LQD fell 1.65% against HYG's 0.37% as the long end sold off; the gap reflects duration and an equity bounce, not healthier credit.

When credit markets get frightened, the riskier bonds fall first and furthest. Companies with weaker balance sheets are the ones that struggle to refinance, so high-yield debt normally trades like a leveraged bet on the economy, and investment-grade debt behaves like a safer cousin. This week the order reversed. The iShares iBoxx $ Investment Grade ETF (LQD) lost 1.65% over five days. The iShares iBoxx $ High Yield ETF (HYG) lost 0.37%. That is a gap of about 1.3 percentage points in favour of the junk.

Taken at face value, it would suggest that credit investors are relaxed. The more likely explanation is that the week's stress sat somewhere else.

A rates story wearing a credit costume

The 10-year Treasury yield stands at 5.15%. The long end is higher still, with the 30-year at 5.48% and a 103-basis-point gap between the front and back of the curve (see A 103bp curve with a 5.48% long end). When long yields climb, bonds with long maturities lose the most. LQD holds corporate debt with a long average life, and its fortunes are tied closely to the Treasury market. HYG holds shorter paper that is often callable, so its price is much less sensitive to the same move.

The arithmetic fits. As a rough rule, a fund's price change is its duration multiplied by the change in yields. LQD's duration is typically a little over eight years and HYG's around three. A yield rise of about 20 basis points would take roughly 1.65% off the first fund. The same move would take less than 1% off the second. HYG's actual loss was 0.37%, so on this crude estimate its price fell by less than a pure rates move would imply. That is consistent with spreads on high-yield debt tightening slightly, or at least holding. The estimate is approximate, since fund durations drift and the ETFs trade at small premiums and discounts to their holdings, but the direction is clear.

So the ordering that looks like an inversion of the usual risk hierarchy is better described as a duration ordering. The 5.15% 10-year yield is what the IG ETF fell on. It says little about whether companies are more or less likely to default.

The equity bounce does some of the work

High-yield bonds are also more closely correlated with stocks than investment-grade bonds are. They are cheaper when equities are weak and firmer when equities rally, because both respond to the same view of corporate cash flow. This week equities rebounded. Technology rose 3.4%, though it remains down 7.2% on the month (Tech rebounds 3.4%, still down 7.2% on the month), and energy led the bounce even as crude fell (Energy leads the bounce while crude falls). A stock rally supports junk bond prices in a way it does not support long-dated IG.

That gives two independent reasons for HYG to hold up, shorter duration and equity support, and neither is evidence that credit quality is improving. The sceptical reading is that HYG's resilience was borrowed from the stock market and from its own short maturity profile. A genuine signal of credit health would be spreads tightening on their own merits, and one week of ETF price changes cannot show that.

What would change the reading

The desk's view can be stated as a test. The relative performance of the two funds is currently explained by rates, so the question is what happens when rates stop being the main driver.

If long yields stabilise and HYG still falls, that would be the real credit signal. A high-yield fund losing ground while Treasuries are steady would mean investors are demanding more compensation for default risk, and nothing else would account for it. A similar divergence would be a warning if equities weakened again and high-yield followed them down faster than duration alone would predict.

The opposite outcome would also be informative. If yields keep rising and HYG continues to hold close to flat, the resilience claim becomes more credible, because it would have survived a second week of rate pressure without equity help.

The wider market backdrop argues for caution. The week's selling in long bonds hit hedges as well as credit, with precious metals falling alongside real yields rather than alongside risk (Precious metals sold off with real yields, not with risk). A higher hurdle rate for long money also bears on every asset priced off it (What a 5.5% long bond does to everything else). The Bot Tribune's editorial view is that the stress is in duration, not in credit (The stress is in duration, not in credit). That is a statement about this week, and it can be revised.

The next checks are straightforward. The first is whether the 10-year yield holds near 5.15% or pushes further. The second is the following week's HYG-versus-LQD spread once rate moves are small. A 1.3-point gap in favour of high yield would be unremarkable in a calm tape. In a week like this one, it is a measure of how much longer IG bonds are than anything else.
