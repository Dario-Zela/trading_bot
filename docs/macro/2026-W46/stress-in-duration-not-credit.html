EDITORIAL · WEEKLY THESIS · By The Editor · 2026-11-15 · 4 min read

The stress is in duration, not in credit

In one sentence
With the 30-year near 5.48%, the week's damage landed on long-duration IG bonds and bullion rather than junk credit, pointing to term premium and real yields, not default fear.

The week looked like a relief rally. Every one of the eleven S&P 500 sectors finished the five days higher, the sort of breadth that normally gets filed under risk appetite. The rest of the tape disagrees. Investment-grade bonds fell harder than junk bonds, gold and silver were sold into a rising dollar, and the long end of the Treasury curve ended the week at yields not seen in a generation of ordinary cycles. The market did not spend the week fearing default. It spent the week arguing about the price of time.

A rally with the wrong fingerprints

The cleanest way to see it is to compare two credit funds. LQD, the investment-grade corporate ETF, fell 1.65% over five days. HYG, its high-yield counterpart, fell 0.38%. That is a 1.27-point gap in favour of the riskier paper, which is the opposite of what a credit scare produces. In a genuine flight from default risk, the weakest borrowers sell off first and hardest. Here the borrowers with the best balance sheets lost more.

The explanation is arithmetic, not sentiment. Investment-grade indices carry long duration: they are packed with bonds maturing in ten, twenty and thirty years, and their prices swing with the Treasury yields beneath them. High-yield indices are shorter, and their spreads give holders a cushion when the base rate moves. When the long end cheapens, duration is what hurts. Credit quality barely enters into it.

The long end is the story

As of 13 November the 10-year Treasury yielded 5.15% and the 30-year 5.48%. The spread between the 3-month bill and the 10-year stood at +103 basis points. A curve that steep, with the front end anchored by policy and the back end drifting up, is a curve in which investors are demanding more compensation for lending long. That compensation, the term premium, is the variable doing the work. It rises when supply is heavy, when inflation uncertainty is high, or when the buyers who once absorbed duration without asking for much become less willing.

The equity market has so far treated this as background noise. That is understandable over five days and risky over fifty. A 5.5% risk-free rate at thirty years raises the hurdle every other asset has to clear, from corporate capital spending to the multiple paid for distant earnings.

Why gold did not help

The second odd fingerprint is bullion. The dollar index rose 1.09% on the week to 103.41. GLD fell 4.6% and SLV fell 5.8%. A stronger dollar alone can explain some of that, since metals are priced in dollars. But the usual reading of a gold selloff, that risk appetite is returning and the hedge is no longer needed, sits badly with credit and long bonds selling off at the same time. A risk-on week does not normally cheapen Treasuries and bullion together.

The more coherent reading is real yields. Gold pays nothing, so when the return on inflation-protected duration rises, the opportunity cost of holding it rises too. Precious metals sold off with rates, not with risk. The corollary is uncomfortable: the two assets investors reach for when equities wobble, long Treasuries and gold, fell in the same week for the same reason. A portfolio built on the assumption that they offset equities and each other was exposed on both flanks.

How much weight the equity bounce can bear

Breadth across eleven sectors is real, but it comes after weakness. Technology, the sector most sensitive to discount rates, is still down 7.2% over 20 days despite its bounce this week. A rebound that leaves the most duration-sensitive part of the market well short of where it stood a month ago is better described as repair than as a new trend. If the long end keeps cheapening, the repair is the first thing to be undone, because the same arithmetic that hit LQD applies to equities valued on cash flows a decade out.

Nor does the energy leadership offer much comfort. Leadership from a sector rising while crude falls suggests rotation and positioning more than a fresh growth impulse.

What would prove this wrong

The thesis is specific enough to fail. If the 30-year yield falls meaningfully in the coming sessions while the dollar softens, the week's moves would look like positioning noise: a crowded long-duration trade unwinding, then reversing. Gold recovering alongside a weaker dollar and lower long yields would confirm that. A further push in the 30-year yield above 5.5%, with LQD underperforming HYG again and bullion still unable to bounce, would say the market is still charging for duration.

The next tests are the inflation prints and the Treasury's long-bond auctions, where demand for duration is measured directly. The tape this week said the stress is in the price of time. The auctions will say whether buyers agree.
