RISK · SAFE HAVENS · By S. Vance · 2026-11-15 · 4 min read

Precious metals sold off with real yields, not with risk

In one sentence
Gold fell 4.6% and silver 5.8% in a week when equities rose, and long bonds also lost money, which leaves portfolios with fewer working hedges.

A week in which stocks rose should have been a quiet one for the hedges. It was not. Long investment-grade bonds lost money, and so did gold and silver. The assets that usually cushion a portfolio when equities fall were all falling at the same time as the equities were rising.

The numbers are plain. The gold fund GLD fell 4.58% over five days, and the silver fund SLV fell 5.85%. LQD, the long-dated investment-grade bond ETF, lost 1.65%. The 30-year Treasury yield ended the week at 5.48%, and the dollar index (DXY) rose 1.09%. Tech rebounded 3.4%, though it is still down 7.2% on the month.

Two hedges, one driver

Gold pays no income, so what it costs to hold depends on what a safe bond pays. When long yields climb and the dollar firms, the case for holding bullion weakens on both counts. Bullion priced in a stronger dollar gets more expensive for foreign buyers. A 5.48% long bond is a real competitor for the same defensive money.

The pattern fits that story better than it fits a story about risk appetite. If gold were simply a fear trade, it would have fallen alongside the safe-haven bid. Instead equities rose, the long end sold off, the dollar rose, and bullion fell. This piece has no data on inflation-linked yields, so the link to real yields is an inference from the nominal moves and not a measured one. The direction of the evidence is consistent, though: the metals moved with the rates market and the dollar, not with the stock market.

Silver shows the same thing, only louder. SLV is down 14.33% over 20 days, against 6.95% for gold over the same stretch. Silver has an industrial side that tends to suffer when the dollar is firm and commodities are soft, and it is the more volatile of the two. That makes it the sharper reading of the pressure on metals, not a separate signal.

What the hedge book assumes

The usual defensive allocation leans on two ideas. Long bonds rally when growth scares markets, and gold holds up when confidence in other assets slips. Each has a bad regime in which it fails. The first fails when the sell-off is about the cost of long-term money, not about growth. The second fails when that cost is rising and the dollar is strong.

This week showed both bad regimes at once. Credit tells a similar story: high yield beat investment grade by 1.3 points, a gap that points to interest-rate sensitivity and not to credit fear. The stress is in duration, not in default risk.

While equities were rising, this cost little. The exposure only matters if stocks turn. A portfolio built with bonds and bullion as its shock absorbers would then find the first one already impaired and the second one trading on the same inputs. Equities have bounced, but tech is still well below where it started the month, so the cushion is thinner than the equity tape suggests.

The trigger

The specific risk is a further rise in the 30-year yield through 5.5% with the dollar still firm. The yield is at 5.48%, so the level is within a rounding error of being crossed. A move above it, alongside a rising dollar, would extend the conditions that hurt both hedges this week. Equities would then be absorbing a higher hurdle rate for long-dated cash flows, and the assets meant to offset that would be moving the wrong way.

The argument has limits. One week does not establish a regime, and a pullback in yields or the dollar would take pressure off both bonds and metals at once, which is the same correlation working in the other direction. Gold is also down 6.95% over 20 days, so some of the repricing is already done. A softer dollar would be the first sign that the pressure is easing.

The things to watch are the 30-year against 5.5%, the dollar index, and whether bullion keeps trading with yields. If the 30-year holds below 5.5% and the dollar eases, the hedge book recovers its footing. If both break the other way, gold and long bonds will be falling together at a moment when equities are the only thing still holding up.
